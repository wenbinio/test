# Health Data Sharing in Singapore: Legislative and Normative Instruments — A Verified Roundup

**State of the law researched as at 24 August 2026; adversarial re-verification against primary sources completed 29 August 2026.**

This roundup catalogues every legislative instrument and the principal normative instruments (codes of practice, advisory guidelines, ethics guidelines, policy agreements and government frameworks) that govern the sharing of health data — clinical, administrative and genomic — in Singapore. Each entry gives the instrument's citation, type and status, its pinpoint provisions relevant to health data sharing, and an executive summary.

**Method.** Six parallel research agents compiled this roundup from primary sources (Singapore Statutes Online consolidations and gazetted texts, MOH, PDPC, CSA, BAC, SMC, trustplatform.sg, npm.sg and entrust.gov.sg), each flagging anything it could not confirm. A second wave of ten adversarial verification agents then attempted to refute every material proposition against the primary sources — re-fetching statute text, matching official PDFs byte-for-byte, and checking paragraph numbers against page images where PDF text layers drop them. Every confirmed refutation and precision correction has been applied; the Annex records what the adversarial pass caught and the few propositions that remain **[UNVERIFIED]**.

---

## Headline findings

1. **"Entrust" is real — and it is two things.** **TRUST** (Trusted Research and Real world-data Utilisation and Sharing Tech, trustplatform.sg) is Singapore's national trusted research environment for health and health-related data; its **Data Access Committee** is the body that governs research access to national health and genomic data. **enTRUST** ("enabling Trusted Research and real-world data Utilisation and Sharing Tech", entrust.gov.sg) is GovTech's cross-sector generalisation of the TRUST infrastructure and now serves as TRUST's members' portal. Neither has an enabling statute; both are contractual/administrative regimes. See Part III.
2. **The new anchor statute is the Health Information Act 2026 (No. 1 of 2026)** — passed 12 January 2026, assented 3 February 2026, gazetted 12 February 2026 — and it is **wholly uncommenced** as at 24 August 2026, with **no subsidiary legislation** yet made. MOH's stated intention is effect from early 2027, with batched compliance deadlines running from September 2027 to March 2030 (administrative, not statutory).
3. **Singapore has no special statutory category for health data.** The PDPA treats health data as ordinary personal data; heightened treatment arrives through sectoral statutes (HIA, HBRA, NRDA, IDA, MLSA), through PDPC guidance treating health information as a sensitive "target attribute", and through the deemed-significant-harm classes in the data breach notification regime.
4. **The architecture is shifting from consent to statutory authorisation.** HIA ss 11 and 50, IDA s 10(4) and MLSA s 29(3) each displace consent (and in several cases contractual and professional confidentiality) with purpose-limited statutory gateways; the HIA then expressly neutralises PDPA consent as an alternative gateway in seven provisions (HIA ss 16(2), 30(6), 38(7), 39(3), 40(7), 43(6), 57(2)).
5. **Re-identification is being criminalised layer by layer**: IDA s 59A(7)(b) (national public health research), PDPA s 48F, PSGA s 8, and (once commenced) HIA ss 41(2)(c) and 42(2).
6. **Research access to identifiable data runs through four different gates depending on the source**: the PDPA Second Schedule research exceptions (private sector), the HBRA Fifth Schedule IRB waiver (human biomedical research), NRDA s 12 (disease registries, with a statutory anonymisation-first test), and the TRUST/PRECISE data access committees (national platforms, contractual).

# Part I — Primary legislation (and subsidiary legislation under it)

## 1. Health Information Act 2026

**Citation:** Health Information Act 2026 (No. 1 of 2026). Bill No. 20/2025 (first reading 5 November 2025); passed by Parliament 12 January 2026; assented 3 February 2026; gazetted 12 February 2026 (Acts Supplement No. 2 of 2026, dated 13 February 2026).
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; Ministry of Health.
**Status:** **Uncommenced as at 24 August 2026** (SSO status line: "Uncommenced"). Section 1 provides the Act "comes into operation on a date that the Minister appoints by notification in the Gazette"; no commencement notification and **no subsidiary legislation** had been gazetted as at 24 August 2026 (confirmed by enumeration of the 2024–2026 Subsidiary Legislation Supplements). MOH intends effect from early 2027.
**Structure:** 9 Parts, 114 sections, 4 Schedules.

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **s 3(6)–(7)** — defines "individually-identifiable health information" (linkage test) and "anonymised health information" (health information that is not individually-identifiable).
- **s 4** — purposes: NEHR-based collection/storage/disclosure; a framework for sharing individually-identifiable information for healthcare, community health services and health programmes; confidentiality, integrity and availability of health information.
- **s 6** — defines **"excluded purpose"**: employment/appointment decisions, engagement of contractors, platform-work agreements, **insurance underwriting, renewal and claims**, and prescribed purposes — access for these is barred.
- **s 7** — defines the national electronic records system (NEHR); **s 8(1)** — the Minister may by Gazette order designate a **System Operator** (in practice Synapxe; no designation order yet gazetted).
- **s 11(1)** — **consent override**: "Despite any other written law or rule of law, or any obligation under contract," individual consent is **not required** for contribution to, or authorised access/collection/disclosure/use of, NEHR information under Part 2. **s 11(2)** — consent is equally not a basis for access.
- **s 12(1)** — **mandatory contribution**: specified contributors (HCSA licensees, retail pharmacy licensees, and persons/agencies listed in the First Schedule) must contribute the health information types listed in the First Schedule (visit events, medications, laboratory results, discharge summaries, vaccinations, etc.).
- **s 16(1)–(2)** — gateway rule: access/collection/disclosure of accessible health information only in accordance with Part 2 or under another written law; **PDPA Part 4 consent does not count** as such other written law.
- **s 17** — patients (and authorised representatives) may access their own record.
- **s 19(1)–(4)** — specified users may access **only** for provision of a healthcare service to the patient (never for an excluded purpose) or for a "specified examination" under a written law listed in the Third Schedule.
- **s 21** — no onward disclosure by users except internally, on a need-to-know, minimum-necessary basis.
- **ss 24–27** — **derived information** regime: "type 1" (individually-identifiable) approvable by the Minister for purposes **relating to or promoting public health** (s 25(2)); "type 2" (aggregated/anonymised) approvable where **in the public interest** (s 25(3)); requestors may then use it **without consent** (s 26); **s 27(5)(c)** — prohibition on any act to identify an individual from type 2 derived information.
- **ss 29–33** — patient **access restrictions**: class 1 (all information) and class 2 (targeted) restrictions (s 29); a prescribed minimum data set remains visible notwithstanding a restriction (s 30(3)); **emergency "break-glass" override** whether or not the individual consents (s 30(4)); pre-commencement NEHR opt-outs are deemed converted into access restrictions (s 33).
- **ss 38–42** — offences: improper access by users (s 38: up to $50,000/2 years, doubled on repeat; an aggravated tier for access or collection **for an excluded purpose** at s 38(5): up to $100,000/4 years, and $200,000/7 years on a prior qualifying conviction); access by unauthorised persons (s 39: up to $100,000/4 years, PDPA consent expressly no defence, s 39(3)); unauthorised disclosure (s 40); derived-information offences including **re-identification of type 2 derived information** by requestors (s 41(2)(c)) and by anyone (s 42(2), penalty in s 42(3): up to $100,000/4 years).
- **Part 3 (ss 45–60)** — the **non-NEHR sharing channel** for "relevant information" in Fourth Schedule **use cases** (Healthier SG and Age Well SG identification and outreach, between public healthcare institutions, cluster HQs (NHG, NUHS, SingHealth), the Agency for Integrated Care and public agencies): **s 50** — consent and contractual confidentiality overridden; **s 52(2)–(3)** — a written **data sharing agreement** specifying parties, use case, information types and authorised personnel is mandatory; **ss 53–56** — minimum-necessary, genuine-need and use-limitation duties; **s 57** — no onward disclosure (PDPA consent again no gateway, s 57(2)); **s 60** — offences (up to $50,000/2 years; $100,000/4 years on repeat).
- **Part 4 (ss 61–71)** — security: designated compliance individuals (s 65); reasonable data-security controls including data classification (s 66); retention/disposal (s 67); cybersecurity safeguards, extending to health data intermediaries (HDIs) (s 68); mandatory policies (s 69); **incident management framework** (s 70); directions (s 71).
- **Part 5 (ss 72–82)** — notification: assessment and notification of notifiable cybersecurity incidents (ss 74–75) and **notifiable data breaches** (significant harm or significant scale, ss 77–79) to the **Minister**, plus notification of affected individuals for significant-harm breaches (s 80); penalties up to **$1 million** for entities (s 82(1)).
- **Part 7 (ss 86–88)** — **portability**: relevant HDIs must implement practices for accurate, complete and timely transfer of health information at a contributor's/user's request (the anti-lock-in provision aimed at HIMS vendors).
- **s 90** — Minister may issue **codes of practice** on conduct, security and portability.
- **s 113** — related amendment inserting s 28(2)(ba) into the Human Organ Transplant Act 1987 (disclosure "as required or permitted under the Health Information Act 2026").

**Implementation guidance (administrative, pre-commencement):** MOH Circular MOH-MHC-0018-2026 (6 March 2026) sets a batched timeline — **Batch 1 by September 2027** (GP outpatient medical, acute and community hospitals, clinical and radiology laboratories, nuclear medicine services), **Batch 2 by September 2028** (specialist outpatient, nursing homes, contingency care, renal dialysis), **Batch 3 by March 2030** (dental, ambulatory surgery, assisted reproduction, retail pharmacy); unlisted licensees need not contribute but must implement cyber/data security measures by September 2028. Contribution is prospective only. See Part II item 22 for the supporting instruments.

**Executive summary.** The HIA is Singapore's first purpose-built health-information statute and will, once commenced, become the dominant law of health data sharing. It does three separable things: it puts the National Electronic Health Record on a statutory footing with *mandatory* contribution by licensees and access confined to direct patient care and listed statutory examinations; it creates a parallel, non-NEHR channel for sharing "relevant information" between named public-sector actors for gazetted use cases such as Healthier SG outreach, gated by mandatory data sharing agreements; and it overlays a security, breach-notification and portability regime with penalties up to $1 million. Its defining legal move is the displacement of consent: ss 11 and 50 override other law, rules of law and (for Part 3) contractual confidentiality, replacing consent with closed lists of permitted actors, purposes and information types — and seven provisions (ss 16(2), 30(6), 38(7), 39(3), 40(7), 43(6) and 57(2)) shut the door on PDPA consent as an alternative route. Employment and insurance uses are excluded purposes outright, and secondary use is channelled through a ministerially approved "derived information" regime backed by new re-identification offences. Patient control survives only as access restrictions subject to an always-visible minimum data set and an emergency break-glass override. None of it is in force yet: as at 24 August 2026 the Act awaits commencement, and every operative obligation currently rests on the pre-existing statutes below.

## 2. Personal Data Protection Act 2012 (PDPA)

**Citation:** Personal Data Protection Act 2012 (Act 26 of 2012), 2020 Revised Edition; last amended by the Statutes (Miscellaneous Amendments) Act 2025 (Act 19 of 2025) with effect from 5 December 2025.
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; Personal Data Protection Commission (IMDA designated as the Commission, s 5).
**Status:** In force ("Current version as at 24 Aug 2026" on SSO). Part 6B (data portability) and the Twelfth Schedule, inserted by the PDP (Amendment) Act 2020, remain **uncommenced**.

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **s 2(1)** — "personal data" (identifiability, including via other accessible information). **No special/sensitive category exists**; health data is ordinary personal data. "Prescribed healthcare body" is the Act's only health-specific defined term.
- **s 4(1)(c)** — Parts 3–6B impose **no obligation on any public agency** (Government, ministries, organs of state, and statutory bodies specified under s 2(2)). **s 4(6)(b)** — other written law prevails over the PDPA to the extent of inconsistency (the gateway for the HCSA, IDA, NRDA, HBRA, MLSA and, when commenced, the HIA).
- **s 4(4)(b)** — deceased individuals: only the disclosure provisions and s 24 (protection) apply, for 10 years after death.
- **s 13** — consent or statutory authority required for collection, use and disclosure; **s 14(2)(a)** — consent may not be made a condition of service beyond what is reasonable to provide it.
- **s 15(1)–(2)** — deemed consent by conduct, extending down a disclosure chain for the same purpose; **s 15(3)–(9)** — deemed consent by contractual necessity.
- **s 15A** — **deemed consent by notification**: adverse-effect assessment, notification and opt-out; unavailable for direct marketing (reg 13, PDP Regulations 2021).
- **s 17 + First Schedule** — consent exceptions, notably: Part 1 para 1 (vital interests, with after-the-fact notification duty); **Part 1 para 2 (emergency threatening life, health or safety)**; Part 1 para 3 (health/safety seriously affected, consent not timely); **Part 1 para 4 (contacting next-of-kin or friend of an injured, ill or deceased individual)**; Part 3 para 1 (legitimate interests, with documented assessment); Part 3 para 8 (data volunteered by another individual — the PDPC's basis for family-history information, see Part II item 14); Part 4 (business asset transactions — clinic sales); Part 5 (business improvement within corporate groups).
- **s 17 + Second Schedule** — the **research exceptions**:
  - **Use without consent — Second Schedule, Part 2, Division 3, para 1**: research purpose; cannot reasonably be accomplished without identifiable form; **clear public benefit**; results not used to make decisions affecting the individual; published results do not identify individuals. *(Four conditions; no impracticability-of-consent condition.)*
  - **Disclosure without consent — Second Schedule, Part 3, Division 2, para 1**: the same conditions **plus** "(b) it is impracticable for the organisation to seek the individual's consent for the disclosure". *(Five conditions.)*
  - **There is no research exception for collection.** Collection relies on consent, deemed consent, a First Schedule head, or Second Schedule Part 1 para 1 (data disclosed by a public agency, collected consistently with the purpose of that disclosure).
  - **Second Schedule, Part 3, Division 1, para 3** — health-specific: disclosure **to a public agency, for policy formulation or review**, of personal data about an individual who is a current or former **patient of** an HCSA licensee (para 3(b)) or of a **prescribed healthcare body** (para 3(c)) — the paragraph identifies whose patient the individual is, not who discloses. (Para 3(a), which referenced the repealed PHMCA regime, was deleted by Act 19 of 2025 wef 5 December 2025.)
- **Part 6A (ss 26A–26E)** — data breach notification: notifiable if significant harm to an affected individual or significant scale (s 26B); notify the Commission within **3 calendar days** of assessment (s 26D(1)); notify affected individuals for significant-harm breaches (s 26D(2)), excused by remediation or pre-existing technological protection (s 26D(5)); notification breaches no secrecy duty or professional conduct rule (s 26D(8)); **s 26E** — a data intermediary processing for a **public agency** must notify that agency without undue delay (the one Part 6A duty reaching vendors *of public agencies*; s 4(2) preserves both s 26C(3)(a) and s 26E against data intermediaries generally, alongside ss 24–25 under Part 6).
- **Part 9B (ss 48C–48F)** — offences for egregious mishandling by individuals: unauthorised disclosure (s 48D), use for gain/harm (s 48E), and **unauthorised re-identification of anonymised information** (s 48F) — each up to $5,000 and/or 2 years; the **Eleventh Schedule** provides a safe harbour for testing anonymisation effectiveness and security. Part 9B does not apply to relevant public officials in Singapore public sector agencies, or to MAS directors/officers/employees (s 48C(2)) — public officials are covered by PSGA ss 7–8 instead.
- The Act nowhere defines "anonymisation"; anonymised data simply falls outside the s 2(1) definition (see the PDPC guidance in Part II items 15–16).

**Key subsidiary legislation:**

- **Personal Data Protection Regulations 2021 (S 63/2021)**, in force 1 February 2021, last amended by S 86/2026 wef 2 March 2026: cross-border transfer rules (regs 9–12 — comparable protection via law, contract, BCRs, any other legally binding instrument, or certification; S 86/2026 added the Global CBPR and Global PRP systems to the recognised certifications; reg 10(2)(c) carves out transfers necessary for the vital-interests/emergency/next-of-kin and national-interest heads); prescribed content and retention of the s 15A and legitimate-interests assessments (regs 14–15); **reg 16 + First Schedule — the "nearest relative" ladder** for exercising a deceased individual's rights (spouse, child, parent, sibling, other relative) within the 10-year window — a will-appointee or personal representative ranks ahead of the ladder (reg 16(2)).
- **Personal Data Protection (Notification of Data Breaches) Regulations 2021 (S 64/2021)**, in force 1 February 2021, last amended by S 800/2024 wef 15 October 2024: **reg 4** — significant scale = **500 or more** affected individuals; **reg 3(1)(a) + the Schedule** — deemed significant harm where a breach couples the individual's **full name/alias/identification number** with a Schedule Part 1 category, which include: **para 18 — diagnosis/treatment by a health professional of STIs, HIV, schizophrenia or delusional disorder, or substance abuse/addiction**; para 17(d) — health information inside insurance claims; para 19 — donation or receipt of a human egg or sperm, contraception and abortion; para 20 — organ donation/transplant; para 21 — suicide/attempted suicide; para 22 — abuse. Ordinary diagnoses and medication lists are **not** deemed categories — they fall to the general significant-harm assessment.
- **Personal Data Protection (Statutory Bodies) Notification 2013 (S 149/2013)** — gazettes which statutory bodies are "public agencies"; includes the **Health Promotion Board (item 16), Health Sciences Authority (item 17), Communicable Diseases Agency (item 9A)** and Singapore Medical Council (item 55).
- **Personal Data Protection (Prescribed Healthcare Bodies) Notification 2015 (S 90/2015)** (as amended by S 69/2021) — prescribes, for the Second Schedule: **Agency for Integrated Care Pte Ltd, National Healthcare Group Pte Ltd, National University Health System Pte Ltd, Singapore Health Services Pte Ltd**, and approved providers under the Medical and Elderly Care Endowment Schemes Act.
- **The public healthcare clusters and restructured hospitals are *not* public agencies** — they are companies fully subject to the PDPA. Confirmed by their absence from S 149/2013, by the S 90/2015 Schedule listing them as private companies (Pte Ltd), and by the enforcement decision *Re Singapore Health Services Pte Ltd & Ors* [2019] SGPDPC 3 ($250,000 penalty on SingHealth, $750,000 on IHiS, for the 2018 breach).

**Executive summary.** The PDPA is the sector-neutral baseline for health data in the private sector — which, because the clusters and restructured hospitals are companies, includes most of Singapore's hospital system. It has no special regime for health data: sharing identifiable patient information requires consent, deemed consent (by conduct, contractual necessity or notification), another written law, or an enumerated exception. Clinical care runs in practice on deemed consent by conduct; emergencies, family contact and volunteered family history each have a First Schedule head. For secondary research, the Act splits use (four conditions, Second Schedule Part 2 Division 3) from disclosure (the same plus impracticability of consent, Part 3 Division 2), and offers no research basis for fresh collection at all. Anonymised data exits the Act entirely, but the boundary is policed by the s 48F re-identification offence with an Eleventh Schedule testing safe harbour. Breach notification runs on a two-limb trigger with a three-day clock, and the NDB Regulations single out precisely the diagnoses society treats as most stigmatising — HIV/STIs, schizophrenia or delusional disorder, substance abuse and addiction, gamete donation/receipt, contraception and abortion, suicide — as deemed-harm categories notifiable to patients regardless of scale. Public agencies (MOH, HPB, HSA, CDA) are excluded from the operative Parts altogether, which is why the PSGA (item 7) and the Government's internal manuals matter; and s 4(6)(b) subordinates Parts 3–6B of the Act to the sectoral statutes (the Do Not Call and offence Parts are not subordinated), so PDPA analysis is never the final word on a Singapore health-data flow.

## 3. Healthcare Services Act 2020 (HCSA)

**Citation:** Healthcare Services Act 2020 (No. 3 of 2020); phased commencement in three tranches — 3 January 2022 (S 1030/2021), 26 June 2023 and 18 December 2023 (S 799/2023) — save that **s 28 (approval of employment of individuals by certain licensees) remains uncommenced** as at August 2026.
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; MOH — administered by the Director-General of Health (s 7).
**Status:** In force ("Current version" on SSO; no HCSA commencement notification has been gazetted since S 799/2023).

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **s 5** — the Act does not apply to healthcare services provided by the Government.
- **s 13** — open-textured **licence conditions** power (the vehicle for data-related conditions, e.g. on telemedicine and genetic testing services).
- **s 27** — the core statutory records provision: licensees must keep and maintain, for prescribed periods and in the prescribed manner, records **relevant to the monitoring or evaluation of a licensable healthcare service or its provision** (s 27(1)); implement prescribed safeguards protecting the records **and any computer system used to keep and maintain them** against accidental or unlawful loss, modification or destruction, and unauthorised access, disclosure, copying, use or modification (s 27(2)(a)); monitor and evaluate those safeguards (s 27(2)(b)); ensure staff awareness of confidentiality, integrity and availability roles (s 27(2)(c)); take care in disposal (s 27(3)); not keep inaccurate records (s 27(4)). Offence: up to **$20,000 and/or 12 months** (s 27(5)). The *patient health record* duty is imposed not by s 27 but by regs 37–38 below, made under s 57(2)(d) and (p).
- **s 36** — Director-General's power to require any licensee to produce documents and information, including in legible form.
- **s 38** — **codes of practice** power; the s 38(2) subject-matter list covers conduct, clinical and ethical aspects, management and care of patients — but contains **no express data-security or portability head** (contrast s 57(2)(p), which *is* an express regulation-making head for record safeguards including the computer systems holding records; the gap HIA s 90 fills is in the codes power).
- **s 51(2)** — the Director-General and authorised officers **must not disclose medical record information** except (a) for administering a closed list of twelve Acts (including the HBRA, IDA, NRDA, MediShield Life Scheme Act, Human Organ Transplant Act), (b) for professional-discipline complaints, or (c) for any other purpose **with the consent of the person to whom the information relates or their representative**; **s 51(1)** — non-compellability in proceedings.
- **s 57(2)(d)** — regulation-making power for "the records of patients, customers and staff that licensees must keep and maintain".
- **s 58** — repeals the Private Hospitals and Medical Clinics Act (effective 18 December 2023; see item 13).

**Key subsidiary legislation — Healthcare Services (General) Regulations 2021 (S 1035/2021)** (version in force from 14 October 2024):

- **reg 25** — security of equipment data: protection against unauthorised local or remote electronic access; regular review; secure transmission to authorised recipients.
- **reg 37** — mandatory content of the **patient health record** (identity fields; and where available: consultations, history, clinical findings, care plans, laboratory and imaging results, allergies, consent forms, discharge summaries, adverse events, etc.), kept as the Director-General specifies.
- **reg 38** — duty to keep every patient health record **confidential**, maintain confidentiality/integrity/security at all times, extend the same duties to **extracts and aggregated compilations** (reg 38(2)), implement and monitor safeguards, and take care in disposal.
- **reg 39** — on cessation or transfer of care: consult the patient about transfer/disposal of the record and **transfer the record or a detailed medical report** to the incoming licensee, or (upon request) to the patient/authorised representative — currently the only operative data-portability rule in the sector.
- **Healthcare Services (General) (Amendment) Regulations 2024 (S 780/2024)**, wef 14 October 2024 — inserted **reg 39A**: a licensee must ensure every medical certificate is issued by a personnel who is a medical practitioner or dentist and who had provided medical or dental care to the patient, and that it states the practitioner's name and registration number (aimed at questionnaire-only telemedicine).

**Executive summary.** Until the HIA commences, the HCSA is the principal sectoral statute imposing health-record obligations on Singapore's licensed (non-Government) providers, and its posture is custody, not sharing: s 27 and regs 37–38 dictate what a patient record must contain and how it must be secured, monitored and destroyed, extending expressly to extracts and aggregated compilations; reg 39 supplies the only current portability mechanism (record transfer on change of provider, after consulting the patient); and s 51(2) locks up what the regulator itself learns, subject to a closed list of statutory gateways. Outbound sharing by licensees is left to the PDPA and the other statutes in this Part. Its forward-looking significance is structural: the licence-condition power (s 13) and codes-of-practice power (s 38) are the hooks on which MOH hangs instruments like the telemedicine licence conditions and the genetic-testing code of practice (Part II item 20), and the HIA is expressly designed to sit on top of HCSA licensing — the First Schedule of the HIA maps contribution duties onto HCSA licence categories.

## 4. Human Biomedical Research Act 2015 (HBRA)

**Citation:** Human Biomedical Research Act 2015 (Act 29 of 2015), 2020 Revised Edition; version in force from 5 December 2025 (amended by the Healthcare Services Act 2020 wef 3 January 2022, the Healthcare Services (Amendment) Act 2023 (Act 11 of 2023) wef 1 May 2023, and the Statutes (Miscellaneous Amendments) Act 2025 (Act 19 of 2025) wef 5 December 2025). Staged commencement: 1 July 2016; 1 January 2017 (ss 32–33); **1 November 2017** (ss 6–31, 65, 68, Third–Fifth Schedules); **1 November 2019** (ss 34–39, 64).
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; MOH — administered by the **Director-General of Health** (s 4(1); the office of "Director" was replaced by "Director-General" by Act 11 of 2023 wef 1 May 2023).
**Status:** In force.

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **s 3(1)–(2)** — "human biomedical research" includes any research within scope that involves **(e) the use of individually-identifiable human biological material or (f) individually-identifiable health information** — identifiability, not sensitivity, is the trigger.
- **s 2** — "individually-identifiable" uses a linkage/mosaic test ("other information to which the person … has or is likely to have access"); "health information" covers information obtained in providing healthcare or relating to diagnosis etc.; "research subject" includes a person whose material or information is used.
- **s 3(4) + Second Schedule** — carve-outs: national public health research under IDA s 59A; NRDA collection; Statistics Act collection; **clinical trials under the Health Products Act 2007 (para 6) and the Medicines Act 1975 (para 7)** — trial data is governed by the HSA regimes in item 12, not by the HBRA.
- **Part 3 (ss 6–14)** — consent: written, witnessed "appropriate consent" (s 6), preceded by the s 12 disclosures, which expressly cover **future secondary research use of individually-identifiable information (s 12(1)(i))**, identifiable-form participation (s 12(1)(k)), re-identification on incidental findings (s 12(1)(m)) and, for tissue, **export from Singapore (s 12(2)(q))**. Withdrawal does not claw back information already obtained (s 14(3)).
- **s 13(1)(b) + Fifth Schedule, Part 2** — the **IRB consent waiver for secondary use** of biological material or health information: para 3 requires that the research cannot reasonably proceed without identifiable form, obtaining consent would involve disproportionate effort, minimal risk, no adverse effect on rights/welfare, and contribution to the **"greater public good"**; paras 4–5 cover legacy information/material collected before 1 November 2017 (para 5 uniquely requires reasonable re-contact efforts for legacy material).
- **ss 27–29 — the data-protection core**: **s 27(1)–(2)** — safeguard duty over individually-identifiable information/material, expressly including "rendering information or material non-identifiable" (defined, recipient-relative, in s 27(3)) — up to $10,000/12 months; **s 28** — **re-identification prohibition** for data rendered non-identifiable, subject to listed exceptions — up to $20,000/2 years; **s 29(1)** — **prohibition on disclosing research subjects' individually-identifiable information** that came to the person's knowledge in discharging functions or duties under the Act or through conducting or reviewing the research, with a downstream-recipient limb (s 29(2): recipients who knew or had reason to believe the information was unlawfully communicated) — up to $20,000/2 years. **s 39** mirrors s 29 for **tissue donors** — and is broader, carrying no functions-or-duties gateway.
- **s 22(2)(c) and (6)** — conducting research without appropriate consent for the use of identifiable material/information: up to **$50,000/5 years**.
- **Part 4 (ss 15–21)** — statutory IRBs: appointment and notification (s 15); composition, quorum and proceedings (s 18; reg 11(2) of the 2017 Regulations: at least 5 members, medically qualified chair, at least one external scientific and one external lay member); exemption and expedited review (s 17(2); regs 20–21). (The research-regulation provisions — including s 22 and the ss 27–29 data-protection core — sit in Part 5, ss 22–31.)
- **Part 6 (ss 32–39)** — tissue banking, including **s 35(2)(f)–(g)** (export and removal-from-supervision controls).
- **Third Schedule** — *prohibited* research; **Fourth Schedule** — *restricted* research (human eggs/embryos, cytoplasmic hybrids, human-animal combinations — **not genomic data**: there is no genomics-specific statutory tier; genomic data sharing runs on the general provisions).
- **ss 40–41** — codes of practice issued or approved by the Director-General under s 40 have **no legislative effect** (s 40(3)) but are admissible in evidence and must be had regard to in assessing reasonableness (s 41(2)–(3)). (Whether the BAC Guidelines qualify as s 40 codes is not established — their practical weight runs through MOH's governance framework and IRB adoption; see item 17.)

**Key subsidiary legislation (all republished in 2025 editions):**

- **Human Biomedical Research Regulations 2017 (S 621/2017)** — institutional duties; **reg 10D**: every research institution must have, and inform subjects of, a policy on re-identification and return of **incidental findings**; IRB composition and review procedures (regs 11–22).
- **HBR (Restricted Research) Regulations 2017 (S 622/2017)** — approvals and special consent for restricted research.
- **HBR (Tissue Banking) Regulations 2019 (S 702/2019)** — **reg 15(4)**: export of individually-identifiable tissue requires **donor consent to the export plus a recipient undertaking**; reg 16: confidentiality-system duty (offence); reg 19: tissue-bank incidental-findings policy. There is **no equivalent statutory export gate for health *information*** — a genuine gap.
- **HBR (Requirements for Appropriate Consent — Exemption) Regulations 2019 (S 703/2019)** — **reg 2**: the prescribed-witness requirement is disapplied for non-invasive, non-interventional, non-restricted research, with survey/information-collection research deemed such — the practical enabler of pure data research.
- **HBR (Tissue Banking and Notification — Exemption) Regulations 2019 (S 704/2019)** — reg 2 disapplies ss 34–37 where tissue banking is conducted **solely** for the entity's own research or clinical trial falling within IDA s 59A national public health research, Health Products Act 2007 clinical trials, or Medicines Act 1975 clinical trials — a mirror of the Second Schedule paras 3, 6 and 7 carve-outs, not a general self-banking exemption (reg 2(2) reimposes ss 34–37 on any activity not solely for those purposes).
- *(The HBR (Exemption) Regulations 2018 were transitional and expired on 31 October 2019 — they are sometimes miscited as current.)*

**Executive summary.** The HBRA is the operative statute for research use of Singapore health and genomic data, and it bites on identifiability: any research within the Act's purpose gate that uses individually-identifiable health information or biological material is "human biomedical research" requiring IRB approval (or a s 17(2) exemption) and written consent whose content must cover future secondary use (the witness requirement being disapplied for survey and information-collection research by S 703/2019 reg 2), and the linkage-based definition of identifiability makes genomic datasets hard to hold outside the Act. The lawful route to sharing and secondary use without fresh consent is the Fifth Schedule Part 2 IRB waiver, whose "disproportionate effort / minimal risk / greater public good" test is the workhorse of Singapore's retrospective and databank research. Once data is held, three offences apply: a safeguard duty naming de-identification as a compliance measure (s 27), a re-identification ban (s 28), and a disclosure ban that follows the data downstream to recipients on a knew-or-ought-to-know basis (s 29, mirrored for tissue donors by s 39). Two structural points are routinely misstated and worth fixing: the Fourth Schedule's "restricted research" tier is about embryos and human-animal combinations, not genetics — Singapore has no genomics-specific statutory tier — and clinical-trial data is carved out of the HBRA entirely and governed by the thinner HSA records regimes. Cross-border movement is statutorily controlled only for tissue (reg 15(4)), not for health information as such, leaving data-export protection to consent terms, the PDPA transfer rules and contracts.

## 5. Infectious Diseases Act 1976 (IDA)

**Citation:** Infectious Diseases Act 1976, 2020 Revised Edition; version in force from 1 April 2025 (as amended by the Infectious Diseases (Amendment) Act 2024 (Act 13 of 2024) wef 9 April 2024 and the Communicable Diseases Agency Act 2025 (Act 4 of 2025) wef 1 April 2025).
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; MOH / Director-General of Health, with operational functions since 1 April 2025 exercised through the **Communicable Diseases Agency**.
**Status:** In force.

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **s 6(1)–(3)** — criminal **notification duties**: every medical practitioner who has reason to believe a patient has a prescribed infectious disease or is a carrier must notify the Director-General; so must persons in charge of diagnostic laboratories and prescribed classes of persons. **s 6(6)** — reverse presumption of knowledge. Prescribed diseases and timelines: Infectious Diseases (Notification of Prescribed Infectious Diseases) Regulations 2008 (S 612/2008, last amended S 189/2025).
- **s 7(2)** — surveillance: the Director-General may require **any person or class of persons** to provide information and samples and submit to examination.
- **s 10(1)** — power to require any healthcare professional to obtain information from a patient and transmit it; **s 10(4)** — the professional must comply **"despite any restriction on the disclosure of information imposed by any written law, rule of law, rule of professional conduct or contract"** — the strongest confidentiality override currently in force in the health sector.
- **s 25(1)** — the Act's principal confidentiality prohibition (see also the recipient bar in s 25A(7)), and it is **HIV/STI-specific**: persons performing functions under the Act must not disclose identifying information about a person with HIV or an STI, subject to twelve exceptions (consent; administration of the Act; police under specified CPC provisions; court order; treating clinicians; blood/organ/semen/milk banks; **anonymised statistics and epidemiology (g)**; sexual assault victims; the Controller of Immigration; next-of-kin on death; **a Director-General public-interest gateway (k)**; ministerially authorised public-health publication). Offence: up to $10,000/3 months (s 25(2)).
- **s 25A** — authorised disclosures of HIV status to exposed healthcare workers and first responders (s 25A(1)–(2)) and **partner notification** by a practitioner under strict preconditions (s 25A(4)), with an onward-disclosure bar on recipients (s 25A(7)).
- **s 57A** — the Director-General may disclose identifying information about actual or suspected cases/at-risk individuals **to anyone who needs it to prevent spread**, and **to the WHO or a State Party to the WHO** to meet international obligations; recipients are purpose-limited (s 57A(3)) and conditions may be imposed (s 57A(2)).
- **s 57B** — the Director-General may, by written notice, authorise **provider-to-provider disclosure** of identifying information to specified recipients for outbreak prevention, with conditions and purpose limitation (s 57B(3)).
- **s 59A** — **national public health research**: the Director-General may undertake or facilitate research in the public interest (s 59A(1)–(2)) and require any person or class of persons to provide information and samples, including human samples (s 59A(3)(a)); where a medical practitioner must obtain information or samples from patients under their care, this must be done **with the "requisite consent"** (s 59A(3)(b), defined in s 59A(8)); information and human samples **must be anonymised before being furnished unless the Director-General directs otherwise** (s 59A(4)), identifiable material may be required only where the research can be carried out only with it (s 59A(6)), and deliberate re-identification of anonymised material received under the section is an offence (s 59A(7)(b)). Research so conducted is excluded from the HBRA (HBRA Second Schedule para 3).
- **s 67A** — immunity: good-faith, reasonable-care disclosure under the Act or a s 57B authorisation is no offence, no breach of confidence, no civil wrong and no disciplinary matter.
- **ss 46–54 (Part 6)** — vaccination and other prophylaxis (s 46 childhood vaccination; s 47 outbreak vaccination orders binding any person or class of persons); **s 48(2)** — every vaccinator must record all vaccinations and notify both the Director-General of Health or designated CDA officers **and** the vaccinated person (or the child's parent/guardian) — the statutory feed of the **National Immunisation Registry** (which has no express establishing provision of its own).

**Executive summary.** The IDA is the most sharing-permissive statute in the landscape, built principally of compelled-disclosure duties and disclosure-authorising powers. Practitioners and laboratories must notify prescribed diseases on pain of criminal penalty with a reverse burden; the Director-General can compel information and samples from anyone for surveillance and, under s 59A, for national public health research that is carved out of the HBRA altogether — though s 59A carries its own counterweights (a consent gate for practitioner-obtained patient material, an anonymise-before-furnishing default, a necessity test for identifiable material, and a re-identification offence); and s 10(4) overrides every source of confidentiality — statutory, common-law, professional and contractual — when the Director-General requires patient information for outbreak control. Downstream, ss 57A and 57B allow identifying information to flow to whoever needs it to stop transmission (including the WHO), disciplined only by purpose-limitation duties on recipients and a good-faith immunity for disclosers. Against this, the Act's principal confidentiality prohibition, s 25, protects only HIV/STI identity and is porous — twelve exceptions, including an open-ended Director-General public-interest gateway — supplemented by the onward-disclosure bar on s 25A recipients. Since 1 April 2025 the operational machinery (including the National Immunisation Registry, fed by s 48(2) vaccination notifications) sits with the new Communicable Diseases Agency (item 11).

## 6. National Registry of Diseases Act 2007 (NRDA)

**Citation:** National Registry of Diseases Act 2007 (Act 56 of 2007), 2020 Revised Edition; version in force from **5 December 2025** (amended by the Statutes (Miscellaneous Amendments) Act 2025 (Act 19 of 2025), which re-anchored the definition of "healthcare institution" on HCSA-approved premises for nine named licensable services — extensible by prescription of further services and by ministerial declaration of facilities — replacing the obsolete PHMCA-based definition).
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; MOH — Registrar appointed by the Director-General (s 3); administered through the National Registry of Diseases Office.
**Status:** In force; commenced 1 August 2009.

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **s 4–s 5** — establishes the National Registry of Diseases and requires the Registrar to maintain a register of **individually-identifiable information** for each reportable disease; the registers are **not open to public inspection** (s 5(2)).
- **The Schedule** — reportable diseases: **cancer** (including CNS tumours of all behaviours), donor nephrectomy and hepatic resection, **chronic kidney failure**, **acute myocardial infarction**.
- **s 6(1)** — the **manager of a healthcare institution must notify** the Registrar of diagnoses/treatment of reportable diseases (offence: fine up to $2,000, s 6(2)).
- **s 7(2)** — the Registrar may require additional prescribed information and **production of medical records for inspection**.
- **s 8(1)–(3)** — Registry personnel are non-compellable in proceedings and prohibited from disclosing register contents or identifiable information (offence: up to $10,000/12 months).
- **s 9** — release of Registry information **in anonymised form** on request (and mandatorily to the Director-General).
- **s 10** — identifiable disclosure for **national public health programmes** requires Director-General approval, and **s 10(3)** bars approval unless the Director-General "is satisfied that the national public health programme cannot be carried out with anonymised information" — a statutory anonymisation-first test.
- **s 11** — identifiable disclosure to a treating practitioner requires necessity **and the requisite consent** (s 11(c)).
- **s 12** — identifiable disclosure for **public health research** requires that the research cannot be done with anonymised information (s 12(2)(a)), **the requisite consent (s 12(2)(b))**, a quality/public-health benefit finding, and compliance conditions; researchers may not re-disclose identities, nor identify institutions or notifiers without their consent (s 12(3)–(4)); the Registrar may refer applications to a national research advisory committee (s 12(5)).

**Executive summary.** The NRDA is a narrow but instructive statute: it compels identifiable disease reporting into closed national registers (cancer, kidney failure, AMI and donor events), then guards release with the strictest necessity architecture in Singapore health-data law. Anonymised release is comparatively open; identifiable release is permitted only where the purpose *cannot* be achieved with anonymised data — a statutory data-minimisation mandate (ss 10(3), 12(2)(a)) with no counterpart in the PDPA or the HIA — and, for treatment and research, only with consent. It thus sits at the opposite pole from the IDA and the HIA's consent-override architecture, and it matters practically to researchers because registry linkage is a staple of Singapore epidemiology: any project seeking identifiable registry data must budget for the s 12 gate, and after 5 December 2025 the reporting duty attaches to HCSA-approved premises across nine named licensable service categories (extensible by prescription and ministerial declaration).

## 7. Public Sector (Governance) Act 2018 (PSGA)

**Citation:** Public Sector (Governance) Act 2018 (Act 5 of 2018), 2020 Revised Edition; commenced 1 April 2018; version in force from 1 August 2026. The **Public Sector (Governance) (Amendment) Act 2026 (Act 2 of 2026)** (passed 12 January 2026, assented 3 February 2026, gazetted 16 February 2026) is **not yet in force** — no commencement notification as at 29 August 2026 (re-verified against the full 2026 Subsidiary Legislation Supplement).
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; PMO / MDDI (public-sector data governance).
**Status:** In force, as amended up to Act 3 of 2026 wef 1 August 2026 (but unamended by Act 2 of 2026, which awaits commencement).

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **s 2(1)** — "Singapore public sector agency" = public bodies (bodies corporate established by public Act — which **excludes** the healthcare clusters, as companies), ministries and departments (including MOH), organs of state and declared officials. HPB and HSA are Group 1A public bodies (First Schedule, Part 1, items 14–15).
- **s 4(1)–(4)** — ministerial **directions** to agencies on "pertinent subject matter", expressly including **data governance and inter-agency data sharing (s 4(3)(e))** and data analytics (s 4(2)(c)); directions may take the form of circulars or instructions (s 9(1)(a)) and have **no legislative effect** (s 9(2)) but must be complied with (s 10). This is the statutory frame for the (unpublished) Government Instruction Manual on ICT&SS Management ("IM8").
- **s 6(1)** — where a data sharing direction is given, sharing with **another Singapore public sector agency** is authorised **"despite any obligation as to confidentiality under the common law"**; legal privilege and contractual confidentiality are preserved (s 6(2)).
- **s 7** — offences: knowing/reckless **unauthorised disclosure** by a relevant public official (s 7(1)); unauthorised **use** with gain/harm/loss (s 7(3)); contractor misuse — but **only for information other than personal data** (s 7(4); contractors' personal-data misconduct is caught by PDPA Part 9B instead). Each: fine up to $5,000 and/or 2 years.
- **s 8** — knowing/reckless **unauthorised re-identification** of anonymised information under an agency's control (same penalty), with a two-limb safe harbour requiring both a reasonable belief that the re-identification was for a PDPA Eleventh Schedule specified purpose **and** notification of the agency or GovTech as soon as practicable (s 8(2)(c), (4)).
- **Amendment Act 2026 (uncommenced)** — will convert "data sharing directions" into "data sharing **and use** directions"; authorise sharing with **non-public sector persons** subject to ministerial "further authorisation" (new s 4(5)–(8)); extend offences to employees of recipient organisations (new s 7(1A), (4A), again excluding personal data); and insert PDPA s 48C(3) so that Part 9B offences attach to personal data shared out under a direction. MDDI frames this as enabling data flows to community and social-sector partners.

**Executive summary.** The PSGA is the public sector's answer to the PDPA's absence: since public agencies are excluded from the PDPA's operative Parts, intra-government data sharing (MOH, HPB, HSA, CDA and every ministry) runs on ministerial data-sharing directions that override common-law confidentiality between agencies, backed by criminal offences for unauthorised disclosure, misuse and re-identification by public officials — offences enacted with the PSGA itself (in force 1 April 2018, before the SingHealth breach) and **expanded** post-SingHealth by the PDP (Amendment) Act 2020 wef 1 February 2021, which recast the use offence around gain/harm/loss, split out the contractor offence (non-personal data only) and added the re-identification safe harbour. Two boundaries define its reach into health data. First, the public healthcare clusters and restructured hospitals are companies, not public bodies, so PSGA directions cannot authorise MOH–cluster flows; those run instead on the PDPA's public-agency disclosure exceptions (Second Schedule Part 3 Division 1 paras 1 and 3) — and, prospectively, on HIA Part 3. Second, PSGA directions are administrative and unpublished, so a material layer of the rules actually governing government-held health data (IM8 and the Government Data Security Policies incorporating the 2019 PSDSRC measures) is not publicly citable. The 2026 Amendment Act will, when commenced, extend directed sharing beyond government to vetted external partners with a two-key authorisation structure — passed the same day as the HIA, it is the other half of Singapore's 2026 data-sharing settlement, and equally not yet in force.

## 8. Cybersecurity Act 2018

**Citation:** Cybersecurity Act 2018 (Act 9 of 2018), 2020 Revised Edition; principal commencement 31 August 2018 (except ss 24–35 and the Second Schedule — the Part 5 licensing regime, commenced 11 April 2022); as amended by the **Cybersecurity (Amendment) Act 2024 (Act 19 of 2024)** — passed 7 May 2024, key provisions commenced **31 October 2025** (S 677/2025); version in force from 31 October 2025.
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; Commissioner of Cybersecurity / Cyber Security Agency (CSA).
**Status:** In force. New Parts 3C (entities of special cybersecurity interest) and 3D (foundational digital infrastructure — cloud and data centres) are enacted but **uncommenced**.

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **First Schedule — essential services**: healthcare appears as two items — **item 10 "Acute hospital care services"** and **item 11 "Services relating to disease surveillance and response"** (the First Schedule's 46 services sit under 11 sector headings).
- **s 7(1)** — designation of **provider-owned critical information infrastructure (CII)** (the 2024 Act renamed the concept); **s 7(1A)** — extension to systems located wholly outside Singapore; "computer/computer system" now includes **virtual** systems (s 2, as amended).
- **ss 10, 12–16** — CII owner duties: furnish information on design/configuration/security (s 10); comply with written directions (s 12); notify ownership changes within 7 days (s 13, penalty up to $100,000/2 years); **report prescribed cybersecurity incidents** affecting the CII and interconnected systems (s 14); biennial compliance audits and **annual** cybersecurity risk assessments, each reported within 30 days (s 15(1)–(2)); participate in exercises (s 16). *(Former s 11 — codes of practice — was deleted by the 2024 Act; the power now lives in s 35A.)*
- **Part 3A (ss 16A–16L)** — the 2024 Act's **third-party-owned CII regime**: a provider of essential services running its CII on a system it does not own (s 16A(1)(b) — cloud being the paradigm case but not the statutory limit) can be designated as responsible, and must then obtain **legally binding commitments** from the owner that prescribed cybersecurity standards will be maintained (s 16F) and that incidents will be notified (s 16I) — on pain of being ordered to stop using the system (ss 16F(2)–(3), 16I(2)). Operationalised by the Cybersecurity (Providers of Essential Service Responsible for Cybersecurity of Third-Party-Owned CII) Regulations 2026 (S 490/2026), in force 13 July 2026.
- **s 35A** — codes of practice. The operative code is the **Cybersecurity Code of Practice for Critical Information Infrastructure 2026 (CCoP 2026, Release 1)**, issued under s 35A(1)(a)(i) — so binding owners of *provider-owned* CII only, not Part 3A designated providers — effective **29 July 2026**, with phased compliance to 29 July 2027 (board accountability, interconnected-systems section) and 31 December 2027 (Cyber Trust Mark Advocate (Tier 5) certification, cl 3.3.1); a separate cloud-services CCoP, filling the Part 3A gap, is planned for H2 2026.
- Interface: **HIA s 83(2)** expressly preserves the Cybersecurity Act's operation alongside HIA Part 6.

**Executive summary.** The Cybersecurity Act protects the systems on which health data lives rather than the data itself, and reaches healthcare through two designated essential services — acute hospital care and disease surveillance/response. A designated hospital EMR or national surveillance platform carries information-furnishing, incident-reporting, audit and exercise duties, and since 31 October 2025 the amended Act closes the cloud gap: a hospital whose critical system runs on a hyperscaler must now bind that provider contractually to prescribed security and incident-notification standards or be ordered off the platform, with the 2026 regulations and the CCoP 2026 supplying the operative detail. The enacted-but-uncommenced Parts 3C and 3D will eventually regulate major cloud and data-centre providers directly. For health data governance the Act matters as the third layer of a stack: a healthcare provider can simultaneously face CSA obligations here, PDPC breach-notification obligations under the PDPA, and — once the HIA commences — MOH-facing incident and breach notification under HIA Parts 4–5, which are expressly cumulative with this Act.

## 9. MediShield Life Scheme Act 2015 (MLSA)

**Citation:** MediShield Life Scheme Act 2015 (Act 4 of 2015), 2020 Revised Edition; version in force from 1 April 2025 (amended by Act 40 of 2024).
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; MOH/CPF Board.
**Status:** In force.

**Pinpoint provisions (Part 5 — Disclosure of information, ss 26–30):**

- **s 26** — ministerially certified requisitions of **confidential information** held by Government departments and public authorities for administering the Scheme or disbursing healthcare-related subsidies; the holding entity "is to comply".
- **s 27(1)** — certified requests for **health information held by HCSA-licensed medical institutions and the practitioners they employ or engage who attended the patient**, to assess pre-existing-condition premium loading or claims; **s 27(2)(b)(ii)** — onward disclosure to an insurer only as a report stating **whether** a pre-existing condition exists "(without identifying the pre-existing medical condition)" and the loading amount; **s 27(3)–(4)** — patient **opt-out** right, but only from the s 27(1)(a) premium-loading limb (no opt-out from claims assessment); minors from age 16, provided no person liable for the minor's premium objects.
- **s 28** — certified requests for **means information** to assess eligibility for grants/subsidies/benefits or to determine whether the Part 3 premium-recovery powers should be exercised (s 28(1A)); onward provision only as an **eligibility-tier report** (s 28(3)); opt-outs from provision (s 28(4)) and separately from disbursement disclosures (s 28(5)); minors from 16, subject to no objection by the premium payer (s 28(6)).
- **s 29(1)** — offence of unconsented access/use/disclosure of information obtained under ss 26–28 (up to $5,000/12 months); **s 29(2)** — good-faith immunity covering statutory offences, breach of confidence, civil liability and professional discipline; **s 29(3)** — ss 26–28 and the immunity apply "**despite any other written law**" (s 29(3)(a)) and whether or not the person is under any obligation of confidence "imposed by any written law **or otherwise**" (s 29(3)(b)).

**Executive summary.** The MLSA's Part 5 is easy to overlook and remarkable in its drafting: to run national health insurance and means-tested subsidies, it lets the Minister certify **requests** for clinical information from HCSA-licensed institutions and their attending practitioners — requests s 27 does not make compellable, but which s 29(2) immunises against criminal, civil and disciplinary consequence — and compellable requisitions of confidential government-held information (s 26) and means data from relevant authorities (s 28), applying "despite any other written law" and irrespective of any obligation of confidence (s 29(3)). Its saving graces are equally instructive: genuine patient opt-outs (ss 27(3), 28(4)–(5), limited to the premium-loading and disbursement limbs) and hard output-minimisation — an insurer may learn only *that* a pre-existing condition exists and the loading, never the diagnosis, and a scheme administrator only the eligibility tier. It is the clearest existing model of the "statutory authorisation + minimisation" design the HIA now generalises.

## 10. Human Organ Transplant Act 1987 (HOTA)

## 12. Clinical trials regulations (HSA regimes)

**Citations:** **Health Products (Clinical Trials) Regulations 2016 (S 331/2016)** (made under s 72 Health Products Act 2007 by the Health Sciences Authority with the Minister's approval; commenced 1 November 2016) and **Medicines (Clinical Trials) Regulations 2016 (S 335/2016)** (made under s 18 Medicines Act 1975 by the Minister for Health; commenced 1 November 2016); both consolidations in force from 18 December 2023.
**Type / administering body:** Subsidiary legislation; Health Sciences Authority (regulator).
**Status:** In force. Clinical trials under these regimes are **excluded from the HBRA** (HBRA Second Schedule paras 6–7).

**Pinpoint provisions:** **reg 19(1)(n)–(o)** (both sets) — trial subjects must be told **who will access their medical records** (including HSA's inspection power) and how far identifying records will be kept confidential. **reg 13 + First Schedule para 11** (both sets) — the GCP principles bind sponsors and principal investigators, including para 11: "The confidentiality of records that could identify subjects must be protected…" — criminally enforceable (S 331/2016 reg 29(1)(a): up to $20,000/12 months; S 335/2016 reg 28(1)(a): up to $5,000/2 years). **reg 23** (both) — sponsor/investigator records enabling evaluation of "the quality of the data produced", available to HSA at all times, retained until the latest of several dates, one of which is **6 years after the conclusion of the trial**. **regs 23A(3)–(4)** (S 331/2016 only) — for cell/tissue/gene therapy products, a mandatory system linking product to subject "and vice versa", retained at least **30 years** after product expiry (or a shorter period HSA allows in a particular case) — a statutorily compelled long-term identifiability link. **reg 27** (S 331/2016 only) — a permissive gateway (for the purposes of HPA s 66(2)(d)) specifying when **HSA may disclose** confidential information relating to an investigational health product (commercial confidentiality, not subject data); **reg 28** — a discretionary power for HSA to publish trial particulars in a clinical trial register. The Medicines regulations contain no confidentiality *regulation* and no register provision (their reg 27 is an information-gathering power) — though the First Schedule GCP confidentiality principle applies in both regimes.

**Executive summary.** Clinical-trial data sits in its own lane: carved out of the HBRA, it is governed by consent-disclosure, GCP and records-and-inspection rules rather than by the HBRA's specific safeguard, re-identification and disclosure offences (ss 27–29). Sponsor-side confidentiality is not absent — the GCP principle in First Schedule para 11 of both regulations requires protection of records that could identify subjects, criminally enforceable through the reg 13 conduct duty — but it is a generalised principle rather than an articulated offence structure. Subject protection otherwise runs through disclosure at consent (who sees their records, including HSA), and the CTGT traceability rules cut the other way entirely, compelling a product-to-subject identifiability link kept for at least 30 years. Only the Health Products set carries the HSA disclosure gateway and register-publication powers, and the two regulations have different makers (HSA versus the Minister).

# Part II — Codes of practice, advisory guidelines, ethics guidance and policy instruments

## 14. PDPC Advisory Guidelines for the Healthcare Sector

**Citation/status:** Issued 11 September 2014; **revised 20 September 2023** (current as at 24 August 2026 — not yet updated for the HIA). Issued by the PDPC under **PDPA s 49(1)**, developed with MOH. **Advisory only** — not legally binding; the Act and Regulations prevail on inconsistency.

**Pinpoint positions:** para 2.3 — defines deemed consent by conduct, which paras 2.18–2.19 then apply to the clinical episode; para 2.18 (worked example) — registration at a hospital is deemed consent to collection/use/disclosure for the visit and related care, including internal case conferences; **para 2.24** — consent for treatment covers maintaining records on a database and **disclosing records to other healthcare institutions through that database** for the consented purpose (the key paragraph for shared-records design); para 2.21 — a relative's family history volunteered by the patient is collected without the relative's consent under First Schedule Part 3 para 8; para 2.22 — teaching materials need notification/consent unless anonymised; internal quality assurance generally does not; para 2.23 — emergencies run on First Schedule Part 1 para 2, with a recommendation that the collapsed patient himself be notified of the collection, use or disclosure and its purpose as soon as practicable; paras 2.14–2.16 and worked example 2.27 — the research exceptions, expressly locating use in Second Schedule Part 2 Division 3 and adding the impracticability condition for disclosure, with IRB opinion as a relevant consideration; para 4.4–4.5 — retention (no fixed statutory period; MOH's 2022 Revised Guidelines for the Retention Periods of Medical Records flagged); para 5.2 — organisations must continue to comply with the PHMC Act, HCSA, NRDA, IDA and Advance Medical Directive Act, which prevail (PDPA s 4(6)(b)). *(Negative finding: the current edition contains no general next-of-kin disclosure guidance — "next-of-kin" appears nowhere; the statutory basis for family contact remains First Schedule Part 1 para 4.)*

**Executive summary.** This is the only PDPC instrument written specifically for health data and the primary interpretive text mapping the PDPA onto clinical practice. Its central moves are to rest routine care on deemed consent by conduct — stretched, at para 2.24, to cover shared clinical databases serving the treatment purpose — while holding a restrictive line on secondary purposes: no bundling of research or teaching consents into care, anonymise where possible, and use the two-tier research exception (with documented impracticability for disclosure) where not. Chapter 5 is its most consequential doctrinal statement: the PDPA yields to the sectoral statutes, so these Guidelines are the start, never the end, of a Singapore health-data analysis. A real currency gap: revised September 2023, they say nothing about the HIA.

## 15. PDPC Advisory Guidelines — Key Concepts and Selected Topics

**Citations/status:** *Advisory Guidelines on Key Concepts in the PDPA* — issued 23 September 2013, **revised 29 April 2026** (current). *Advisory Guidelines on the PDPA for Selected Topics* — issued 24 September 2013, **revised 23 May 2024** (current). Both PDPC, s 49(1), non-binding.

**Pinpoint positions (health-data-relevant):**

- **Key Concepts paras 12.80–12.83** — the authoritative gloss on the research exceptions: use conditions restated (12.80); disclosure adds impracticability (12.81); **12.82** — "impracticable" factors: no current contact information; disproportionate resource/time demands relative to grant and timeline (no fixed subject-number threshold); consent would skew or bias the research; **12.83** — "mere inconvenience … would not amount to 'impracticability'".
- **Key Concepts para 12.69(b)** — sensitivity of the data raises the likely adverse effect in deemed-consent-by-notification and legitimate-interests assessments — the general-regime hook for treating health data as higher-risk.
- **Key Concepts ch 20** — breach notification walked through, including the deemed-harm classes (20.15: full name/alias/ID number plus, inter alia, "specified medical information" — STI/HIV, schizophrenia or delusional disorder, substance abuse; a worked example makes a ~50-patient STI-records breach notifiable to Commission and patients) and the 500-individual scale limb (20.19–20.21).
- **Key Concepts (p 73 worked example)** — a healthcare group may **not** rely on the business-improvement exception to share medical records with an affiliated insurer for claims automation: aggregated data would do, and "the sharing of medical information for this purpose is unlikely to be considered appropriate to a reasonable person".
- **Selected Topics ch 2 (Analytics and Research)** — anonymise first (2.1); business improvement vs the "upstream" research exception (2.3–2.8); **2.9** — retention for research is a valid business reason only with "an immediate and demonstrable intent" to analyse — no indefinite "future research" retention of identifiable data.
- **Selected Topics ch 3 (Anonymisation)** — the PDPC's legal test: data is not anonymised "if there is a serious possibility that an individual could be re-identified", judged on data-plus-context and safeguards (3.4); de-identification (mere removal of direct identifiers) is expressly **not** anonymisation (3.2); health information is a sensitive "target attribute" (3.3(c)); quantified thresholds — **k=5 with safeguards for external sharing, k=3 for internal use** (3.9); the "motivated intruder" test (3.36–3.38); HIV records as the paradigm case for restraint even below the threshold (3.28); release to "a closed community of researchers" as a risk control (3.29); recipients of properly anonymised data are outside the Act but should be contractually barred from re-identification, which is in any event a Part 9B offence (3.18, 3.40); check the destination jurisdiction's definition before exporting "anonymised" datasets (3.44).

**Executive summary.** These two horizontal guidelines carry the doctrinal weight for health-data research and anonymisation. Key Concepts supplies the only official elaboration of the research exceptions' impracticability condition — a demanding, documented standard under which inconvenience never suffices — and the worked examples draw a bright line against sharing medical records with insurers under the business-improvement head. Selected Topics chapter 3 is, in substance, Singapore's anonymisation law: a contextual "serious possibility of re-identification" test with regulator-quantified k-anonymity thresholds, an explicit sensitivity uplift for health information, and endorsement of controlled research enclaves — the conceptual foundation on which TRUST's "anonymised to Whole-of-Government standards" model rests. Both are advisory; both are current (April 2026 and May 2024 editions).

## 16. PDPC anonymisation and privacy-enhancing-technology guides

**Citations/status:** *Guide to Basic Anonymisation* (first published 31 March 2022 — pdpc.gov.sg's migrated pages now display later stamps; the original announcement URL fixes the release date — file updated 24 July 2024) — a practical guide, expressly **not** implying PDPA compliance, scoped to structured tabular textual data only (non-textual data — audio, video, images, biometric data — is expressly out of scope, as are complex or longitudinal datasets, which the guide refers to expert treatment); five-step workflow with technique annexes and k-anonymity computation. *Guide on Synthetic Data Generation* — released 15 July 2024 as a "proposed guide" (the 24 September 2024 date on the current PDPC listing is the migrated page's publish stamp, matching the served file's September 2024 build), developed with **A*STAR**, supported by IMDA and offered as a resource within IMDA's PET Sandbox — the regulator's position on when synthetic derivatives escape the PDPA, with anti-re-identification good practices. *Guide on Federated Learning* — published **20 July 2026**, directly relevant to multi-institution health-data collaboration without pooling records. There is **no** PDPC document titled "Guide to Advanced Anonymisation" — the advanced layer is Selected Topics ch 3 plus the PET guides.

**Executive summary.** These operational guides make the anonymisation-first strategy usable: the Basic Anonymisation guide gives health-data holders the workflow and techniques (while warning that complex, longitudinal or non-tabular data — most interesting health data — needs expert treatment beyond its scope), and the newer PET guides chart the sanctioned escape routes from consent friction: synthetic cohorts and federated analysis. None is binding, and none substitutes for the "serious possibility" legal test in the Selected Topics guidelines.

## 17. BAC — Ethics Guidelines for Human Biomedical Research (2021)

**Citation/status:** *Ethics Guidelines for Human Biomedical Research*, Bioethics Advisory Committee, **2021 revised edition** (published 27 October 2021; first edition 2015). Advisory and without statutory force: the BAC states its reports "have all been accepted by the MOH as providing guidance on matters not covered by statute, subsidiary legislation, or otherwise" (para 1.19), and its FAQ confirms it "does not possess any legislative or regulatory powers". (The often-quoted line that the BAC "is a policy advisory body, not an executive body; hence it has no supervisory or regulatory power" appears only in the 2012 consultation paper reproduced as Annexe A, not in the operative 2021 text.) Practical force runs through MOH's Governance Framework for Human Biomedical Research and IRB adoption; HBRA s 41(3), by its terms, applies only to codes issued or approved by the Director-General under s 40 — a status the BAC Guidelines do not claim.

**Pinpoint positions:** para 4.2 — three-tier taxonomy (identified / reversibly de-identified / irreversibly de-identified; the first two are "identifiable"); para 4.17 — IRB review (or exemption from review) for all personal-information research, with IRB discretion between specific and general consent; para 4.18 — de-identify as early as possible, proportionality; para 4.21 — five-limb waiver test for epidemiological/public-health research and the use of medical records for research; **para 4.22** — research-derived health information should not be disclosed to third parties "including employers or insurance companies" (the ethical antecedent of the insurance Moratorium, item 21); paras 3.38–3.42 — **duty to return clinically significant incidental findings**, actionable or not, to participants who asked to know; **para 3.48 and 5.14** — endorsement of **general (broad) consent** for storage and future use without re-consent, subject to donor-imposed limits and IRB oversight; para 5.15 — specific consent for "sensitive" research; para 5.6 — the "gift" model extended to research data, enabling sharing and re-analysis subject to safeguards; para 5.5 — international collaborations require agreed common ethical standards; **para 6.15** — whole-genome research participants must be told of "the difficulty in guaranteeing their anonymity with complete certainty" and of practical limits on withdrawal; para 6.17 — clinical germline modification should not be permitted "at this stage", pending further evidence of efficacy and safety.

**Executive summary.** The 2021 Guidelines are the national ethics baseline that the HBRA's open-textured provisions presuppose, and their data positions are more developed than the statute's: a de-identification taxonomy finer than the HBRA's binary, an explicit endorsement of broad consent under IRB supervision, a hardened duty to return significant incidental findings, a purpose-limitation norm keeping research data away from employers and insurers, and — for genomics — a candid concession that whole-genome anonymity cannot be guaranteed, converting privacy protection into a matter of governance and security rather than data-state. Not law — and not shown to be a s 40 HBRA code attracting s 41(3) — but adopted in practice by MOH's governance framework and the IRB system as the national research-ethics baseline.

## 18. BAC — Ethical Use of Big Data and Artificial Intelligence in Biomedical Research (2025)

**Citation/status:** *Ethical Use of Big Data and Artificial Intelligence in Biomedical Research*, BAC, published **21 February 2025** (after a 2023 public consultation). Advisory. **Citation caution:** recommendations are cited by paragraph (12.1–12.46; chapter 12 runs to 12.50, with 12.47–12.50 being the conclusion), not as numbered "Recommendations".

**Pinpoint positions:** 12.2 — solidarity: data as a collective resource; 12.12 — high-quality, reproducible data "should also be shared for subsequent scientifically valid proposals"; 12.16 — federated and swarm learning recommended; 12.18 — consent communications must include re-identification risks; **12.21** — international collaborations should "only share data with countries that have similar ethical guidelines"; 12.22 — institutional data-governance frameworks for sharing, especially public-private, with audit mechanisms; 12.23 — institutions as **custodians, not owners**; 12.24 — custodians must comply with (then-Bill) health-information legislation; 12.28 — early anonymisation, data minimisation, lineage tracking, periodic anonymity reassessment; 12.33 — public incidental-findings policies; robust IRB review before returning late findings not foreseen at consent; **12.37** — IRBs to evaluate the suitability of the consent model ("specific, broad, dynamic"); 12.38 — codes of practice for research data sharing with sanctions (including funding withdrawal) for deliberate re-identification, building on the **Five Safes**.

**Executive summary.** The 2025 report is the BAC's data-sharing manifesto: it reframes biomedical data as a solidarity resource whose *sharing* is an ethical good, then disciplines that stance with governance — custodianship not ownership, documented frameworks and audits for public-private flows, privacy-preserving computation, lineage tracking, and a striking ethical data-localisation norm limiting international sharing to jurisdictions with similar ethical standards. It is also the first BAC instrument to name dynamic consent (para 12.37 lists it among the consent models IRBs must evaluate) and to anticipate the health-information legislation. Its Five-Safes-based recommendation for research data-sharing codes of practice reads as an ethical blueprint for what TRUST already operationalises.

## 19. SMC Ethical Code and Ethical Guidelines (2016)

**Citation/status:** *SMC Ethical Code and Ethical Guidelines*, 2016 edition — published 13 September 2016, in force 1 January 2017; **still the current edition as at 2026** (a revision announced in December 2020 has not issued). Professional code of the Singapore Medical Council; not legislation — "if there is a conflict between the ECEG and the law, the law takes precedence" (Preamble 1(7)) — but serious disregard grounds disciplinary proceedings under the Medical Registration Act (Preamble 1(6)).

**Pinpoint positions — Guideline C7 (Medical confidentiality)** *(note: C7, not C6 — C6 is consent)*: maintain confidentiality unless the patient consents or an exception applies; take reasonable care over record-system security and comply with security protocols; **do not access patient information if not involved in the patient's care** (the anti-snooping rule); no careless or social-media dissemination; unconsented disclosure defensible when mandated by law, necessary to protect patients or others from harm, beneficial to minors via guardians, or in the patient's best interests; **in research, abide by IRB-imposed confidentiality requirements** (making IRB data conditions professionally enforceable); care-team sharing permitted to facilitate care; confidentiality persists after death, except for next-of-kin/executor requests, "legitimate clinical audit, education or research", or official inquiries.

**Executive summary.** The ECEG supplies the professional-conduct layer beneath the statutes: nearly all Singapore health data originates in a clinical encounter governed by C7, whose disclosure gate is narrower than HBRA s 29's exception list or the IDA's gateways — meaning a doctor can face disciplinary exposure for a disclosure the statutes permit, unless a statutory override (IDA s 10(4), MLSA s 29(2), PDPA s 26D(8)) expressly covers it. For data governance its two working parts are the anti-snooping rule, which polices clinician-researchers' access to records they do not clinically hold, and the research bullet, which turns IRB confidentiality conditions into professional obligations.

## 20. MOH Code of Practice — clinical genetic and genomic testing

**Citation/status:** *Standards for the Provision of Clinical Genetic/Genomic Testing Services (CGTS) and Clinical Laboratory Genetic/Genomic Testing Services (LGTS)* — Code of Practice issued **1 July 2018** under the PHMCA; updated by **MOH Circular No. 234/2020 (16 December 2020)** in consultation with the Genetic Testing Advisory Committee. **Transitional status:** its enabling statute was repealed on 18 December 2023; **MOH Circular No. 11/2025 (14 March 2025)** announced its translation into HCSA licence conditions and guidelines (Level 3 CGTS to become an MOH-approved "Specified Service" under the Outpatient Medical Service), with detailed requirements promised for 2H 2025 and a sunrise period. **No CGTS licence conditions or guidelines have issued as at August 2026** — the HCSA Outpatient Medical Service and Clinical Laboratory Service circular lists (checked to July 2026) contain none, and MOH's emerging-regulatory-policy page still cites Circular 11/2025 as the current position: the promised update has slipped by roughly a year.

**Pinpoint positions:** Part B, para 3.1(d) — general principle: protection of privacy and confidentiality of personal data; para 21.3 — documented consent for Level 3 tests must cover confidentiality extent (i), further use of genetic information **including after death** (o), sample use and disposal (p), retention period and storage location of unused samples (q, in the "if applicable" block), and **(r) a separate HBRA-compliant consent form** where samples are stored for current or future research — the express clinical-to-research hand-off (para 21.1 governs *who* gives consent); para 22 — disclosure of Level 3 results to the patient or authorised substitute decision-makers; **para 37.3** — genetic test results and genetic information must not be disclosed "to any third parties **(including family members)**" without prior consent of the patient or the appropriate substitute decision-maker — a stricter default than the ECEG's care-team allowance, blocking unconsented cascade disclosure to relatives; para 37.1 — administrative, technical and physical safeguards; para 36 — outsourcing to foreign laboratories is gated on **accreditation** (Annex F) plus five further technical and reporting conditions in para 36.2 — none of them a data-protection condition on genetic data leaving Singapore; para 41.1 — prohibition on direct-to-consumer provision of clinical genetic tests by manufacturers/suppliers. Para 1.4 requires compliance with other applicable laws, expressly naming the PDPA alongside the PHMC Act, HPA, MCA and HBRA — though the operative confidentiality provisions (paras 22, 37) are drafted without reference to it.

**Executive summary.** This Code is the principal instrument regulating clinical genomic data at the point of generation (operating alongside the PDPA, HBRA and ECEG, to which its para 1.4 defers), and its heart is para 37.3's family-inclusive consent gate: in Singapore clinical practice, a relative has no access to a patient's genetic result without the patient's (or substitute's) consent — the cascade-testing question is resolved for confidentiality, and any duty-to-warn argument must be built from the NMEC's older guidance (item 26) and general law. Its research hand-off is equally clean: clinical consent never carries a sample into a biobank — a separate HBRA consent is required. Its weaknesses are its transitional legal footing since the PHMCA repeal and its accreditation-and-quality approach to cross-border outsourcing, which leaves genomic data exports without a data-protection condition.

## 21. Moratorium on Genetic Testing and Insurance (MOH–LIA)

**Citation/status:** *Amended and Restated Moratorium on Genetic Testing and Insurance between MOH and the Life Insurance Association* (the "2025 Moratorium"), effective **30 June 2025**; original entered into **27 January 2021** and operative for policies signed from **27 October 2021** (the 2021 Moratorium continues to govern policies signed from 27 October 2021 to 29 June 2025); amended after a 2023–2024 mid-term review, principally to protect the national Familial Hypercholesterolaemia programme launched 30 June 2025 (MOH Circular 53/2025 of 27 August 2025, superseding 33/2025, and Circular 57/2025 of 9 September 2025, superseding 36/2025). **A bilateral policy agreement, not legislation** — enforced as a condition of LIA membership with annual compliance reporting to LIA and MOH (para 10); open-ended, lapsing only if replaced by legislation (para 11); reviewed every three years (para 12). **Statutory replacement is now in motion:** MOH opened a public consultation on a **Genetic Information Protection Bill** on 7 August 2026 (closing 4 September 2026), with the Bill expected to be tabled in Parliament in 2027 and to bar both **insurers and employers** from requiring genetic testing or requesting and using genetic information.

**Pinpoint positions:** para 2 — insurers must not require or pressure anyone to take a genetic test, nor request residents to take predictive tests; **para 3** — results of genetic tests conducted "in the context of Biomedical Research" may **never** be used for underwriting, "regardless of where or when the research was conducted or the nature of the research", even where the para 4(b) exception would otherwise apply — the research shield underpinning Precision Medicine recruitment; para 4(a)–(b) + Table 1 — predictive results usable only where three cumulative conditions are met: an approved insurance type, aggregate cover above the Approved Financial Limit — defined as the **higher of** the stated floor or the 99th-percentile sum assured for that product class at underwriting (floors: S$2m for life/TPD, Huntington's only; S$500,000 critical illness and S$10,000/month disability income, HTT/BRCA1/BRCA2) — and an approved test; para 5 — never for health insurance or Integrated Shield Plans, never a **relative's** test result, and never a test taken after cover started unless consented to before cover began (para 5(ii)); para 6 — a disclosed result may be considered only if favourable, "notwithstanding any other provision"; para 7 — ban on using direct-to-consumer test results (absolute as against the para 4(b) exception, though para 6's favourable-result allowance appears to survive); para 8 — protection for FH-programme tests (LDLR, APOB, PCSK9), conditional on enrolment with an MOH-approved facility and the programme/legislation timing limb.

**Executive summary.** The Moratorium is Singapore's answer to genetic discrimination in insurance, done by agreement rather than statute: a research-result shield with global and perpetual reach (para 3), a near-total bar on predictive-test underwriting softened only by a narrow high-value "double key" exception, and a DTC ban that holds against that exception. Its enforcement is private (LIA membership condition, FIDReC/mediation for consumers), its versions bifurcate by policy date, and its own text contemplates statutory replacement — now concretely in prospect: MOH's Genetic Information Protection Bill consultation (August–September 2026, Bill expected 2027) would supersede it for insurers and extend the prohibition to employers, alongside the HIA's insurance "excluded purpose" and NEHR access bans. For genomic data sharing, para 3 is the operative promise made to every research participant: what the cohort learns, the underwriter never uses.

## 23. AI in healthcare — AIHGle 2.0 and the model frameworks

**Citation/status:** *Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare Guidelines (AIHGle), Version 2.0*, **MOH and HSA, published March 2026** (issued under MOH circular MOH-MHC-CIR-0024-2026 of 13 March 2026; co-development acknowledged with Synapxe, IMDA, PDPC and professional bodies; the October 2021 first edition, attributed by secondary sources to MOH/HSA/IHiS, is nowhere referenced in the 2.0 text itself). Advisory: it "complements prevailing legislation" (cl 2.1.2). Horizontal layer: PDPC/IMDA **Model AI Governance Framework** (2nd ed, 21 January 2020); **Model AI Governance Framework for Generative AI** (AI Verify Foundation/IMDA, 30 May 2024 — nine dimensions including data); **Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI** (IMDA, 22 January 2026, updated May 2026); PDPC **Advisory Guidelines on Use of Personal Data in AI Recommendation and Decision Systems** (issued 1 March 2024 — mapping AI development, testing and monitoring to the **business-improvement** and **research** exceptions, with consent and legitimate-interests analysis reserved for the deployment phase).

**Pinpoint positions (AIHGle 2.0):** cl 2.2.2(a) — continuous-learning models risk "personal data disclosure after input data is ingested"; developers directed to personal-data-law compliance and **privacy-enhancing technologies** (ch 5.3.3); ch 6.7 + fnn 24–26 — incident routing: data breaches to PDPC, device adverse events to HSA, CII incidents to CSA under the Cybersecurity Act and CII Regulations (others to SingCERT); Table 6 — expressly an "Examples of Key Legislation and Guidelines" list (illustrative, "as at the date of publication"), naming the PDPA, HCSA, HBRA, device regulations, the CS/DS Essentials (2026), both BAC instruments and the PDPC AI guidelines — but **not** the HIA (uncommenced at publication), and, of the model AI frameworks, only the 2nd-edition Model AI Governance Framework.

**Executive summary.** Singapore governs healthcare AI by layered voluntary guidance over hard sectoral law, with no AI statute. AIHGle 2.0 is the sector instrument: non-binding but backed by HSA device registration and HCSA licensing, it treats data protection as a design constraint (PETs, anonymisation, ingestion risk) and routes incidents to three regulators. For data-sharing purposes its significance is mostly indexical — Table 6, though expressly illustrative, is the nearest official cross-reference to the instruments in this roundup — while the PDPC's 2024 AI guidelines answer the practical question of which PDPA bases permit training clinical models on patient data (business improvement and research).

## 24. IMDA/PDPC Trusted Data Sharing Framework

**Citation/status:** Launched **28 June 2019**; the current document is the 2019 framework (91 pages; file build October 2020). Voluntary and expressly "not … an authoritative statement of the law". Claims of a "2024 edition" are unsupported — only the IMDA webpage carries a 2024 last-updated stamp. Accompanied by template **bilateral, multilateral and multilateral-with-lead data sharing agreements**, confidentiality-agreement and consent templates, and a data-valuation guide.

**Pinpoint structure:** Part 2 — legal/regulatory considerations (2.1: can the data be shared; 2.2: constructing a data sharing agreement, with the DSA term list in s 2.2 itself, sample legal templates signposted in Appendix V, and the overall data-sharing checklist at Appendix X); Appendix VI — dynamic and iterative consent; Appendix VII — PDPA exemptions (pre-2021 law — now dated); Part 4.3 — secondary use; Part 4.4 — retention and disposal.

**Executive summary.** The TDSF is a playbook, not law: its enduring value for health data lies in its template data sharing agreements — the closest Singapore has to model clauses, directly reusable for hospital-industry or consortium arrangements — and its dynamic-consent appendix, which anticipates longitudinal research needs. Its PDPA analysis predates the 2021 amendments (deemed consent by notification, legitimate interests, breach notification, the restructured research exceptions), so its legal content must be checked against the current Key Concepts guidelines before reliance.

## 25. Government-internal instruments: IM8, Government Data Security Policies, HealthTech Instruction Manual, PSDSRC

**Citation/status:** The Government **Instruction Manual on ICT&SS Management ("IM8")** (since 2001) is the Government's internal data/ICT rulebook — administrative, unpublished, and binding on agencies (arguably operating as a PSGA s 4 direction enforced via s 10, though no public source states this expressly and the PSDSRC report treats IM8 and the PSGA as distinct instruments). Its public window is the **Government Data Security Policies** extract (expressly non-definitive; confirms that the **PSDSRC's 2019 measures have been incorporated into IM8**; provides that agencies *should* perform dataset-level data-security risk assessments; illustrates salted hashing for analytics-versus-operational systems). The **HealthTech Instruction Manual (HIM)**, MOH, is the public-healthcare analogue binding MOH Holdings group entities — covering security baselines, data classification, **inter-entity data sharing protocols** and PDPA obligations — likewise unpublished. The **Public Sector Data Security Review Committee Report** (dated November 2019; submitted to the Prime Minister 26 November 2019 and released 27 November 2019; convened 31 March 2019 after the SingHealth breach; 336 systems across 94 agencies inspected) made five recommendations (24 initiatives, all implemented by 31 March 2024 per MDDI's July 2024 update); its healthcare paragraphs (24–26) prescribed full adoption of the measures for health policy, research, analytics **and administrative** data, contextualised adoption for patient-care systems, announced the HealthTech Instruction Manual, and committed MOH to sector guidelines and "legislation and licensing requirements" — the documented origin of the HIA.

**Executive summary.** A material layer of the rules governing government-held health data is simply not public: IM8 for agencies and the HealthTech Instruction Manual for the public healthcare clusters are binding but unpublished, visible only through summary extracts. What is public is the lineage: the PSDSRC report is the constitutional document of Singapore's post-SingHealth data-security settlement, and its paragraph 26 is the traceable origin of everything in item 22 — including the Health Information Act itself.

## 26. Historical and adjacent ethics/practice guidance

- **National Medical Ethics Committee (NMEC)** — non-statutory MOH advisory committee (est. 1994; "no statutory powers"). Its *Ethical Guidelines on Research Involving Human Subjects* are largely superseded by the HBRA and BAC instruments but remain notable for para 2.6: confidentiality cannot be breached without consent (2.6.1); code identifiable data at the earliest possible time on a need-to-know basis (2.6.2); **access to relatives must in principle be controlled by the subjects themselves** (2.6.3); and — uniquely in Singapore guidance — the contemplation of "a legal duty for the investigator to warn family members of genetically-based risks of severe harm" (2.6.4), a proposition no later instrument squarely endorses — though the genetic-testing Code's para 37.3 confidentiality default (item 20) cuts against it in the clinical setting.
- **National Telemedicine Guidelines (January 2015)**, MOH — the operative telemedicine guidance (often misattributed to the NMEC; MOH consulted in 2024 on draft National Telehealth Guidelines to update them, with no successor confirmed issued as at August 2026), now supplemented by the **Joint MOH-HSA-SMC Circular No. 87/2024 (22 November 2024)** on telemedicine standards (clinical governance and advertising — notably *not* a records instrument; record-keeping stays with the General and OMS Regulations) and reg 39A on medical certificates (item 3).
- **PDPC enforcement practice** — the leading health-data decisions: *Re Singapore Health Services Pte Ltd* [2019] SGPDPC 3 (SingHealth breach; largest penalties to date) and ***Institute of Mental Health* [2025] SGPDPC 1 (21 May 2025, an amended decision superseding one of 15 November 2024)** — no breach of the consent obligation where an attending doctor identified suitable study participants from clinical knowledge and passed their names and appointment times to a research officer, who located and approached one of them in the waiting area (implied consent from a long-standing, prominent clinic notification); the Commission observed *obiter* (having found implied consent) that deemed consent by notification would have been unavailable because the notification did not state the manner or period for opting out; best practice is express consent before using medical information for research recruitment. This is the operative authority on research-recruitment screening — exactly the ground the HIA's derived-information regime will later occupy.

# Part III — Bodies, platforms and access governance (the "Entrust" question answered)

## 27. TRUST — Trusted Research and Real world-data Utilisation and Sharing Tech

**What it is:** Singapore's national trusted research environment ("data platform + programme office + governance framework") for health and health-related data; operationalised **November 2022**; co-developed by MOH, MDDI (the renamed Ministry of Communications and Information, into which the Smart Nation and Digital Government Office was merged from October 2023), GovTech and Synapxe (formerly IHiS). The acronym's expansion appears in official npm.sg/MOH materials; TRUST's own site uses the short name. **Since 1 April 2026** the office has moved from MOH's Office for Healthcare Transformation to the **National Research Foundation** (hosted by A*STAR) as the **"RIE TRUST Office"**, with a mandate extended beyond health to urban solutions, sustainability and the built environment — existing data-request and DAC processes expressly preserved. Site: trustplatform.sg.

**Legal basis:** **None specific — and TRUST claims none.** Users are bound by general law — the platform's FAQ names the **HBRA, Computer Misuse Act, PDPA and Official Secrets Act** — and by contract: institution-level **Data Request Agreements**, contributor-side **Data Contributor Pre-agreements**, and per-analyst **Personal Undertakings**. There is no published, versioned "TRUST Governance Framework" document; the governance is stated on the platform's web pages.

**Governance bodies:**
- **RIE Data System Steering Committee (RIE DSSC)** — strategic oversight; chaired by the Permanent Secretary (National Research & Development), Prof Tan Chorh Chuan.
- **TRUST Data Access Committee (DAC)** — reviews every data request for ethics and public benefit and validates conformity with contributor agreements; chaired by Mr Philip Ong, Deputy Secretary (Development), Ministry of Health; members span healthcare, science, technology, law and ethics, with senior representatives of NUHS, NHG, NUS, Duke-NUS, A*STAR, NTU, the Department of Statistics and the National Cancer Centre Singapore.
- **Research and Industry Data Exchange Subcommittee (RIDES), under MOH** — industry access: an industry partner may access TRUST data only in collaboration with an academic/research/public institution **and** with separate RIDES approval. (The platform's governance page names only the RIE DSSC and the DAC as TRUST's governing committees; RIDES is documented on the collaboration pages as the industry-access gate.)

**Safeguards and process (as stated by TRUST):** the **Five Safes** framework (safe purpose/people/settings/data/output); anonymisation "to Whole-of-Government anonymisation standards", with NRIC numbers and names excluded and identifiers pseudonymised *(TRUST's own pages use "anonymised" and "pseudonymised" in adjacent sentences — do not over-claim full anonymisation, and PRECISE describes SG100K data on TRUST as pseudonymised)*; sensitive datasets accessible only in **MOH-certified Micro-Access Laboratories**; **IRB approval (or an institution-endorsed "Review Not Required" determination, accepted since 26 January 2024) is a precondition** to any data request; no download of data — analysis in-platform, with only aggregate analytical output extractable after vetting by the **TRUST Data Concierge**, and pre-publication review (21 working days); a **Charging Framework** since 1 January 2025; data is not stored perpetually and is deleted upon completion of approved analyses (MOH, 10 October 2024). Requests are consolidated monthly; DAC review takes ~4–6 weeks, provisioning 6–10 weeks post-approval. Transparency: a published register of approved projects (~92 at review). Public involvement: partnership with OPEN Voices, NTU LKCMedicine's patient-partner collective (launched November 2024; inaugural joint engagement session with TRUST in August 2025), and a long-running public-attitudes study with PRECISE and NUS CBmE.

**Executive summary.** TRUST is the answer to "who governs access to Singapore's national health and research data": a controlled-access research environment whose authority is contractual and institutional rather than statutory. Its DAC (MOH-chaired) is the operative gatekeeper for research access to national health data — including, since 2024–2026, the PRECISE-SG100K genomic resource — with RIDES as a second key for industry. Its design tracks the international five-safes orthodoxy and the PDPC's closed-research-enclave guidance, and its 2026 migration to the National Research Foundation as the RIE TRUST Office signals its generalisation beyond health. The important legal observation is negative: nothing in statute compels contributions to TRUST, governs its DAC, or shields its data specifically — the HIA regulates the NEHR, not TRUST — so its accountability rests on the general statutes (HBRA, PDPA, Computer Misuse Act, Official Secrets Act) plus contract.

## 28. enTRUST — GovTech's cross-sector platform

**What it is:** **enTRUST** — "enabling Trusted Research and real-world data Utilisation and Sharing Tech" (entrust.gov.sg) — GovTech's central infrastructure and governance solution for **Public Private Data Sharing (PPDS)** projects, the cross-sector generalisation of MOH's TRUST build ("Before enTRUST, there was the Ministry of Health's (MOH) TRUST — a PPDS project platform in the healthcare sector" — GovTech). The site attributes the platform to GovTech's Data Programme; GovTech's engineering channels credit its Data Science and AI Division. Two components: a metadata catalogue and a secure data-sharing environment (IM-compliant data lab with exfiltration controls). It adopts the Five Safes, holds no directly identifying records, and reports 27+ partner agencies (including SingHealth, NUHS, NHG) and >135TB processed since 2024. **It is now TRUST's members' portal** (TRUST onboarding links to entrust.gov.sg/signIn).

**Executive summary.** This is the entity behind the user-remembered name "Entrust" — real, but infrastructure rather than authority: enTRUST is a platform and service, with no constituting instrument, no published governance charter, and no data-access committee of its own; access decisions remain with data contributors and, for health data, with TRUST's DAC and RIDES. Describe it as the pipes; TRUST is the gate.

## 29. PRECISE and the National Precision Medicine programme (genomic data)

**What it is:** **PRECISE** (Precision Health Research, Singapore), a programme of the Consortium for Clinical Research and Innovation, Singapore (CRIS), is the central coordinator of the **National Precision Medicine (NPM)** programme: Phase I (2017–2021) produced **SG10K_Health** (~10,000 whole genomes); Phase II (2022–2025, NRF-funded) produced **PRECISE-SG100K** (~100,000 participants, 30X whole-genome sequencing at A*STAR's Genome Institute, drawn from the HELIOS, SPHS, SERI-SEED and SingHeart cohorts); **Phase III "SG-INSIGHT" launched 14 November 2025** (MOH/NMRC-funded, MOUs with all three healthcare clusters, target ~450,000 people by 2031, Genomic Innovation Hubs, pharmacogenomics-first clinical rollout).

**Genomic data access governance:**
- **SG10K_Health** — governed by the **NPM Data Access Committee (NPM DAC)** at A*STAR's Genome Institute: qualified investigators at research-intensive organisations; a non-negotiable **Data Access Agreement**; analysis on the **RAPTOR** secure cloud platform without download (all verified from live GIS/npm.sg pages; a reported mandatory onboarding training requirement remains unverified).
- **PRECISE-SG100K** — a staged committee stack: (1) PRECISE Management Team screening (with a parallel Business Development lane for industry proposals); (2) the **PRECISE-SG100K Scientific Committee** (strategic/scientific merit); (3) the **PRECISE-SG100K Data Access Committee** (which approves requests not requiring EHR linkage); and (4) the **TRUST DAC**, whose additional approval is required for access involving linkage to electronic health records (and, under the 2026 call, for projects requiring individual-level data). The resource is resident on TRUST in pseudonymised form. Access is phased: a closed 2024 call selected 37 projects (9 Flagship, 28 Driver); the **2026 Call for Proposals** (2 January–14 February 2026) was limited to lead PIs at local publicly funded institutions with **no industry partnerships permitted**, alignment with participant consent required, and TRUST DAC applications from May 2026; **broad access via TRUST is anticipated from 2027**. PRECISE publishes its access governance as web pages (including a "Data Access Principles (PRECISE SG100K)" page) rather than as a titled, versioned policy instrument.
- Programme oversight: **PRECISE Board Oversight Committee** (chair Mr Peter Ho) — not a data-access body; a lay **Patient and Public Involvement Advisory Panel**; an online listing of approved SG100K projects (whose contents could not be independently rendered for verification).
- Legal frame: participants' genomic data sits under the HBRA (consent, ss 27–29 offences), the BAC guidelines (broad consent, whole-genome candour), the genetic-testing COP for any clinical hand-back, and the Moratorium's para 3 shield against insurance use.

**Executive summary.** Singapore's population genomics is governed by committee and contract, not by genomics legislation: SG10K access runs through an A*STAR data access committee into a no-download secure platform, and SG100K access runs through a staged PRECISE committee stack that terminates, for anything requiring EHR linkage (and, under the 2026 call, individual-level data), in the same TRUST DAC that governs national health data — making TRUST the effective convergence point of Singapore's health and genomic data governance from 2027. The regime is conservative (public-sector researchers only for now; industry case-by-case and excluded from the 2026 call) and transparent about projects but not about instruments: there is no published genomic data-access policy to cite, which is itself a governance finding.

## 30. NEHR, Synapxe and Healthier SG

**NEHR** — the National Electronic Health Record: owned by MOH, operated by **Synapxe** (the national HealthTech agency, renamed from IHiS on 27 July 2023). The HIA provides for ministerial designation of a **System Operator** (s 8) — in practice expected to be Synapxe — with authorised officers empowered to direct the System Operator to act against contravening contributors and users (s 34); healthinfo.gov.sg states Synapxe can investigate potential unauthorised access. Statutory basis: the HIA (item 1) once commenced; until then contribution remains contractual/voluntary (existing arrangements will be deemed approved under HIA s 44). Access is care-only, role-based, rate-limited and audited; administrative staff have no access; insurers and employers "do not and will not" have access (MOH, Parliamentary answer, 7 January 2025). MOH's *Guidelines on Appropriate Contribution, Use and Access to the NEHR* (13 March 2026, item 22) govern care-use; **there is no published NEHR research-access framework** — research access to national data routes through TRUST, and the HIA's derived-information regime (ss 24–27) will supply the statutory secondary-use channel.
**Healthier SG data sharing** — enrolment data flows among clusters, enrolled GPs, MOH agencies (HPB, AIC) and community partners; presently administrative, with HIA Part 3 and the Fourth Schedule use cases built to give it statutory footing (currently drawn to public healthcare institutions, cluster HQs, AIC and public agencies).
**Cluster IRBs/DRBs** (NHG DSRB, SingHealth CIRB, NUS/NTU IRBs) — the HBRA-mandated ethics gate that every TRUST/PRECISE request must clear first.
**HDR UK–NRF health data partnership** (MOU, October 2024; reported on TRUST's news pages): federated analysis across borders (data stays local, only aggregated results are returned); Singapore implementing partners are MOHT, GovTech and A*STAR, with TRUST as the Singapore trusted research environment on which the federated tooling is deployed; disease scope covers cancer, diabetes and rare diseases, with the exemplar analyses on cancer and diabetes.

# Part IV — Cross-cutting observations

1. **Two regimes, one flow.** A single national health-data pathway (restructured hospital → vendor → MOH → researcher) can cross the PDPA/PSGA boundary repeatedly: clusters and hospitals are PDPA "organisations"; MOH, HPB, HSA and CDA are PDPA-exempt public agencies governed by the PSGA and unpublished instruction manuals; the narrow bridges are PDPA s 26E (vendor breach notification to agencies) and the Second Schedule public-agency exceptions.
2. **Consent is being displaced by authorisation-plus-minimisation.** The HIA (ss 11, 50), IDA (s 10(4)) and MLSA (s 29(3)) override consent and confidentiality within purpose-limited channels; the NRDA is the counter-model (consent plus statutory necessity tests). The PDPC's guidance meanwhile narrows consent-free routes in the private sector (impracticability strictly construed; no medical-records-to-insurer sharing under business improvement).
3. **Anonymisation is the load-bearing wall** — and it is guidance, not statute: the "serious possibility" test, k-anonymity thresholds and motivated-intruder analysis live in PDPC advisory guidelines, while statutes increasingly criminalise the breach of the wall (IDA s 59A(7)(b); PDPA s 48F; PSGA s 8; HIA ss 41–42) rather than define it. The BAC and TRUST work on the candid premise that genomic anonymity cannot be guaranteed, substituting governance (Five Safes, enclaves, output vetting) for data-state.
4. **Research access is gate-dependent, not uniform.** Private-sector records: PDPA research exceptions. Identifiable research on people or their data/tissue: HBRA + IRB (waiver via the Fifth Schedule). Registry data: NRDA s 12 (consent + necessity). National platform data: TRUST DAC (+ RIDES for industry; + PRECISE stack for genomics). Clinical trials: HSA regimes. NEHR data: no research route today; HIA derived-information regime tomorrow.
5. **Watch list (as at 29 August 2026):** the **Genetic Information Protection Bill** (MOH public consultation open 7 August–4 September 2026; Bill expected in Parliament 2027, covering insurers and employers, and the designed statutory replacement for the Moratorium); HIA commencement notification and first regulations (expected for early 2027); PSGA Amendment Act 2026 commencement; HCSA licence conditions for genetic testing services (promised 2H 2025 — confirmed still unissued as at August 2026); HCSA s 28's own outstanding commencement; the BAC's overdue Human Nuclear Genome Editing report and its ongoing Genetic and Genomic Testing and Research project; Cybersecurity Act Parts 3C/3D commencement and the cloud CCoP (H2 2026); the PDPC's healthcare guidelines' overdue HIA update; PDPC draft guidelines on personal data in generative AI (consultation closed 14 July 2026); and the 31 December 2026 NRIC-authentication deadline, which bites hard on clinic systems.

# Annex — Verification

**Process.** Wave 1 (research, as at 24 August 2026): six research agents compiled entries from primary sources — Singapore Statutes Online consolidations and gazetted texts, official ministry/regulator sites and PDFs — each producing a verification ledger. Wave 2 (adversarial, completed 29 August 2026): ten adversarial agents re-derived every material proposition against the primary sources — statute text re-fetched or matched byte-for-byte against official copies, paragraph numbers checked against page images where PDF text layers drop them — and attempted to refute each. All confirmed refutations and precision corrections are incorporated in the text above.

**Corrections applied after adversarial review (a record of what the process caught):**
- The gazette reference is **Acts Supplement No. 2 of 2026** (13 February 2026), and HIA Part 5 spans **ss 72–82**; the Act neutralises PDPA consent in **seven** provisions, not three.
- The reported "nuclear medicine batch discrepancy" in the HIA timeline was a PDF-table parsing artifact — both the MOH circular and healthinfo.gov.sg place nuclear medicine services in **Batch 1 (September 2027)**.
- PDPA: s 4(6)(b) subordinates only **Parts 3–6B**; Second Schedule Part 3 Division 1 para 3 turns on whose *patient* the individual is, not who discloses; the NDB Schedule's para 19 covers gamete donation/receipt (not fertility treatment generally) and para 18(c) schizophrenia or delusional disorder (not psychiatric care generally).
- HBRA: administered by the **Director-General of Health** since 1 May 2023 (not the Director of Medical Services); Part 4 is **ss 15–21**; s 39 is broader than s 29 (no functions-or-duties gateway); S 704/2019 mirrors the Second Schedule carve-outs, not a general self-banking exemption.
- Clinical trials: both regulation sets impose a sponsor-side GCP confidentiality duty (First Schedule para 11) — the draft claim that confidentiality obligations "restrain the regulator, not the sponsor" was wrong.
- HCSA: **s 28 has never been commenced**; s 51(2) has a third (consent) gateway; s 57(2)(p) is an express record-safeguards regulation head; "fully repealed" overstated the PHMCA's status (Third Schedule savings persist), and there is no statutory saving for PHMCA-era codes.
- Genetic testing COP: the consent elements are in **para 21.3** (not 21.1), the privacy principle in Part B para 3.1(d), and the COP *does* reference the PDPA (para 1.4); the CGTS licence conditions promised for 2H 2025 were confirmed still unissued.
- Moratorium: the current circulars are **53/2025 and 57/2025** (superseding 33/2025 and 36/2025); the 2021 Moratorium is operative for policies signed from **27 October 2021**; Table 1 figures are floors within a "higher of" formula; the DTC ban is absolute only as against the para 4(b) exception. A live **Genetic Information Protection Bill consultation** (Aug–Sep 2026) was added.
- BAC: the "policy advisory body… no supervisory or regulatory power" line sits only in the 2012 consultation paper reproduced as Annexe A of the 2021 Guidelines; HBRA s 41(3) applies only to s 40 codes, a status the BAC Guidelines do not claim; the 2025 report's recommendations run to para 12.46 (chapter to 12.50).
- PDPC guidance: the hospital-registration worked example is **para 2.18**; the emergency example's notification recommendation runs to the *patient*, not family; the synthetic-data guide's release date is **15 July 2024**; the IMH decision's s 15A analysis is *obiter*, and the clinical screening was done by the attending doctor.
- IDA/MLSA/HOTA/CDA: IDA s 59A carries consent, anonymisation-first, necessity and re-identification-offence safeguards the draft had omitted; Part 6 is ss 46–54; MLSA s 27 requests are immunised but not compellable, and reach only HCSA-licensed institutions and their attending practitioners; HOTA s 28(1) protects the *subject* of a consent and bites only on public identification; S 118/2026 presupposes (rather than removes) the CDA's HCSA licence.
- PSGA/Cybersecurity: the PSGA offences pre-date SingHealth (in force 1 April 2018) and were *expanded* post-breach by Act 40 of 2020; Cybersecurity Act risk assessments are **annual** (audits biennial); the CCoP 2026 binds provider-owned CII owners only; Part 3A's cease-use orders arise under ss 16F(2)–(3) and 16I(2).
- Bodies: "MDDI (formerly SNDGO)" was wrong (MDDI is the renamed MCI); the 2024 SG100K call selected **37** projects (9 Flagship, 28 Driver); the TRUST DAC trigger for SG100K is EHR linkage; OPEN Voices launched November 2024 (TRUST's first joint session August 2025); the "Singapore–UK Health Data Bridge" name is a coinage — the instrument is the HDR UK–NRF MOU.

**Traps for the reader (misconceptions in circulation, corrected in wave 1 and re-confirmed in wave 2):**
- The statute is the **Health Information Act 2026**, not 2025 — and it is wholly uncommenced with no subsidiary legislation.
- The HBRA's **Fourth Schedule** (restricted research) concerns embryos and human-animal combinations, **not** genetic material; the consent-waiver schedule is the **Fifth**; the disclosure offences are **ss 28–29** (not s 27, which is the safeguard duty).
- The SMC ECEG confidentiality guideline is **C7** (C6 is consent).
- IDA **s 25** is HIV/STI-specific; the IDA has no general secrecy provision.
- Cybersecurity Act **s 11 is deleted**; codes of practice now issue under s 35A; "CII" is now "provider-owned CII".
- The "HBR (Exemption) Regulations 2018" are spent (expired 31 October 2019).
- The PDPC "research chapter" is **Selected Topics ch 2 "Analytics and Research"**; its research-exception text covers *use* only — the disclosure limb is in the Healthcare Guidelines and Key Concepts 12.81–12.83.
- The claimed "2024 edition" of the Trusted Data Sharing Framework does not exist as a document; and the widely reported claim that 2026 PDPA regulations tightened breach-notification thresholds is false (S 86/2026 touches only transfer certifications).
