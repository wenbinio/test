# Health Data Sharing in Singapore: Legislative and Normative Instruments — A Verified Roundup

**State of the law verified as at 24 August 2026.**

This roundup catalogues every legislative instrument and the principal normative instruments (codes of practice, advisory guidelines, ethics guidelines, policy agreements and government frameworks) that govern the sharing of health data — clinical, administrative and genomic — in Singapore. Each entry gives the instrument's citation, type and status, its pinpoint provisions relevant to health data sharing, and an executive summary.

**Method.** Six parallel research agents compiled this roundup from primary sources (Singapore Statutes Online consolidations and gazetted texts, MOH, PDPC, CSA, BAC, SMC, trustplatform.sg, npm.sg and entrust.gov.sg), each flagging anything it could not confirm. A second wave of adversarial verification agents then attempted to refute every pinpoint proposition against the primary source. Propositions that could not be independently confirmed are marked **[UNVERIFIED]**; corrections applied after adversarial review are noted in the Annex.

---

## Headline findings

1. **"Entrust" is real — and it is two things.** **TRUST** (Trusted Research and Real world-data Utilisation and Sharing Tech, trustplatform.sg) is Singapore's national trusted research environment for health and health-related data; its **Data Access Committee** is the body that governs research access to national health and genomic data. **enTRUST** ("enabling Trusted Research and real-world data Utilisation and Sharing Tech", entrust.gov.sg) is GovTech's cross-sector generalisation of the TRUST infrastructure and now serves as TRUST's members' portal. Neither has an enabling statute; both are contractual/administrative regimes. See Part III.
2. **The new anchor statute is the Health Information Act 2026 (No. 1 of 2026)** — passed 12 January 2026, assented 3 February 2026, gazetted 12 February 2026 — and it is **wholly uncommenced** as at 24 August 2026, with **no subsidiary legislation** yet made. MOH's stated intention is effect from early 2027, with batched compliance deadlines running from September 2027 to March 2030 (administrative, not statutory).
3. **Singapore has no special statutory category for health data.** The PDPA treats health data as ordinary personal data; heightened treatment arrives through sectoral statutes (HIA, HBRA, NRDA, IDA, MLSA), through PDPC guidance treating health information as a sensitive "target attribute", and through the deemed-significant-harm classes in the data breach notification regime.
4. **The architecture is shifting from consent to statutory authorisation.** HIA ss 11 and 50, IDA s 10(4) and MLSA s 29(3) each displace consent (and in several cases contractual and professional confidentiality) with purpose-limited statutory gateways; the HIA then expressly neutralises PDPA consent as an alternative gateway (HIA ss 16(2), 39(3), 57(2)).
5. **Re-identification is being criminalised layer by layer**: PDPA s 48F, PSGA s 8, and (once commenced) HIA ss 41(2)(c) and 42(2).
6. **Research access to identifiable data runs through four different gates depending on the source**: the PDPA Second Schedule research exceptions (private sector), the HBRA Fifth Schedule IRB waiver (human biomedical research), NRDA s 12 (disease registries, with a statutory anonymisation-first test), and the TRUST/PRECISE data access committees (national platforms, contractual).

# Part I — Primary legislation (and subsidiary legislation under it)

## 1. Health Information Act 2026

**Citation:** Health Information Act 2026 (No. 1 of 2026). Bill No. 20/2025 (first reading 5 November 2025); passed by Parliament 12 January 2026; assented 3 February 2026; gazetted 12 February 2026 (Acts Supplement No. 1 of 2026).
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; Ministry of Health.
**Status:** **Uncommenced as at 24 August 2026** (SSO status line: "Uncommenced"). Section 1 provides the Act "comes into operation on a date that the Minister appoints by notification in the Gazette"; no commencement notification and **no subsidiary legislation** had been gazetted as at 24 August 2026 (confirmed by enumeration of the 2024–2026 Subsidiary Legislation Supplements). MOH intends effect from early 2027.
**Structure:** 9 Parts, 114 sections, 4 Schedules.

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **s 3(6)–(7)** — defines "individually-identifiable health information" (linkage test) and "anonymised health information" (health information that is not individually-identifiable).
- **s 4** — purposes: NEHR-based collection/storage/disclosure; a framework for sharing individually-identifiable information for healthcare, community health services and health programmes; confidentiality, integrity and availability of health information.
- **s 6** — defines **"excluded purpose"**: employment/appointment decisions, engagement of contractors, platform-work agreements, **insurance underwriting, renewal and claims**, and prescribed purposes — access for these is barred.
- **s 7** — defines the national electronic records system (NEHR); **s 8(1)** — the Minister may by Gazette order designate a **System Operator** (in practice Synapxe; no designation order yet gazetted).
- **s 11(1)** — **consent override**: "Despite any other written law or rule of law, or any obligation under contract," individual consent is **not required** for contribution to, or authorised access/collection/disclosure/use of, NEHR information under Part 2. **s 11(2)** — consent is equally not a basis for access.
- **s 12(1)** — **mandatory contribution**: specified contributors (HCSA licensees, retail pharmacy licensees, and persons/agencies listed in the First Schedule) must contribute the health information types listed in the First Schedule (visit events, medications, laboratory results, discharge summaries, vaccinations, etc.).
- **s 16(1)–(2)** — gateway rule: access/collection/disclosure of accessible health information only in accordance with Part 2 or under another written law; **PDPA Part 4 consent does not count** as such other written law.
- **s 17** — patients (and authorised representatives) may access their own record.
- **s 19(1)–(4)** — specified users may access **only** for provision of a healthcare service to the patient (never for an excluded purpose) or for a "specified examination" under a written law listed in the Third Schedule.
- **s 21** — no onward disclosure by users except internally, on a need-to-know, minimum-necessary basis.
- **ss 24–27** — **derived information** regime: "type 1" (individually-identifiable) approvable by the Minister for purposes **relating to or promoting public health** (s 25(2)); "type 2" (aggregated/anonymised) approvable where **in the public interest** (s 25(3)); requestors may then use it **without consent** (s 26); **s 27(5)(c)** — prohibition on any act to identify an individual from type 2 derived information.
- **ss 29–33** — patient **access restrictions**: class 1 (all information) and class 2 (targeted) restrictions (s 29); a prescribed minimum data set remains visible notwithstanding a restriction (s 30(3)); **emergency "break-glass" override** whether or not the individual consents (s 30(4)); pre-commencement NEHR opt-outs are deemed converted into access restrictions (s 33).
- **ss 38–42** — offences: improper access by users (s 38: up to $50,000/2 years, doubled on repeat); access by unauthorised persons (s 39: up to $100,000/4 years, PDPA consent expressly no defence, s 39(3)); unauthorised disclosure (s 40); derived-information offences including **re-identification of type 2 derived information** by requestors (s 41(2)(c)) and by anyone (s 42(2): up to $100,000/4 years).
- **Part 3 (ss 45–60)** — the **non-NEHR sharing channel** for "relevant information" in Fourth Schedule **use cases** (Healthier SG and Age Well SG identification and outreach, between public healthcare institutions, cluster HQs (NHG, NUHS, SingHealth), the Agency for Integrated Care and public agencies): **s 50** — consent and contractual confidentiality overridden; **s 52(2)–(3)** — a written **data sharing agreement** specifying parties, use case, information types and authorised personnel is mandatory; **ss 53–56** — minimum-necessary, genuine-need and use-limitation duties; **s 57** — no onward disclosure (PDPA consent again no gateway, s 57(2)); **s 60** — offences (up to $50,000/2 years; $100,000/4 years on repeat).
- **Part 4 (ss 61–71)** — security: designated compliance individuals (s 65); reasonable data-security controls including data classification (s 66); retention/disposal (s 67); cybersecurity safeguards, extending to health data intermediaries (HDIs) (s 68); mandatory policies (s 69); **incident management framework** (s 70); directions (s 71).
- **Part 5 (ss 74–82)** — notification: assessment and notification of notifiable cybersecurity incidents (ss 74–75) and **notifiable data breaches** (significant harm or significant scale, ss 77–79) to the **Minister**, plus notification of affected individuals for significant-harm breaches (s 80); penalties up to **$1 million** for entities (s 82(1)).
- **Part 7 (ss 86–88)** — **portability**: relevant HDIs must implement practices for accurate, complete and timely transfer of health information at a contributor's/user's request (the anti-lock-in provision aimed at HIMS vendors).
- **s 90** — Minister may issue **codes of practice** on conduct, security and portability.
- **s 113** — related amendment inserting s 28(2)(ba) into the Human Organ Transplant Act 1987 (disclosure "as required or permitted under the Health Information Act 2026").

**Implementation guidance (administrative, pre-commencement):** MOH Circular MOH-MHC-0018-2026 (6 March 2026) sets a batched timeline — **Batch 1 by September 2027** (GP outpatient medical, acute and community hospitals, clinical and radiology laboratories), **Batch 2 by September 2028** (specialist outpatient, nursing homes, contingency care, renal dialysis), **Batch 3 by March 2030** (dental, ambulatory surgery, assisted reproduction, retail pharmacy); unlisted licensees need not contribute but must implement cyber/data security measures by September 2028. Contribution is prospective only. *(Note: the circular's Table 1 places Nuclear Medicine Service in Batch 2, while healthinfo.gov.sg groups nuclear medicine providers with the September 2027 batch — an unresolved discrepancy flagged for the reader.)* See Part II item 22 for the supporting instruments.

**Executive summary.** The HIA is Singapore's first purpose-built health-information statute and will, once commenced, become the dominant law of health data sharing. It does three separable things: it puts the National Electronic Health Record on a statutory footing with *mandatory* contribution by licensees and access confined to direct patient care and listed statutory examinations; it creates a parallel, non-NEHR channel for sharing "relevant information" between named public-sector actors for gazetted use cases such as Healthier SG outreach, gated by mandatory data sharing agreements; and it overlays a security, breach-notification and portability regime with penalties up to $1 million. Its defining legal move is the displacement of consent: ss 11 and 50 override other law, rules of law and (for Part 3) contractual confidentiality, replacing consent with closed lists of permitted actors, purposes and information types — and three separate provisions (ss 16(2), 39(3), 57(2)) shut the door on PDPA consent as an alternative route. Employment and insurance uses are excluded purposes outright, and secondary use is channelled through a ministerially approved "derived information" regime backed by new re-identification offences. Patient control survives only as access restrictions subject to an always-visible minimum data set and an emergency break-glass override. None of it is in force yet: as at 24 August 2026 the Act awaits commencement, and every operative obligation currently rests on the pre-existing statutes below.

## 2. Personal Data Protection Act 2012 (PDPA)

**Citation:** Personal Data Protection Act 2012 (Act 26 of 2012), 2020 Revised Edition; last amended by the Statutes (Miscellaneous Amendments) Act 2025 (Act 19 of 2025) with effect from 5 December 2025.
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; Personal Data Protection Commission (IMDA designated as the Commission, s 5).
**Status:** In force ("Current version as at 24 Aug 2026" on SSO). Part 6B (data portability) and the Twelfth Schedule, inserted by the PDP (Amendment) Act 2020, remain **uncommenced**.

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **s 2(1)** — "personal data" (identifiability, including via other accessible information). **No special/sensitive category exists**; health data is ordinary personal data. "Prescribed healthcare body" is the Act's only health-specific defined term.
- **s 4(1)(c)** — Parts 3–6B impose **no obligation on any public agency** (Government, ministries, organs of state, and statutory bodies specified under s 2(2)). **s 4(6)(b)** — other written law prevails over the PDPA to the extent of inconsistency (the gateway for the HCSA, IDA, NRDA, HBRA, MLSA and, when commenced, the HIA).
- **s 4(4)(b)** — deceased individuals: only the disclosure provisions and s 24 (protection) apply, for 10 years after death.
- **s 13** — consent or statutory authority required for collection, use and disclosure; **s 14(2)(a)** — consent may not be made a condition of service beyond what is reasonable to provide it.
- **s 15(1)–(2)** — deemed consent by conduct, extending down a disclosure chain for the same purpose; **s 15(3)–(9)** — deemed consent by contractual necessity.
- **s 15A** — **deemed consent by notification**: adverse-effect assessment, notification and opt-out; unavailable for direct marketing (reg 13, PDP Regulations 2021).
- **s 17 + First Schedule** — consent exceptions, notably: Part 1 para 1 (vital interests, with after-the-fact notification duty); **Part 1 para 2 (emergency threatening life, health or safety)**; Part 1 para 3 (health/safety seriously affected, consent not timely); **Part 1 para 4 (contacting next-of-kin or friend of an injured, ill or deceased individual)**; Part 3 para 1 (legitimate interests, with documented assessment); Part 3 para 8 (data volunteered by another individual — the PDPC's basis for family-history information, see Part II item 14); Part 4 (business asset transactions — clinic sales); Part 5 (business improvement within corporate groups).
- **s 17 + Second Schedule** — the **research exceptions**:
  - **Use without consent — Second Schedule, Part 2, Division 3, para 1**: research purpose; cannot reasonably be accomplished without identifiable form; **clear public benefit**; results not used to make decisions affecting the individual; published results do not identify individuals. *(Four conditions; no impracticability-of-consent condition.)*
  - **Disclosure without consent — Second Schedule, Part 3, Division 2, para 1**: the same conditions **plus** "(b) it is impracticable for the organisation to seek the individual's consent for the disclosure". *(Five conditions.)*
  - **There is no research exception for collection.** Collection relies on consent, deemed consent, a First Schedule head, or Second Schedule Part 1 para 1 (data disclosed by a public agency, collected consistently with the purpose of that disclosure).
  - **Second Schedule, Part 3, Division 1, para 3** — health-specific: disclosure **to a public agency, for policy formulation or review**, of personal data about an individual who is a current or former **patient of** an HCSA licensee (para 3(b)) or of a **prescribed healthcare body** (para 3(c)) — the paragraph identifies whose patient the individual is, not who discloses. (Para 3(a), which referenced the repealed PHMCA regime, was deleted by Act 19 of 2025 wef 5 December 2025.)
- **Part 6A (ss 26A–26E)** — data breach notification: notifiable if significant harm to an affected individual or significant scale (s 26B); notify the Commission within **3 calendar days** of assessment (s 26D(1)); notify affected individuals for significant-harm breaches (s 26D(2)), excused by remediation or pre-existing technological protection (s 26D(5)); notification breaches no secrecy duty or professional conduct rule (s 26D(8)); **s 26E** — a data intermediary processing for a **public agency** must notify that agency without undue delay (the one Part 6A duty reaching vendors *of public agencies*; s 4(2) preserves both s 26C(3)(a) and s 26E against data intermediaries generally, alongside ss 24–25 under Part 6).
- **Part 9B (ss 48C–48F)** — offences for egregious mishandling by individuals: unauthorised disclosure (s 48D), use for gain/harm (s 48E), and **unauthorised re-identification of anonymised information** (s 48F) — each up to $5,000 and/or 2 years; the **Eleventh Schedule** provides a safe harbour for testing anonymisation effectiveness and security. Part 9B does not apply to relevant public officials in Singapore public sector agencies, or to MAS directors/officers/employees (s 48C(2)) — public officials are covered by PSGA ss 7–8 instead.
- The Act nowhere defines "anonymisation"; anonymised data simply falls outside the s 2(1) definition (see the PDPC guidance in Part II items 15–16).

**Key subsidiary legislation:**

- **Personal Data Protection Regulations 2021 (S 63/2021)**, in force 1 February 2021, last amended by S 86/2026 wef 2 March 2026: cross-border transfer rules (regs 9–12 — comparable protection via law, contract, BCRs, any other legally binding instrument, or certification; S 86/2026 added the Global CBPR and Global PRP systems to the recognised certifications; reg 10(2)(c) carves out transfers necessary for the vital-interests/emergency/next-of-kin and national-interest heads); prescribed content and retention of the s 15A and legitimate-interests assessments (regs 14–15); **reg 16 + First Schedule — the "nearest relative" ladder** for exercising a deceased individual's rights (spouse, child, parent, sibling, other relative) within the 10-year window — a will-appointee or personal representative ranks ahead of the ladder (reg 16(2)).
- **Personal Data Protection (Notification of Data Breaches) Regulations 2021 (S 64/2021)**, in force 1 February 2021, last amended by S 800/2024 wef 15 October 2024: **reg 4** — significant scale = **500 or more** affected individuals; **reg 3(1)(a) + the Schedule** — deemed significant harm where a breach couples the individual's **full name/alias/identification number** with a Schedule Part 1 category, which include: **para 18 — diagnosis/treatment by a health professional of STIs, HIV, schizophrenia or delusional disorder, or substance abuse/addiction**; para 17(d) — health information inside insurance claims; para 19 — donation or receipt of a human egg or sperm, contraception and abortion; para 20 — organ donation/transplant; para 21 — suicide/attempted suicide; para 22 — abuse. Ordinary diagnoses and medication lists are **not** deemed categories — they fall to the general significant-harm assessment.
- **Personal Data Protection (Statutory Bodies) Notification 2013 (S 149/2013)** — gazettes which statutory bodies are "public agencies"; includes the **Health Promotion Board (item 16), Health Sciences Authority (item 17), Communicable Diseases Agency (item 9A)** and Singapore Medical Council (item 55).
- **Personal Data Protection (Prescribed Healthcare Bodies) Notification 2015 (S 90/2015)** (as amended by S 69/2021) — prescribes, for the Second Schedule: **Agency for Integrated Care Pte Ltd, National Healthcare Group Pte Ltd, National University Health System Pte Ltd, Singapore Health Services Pte Ltd**, and approved providers under the Medical and Elderly Care Endowment Schemes Act.
- **The public healthcare clusters and restructured hospitals are *not* public agencies** — they are companies fully subject to the PDPA. Confirmed by their absence from S 149/2013, by the S 90/2015 Schedule listing them as private companies (Pte Ltd), and by the enforcement decision *Re Singapore Health Services Pte Ltd & Ors* [2019] SGPDPC 3 ($250,000 penalty on SingHealth, $750,000 on IHiS, for the 2018 breach).

**Executive summary.** The PDPA is the sector-neutral baseline for health data in the private sector — which, because the clusters and restructured hospitals are companies, includes most of Singapore's hospital system. It has no special regime for health data: sharing identifiable patient information requires consent, deemed consent (by conduct, contractual necessity or notification), another written law, or an enumerated exception. Clinical care runs in practice on deemed consent by conduct; emergencies, family contact and volunteered family history each have a First Schedule head. For secondary research, the Act splits use (four conditions, Second Schedule Part 2 Division 3) from disclosure (the same plus impracticability of consent, Part 3 Division 2), and offers no research basis for fresh collection at all. Anonymised data exits the Act entirely, but the boundary is policed by the s 48F re-identification offence with an Eleventh Schedule testing safe harbour. Breach notification runs on a two-limb trigger with a three-day clock, and the NDB Regulations single out precisely the diagnoses society treats as most stigmatising — HIV/STIs, schizophrenia or delusional disorder, substance abuse and addiction, gamete donation/receipt, contraception and abortion, suicide — as deemed-harm categories notifiable to patients regardless of scale. Public agencies (MOH, HPB, HSA, CDA) are excluded from the operative Parts altogether, which is why the PSGA (item 7) and the Government's internal manuals matter; and s 4(6)(b) subordinates Parts 3–6B of the Act to the sectoral statutes (the Do Not Call and offence Parts are not subordinated), so PDPA analysis is never the final word on a Singapore health-data flow.

## 3. Healthcare Services Act 2020 (HCSA)

**Citation:** Healthcare Services Act 2020 (No. 3 of 2020); phased commencement completed **18 December 2023** (phases: 3 January 2022; 26 June 2023; 18 December 2023).
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; MOH — administered by the Director-General of Health (s 7).
**Status:** In force ("Current version as at 24 Aug 2026").

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **s 5** — the Act does not apply to healthcare services provided by the Government.
- **s 13** — open-textured **licence conditions** power (the vehicle for data-related conditions, e.g. on telemedicine and genetic testing services).
- **s 27** — the core records provision: licensees must keep and maintain records for prescribed periods (s 27(1)); implement prescribed safeguards protecting the records **and the computer systems holding them** against loss, unlawful modification/destruction and unauthorised access, disclosure, copying, use or modification (s 27(2)(a)); monitor and evaluate those safeguards (s 27(2)(b)); ensure staff awareness of confidentiality, integrity and availability roles (s 27(2)(c)); take care in disposal (s 27(3)); not keep inaccurate records (s 27(4)). Offence: up to **$20,000 and/or 12 months** (s 27(5)).
- **s 36** — Director-General's power to require any licensee to produce documents and information, including in legible form.
- **s 38** — **codes of practice** power; the s 38(2) subject-matter list covers conduct, clinical and ethical aspects, management and care of patients — but contains **no express data-security or portability head** (the gap HIA s 90 fills).
- **s 51(2)** — the Director-General and authorised officers **must not disclose medical record information** except for administering an enumerated list of Acts (including the HBRA, IDA, NRDA, MediShield Life Scheme Act, Human Organ Transplant Act) or for professional-discipline complaints; **s 51(1)** — non-compellability in proceedings.
- **s 57(2)(d)** — regulation-making power for "the records of patients, customers and staff that licensees must keep and maintain".
- **s 58** — repeals the Private Hospitals and Medical Clinics Act (effective 18 December 2023; see item 13).

**Key subsidiary legislation — Healthcare Services (General) Regulations 2021 (S 1035/2021)** (version in force from 14 October 2024):

- **reg 25** — security of equipment data: protection against unauthorised local or remote electronic access; regular review; secure transmission to authorised recipients.
- **reg 37** — mandatory content of the **patient health record** (identity fields; and where available: consultations, history, clinical findings, care plans, laboratory and imaging results, allergies, consent forms, discharge summaries, adverse events, etc.), kept as the Director-General specifies.
- **reg 38** — duty to keep every patient health record **confidential**, maintain confidentiality/integrity/security at all times, extend the same duties to **extracts and aggregated compilations** (reg 38(2)), implement and monitor safeguards, and take care in disposal.
- **reg 39** — on cessation or transfer of care: consult the patient about transfer/disposal of the record and **transfer the record or a detailed medical report** to the incoming licensee or the patient/authorised representative — currently the only operative data-portability rule in the sector.
- **Healthcare Services (General) (Amendment) Regulations 2024 (S 780/2024)**, wef 14 October 2024 — inserted **reg 39A**: every medical certificate must be issued by a practitioner who provided care to the patient and must state the practitioner's name and registration number (aimed at questionnaire-only telemedicine).

**Executive summary.** Until the HIA commences, the HCSA is the principal sectoral statute imposing health-record obligations on Singapore's licensed (non-Government) providers, and its posture is custody, not sharing: s 27 and regs 37–38 dictate what a patient record must contain and how it must be secured, monitored and destroyed, extending expressly to extracts and aggregated compilations; reg 39 supplies the only current portability mechanism (record transfer on change of provider, after consulting the patient); and s 51(2) locks up what the regulator itself learns, subject to a closed list of statutory gateways. Outbound sharing by licensees is left to the PDPA and the other statutes in this Part. Its forward-looking significance is structural: the licence-condition power (s 13) and codes-of-practice power (s 38) are the hooks on which MOH hangs instruments like the telemedicine licence conditions and the genetic-testing code of practice (Part II item 20), and the HIA is expressly designed to sit on top of HCSA licensing — the First Schedule of the HIA maps contribution duties onto HCSA licence categories.

## 4. Human Biomedical Research Act 2015 (HBRA)

**Citation:** Human Biomedical Research Act 2015 (Act 29 of 2015), 2020 Revised Edition. Staged commencement: 1 July 2016; 1 January 2017 (ss 32–33); **1 November 2017** (ss 6–31, 65, 68, Third–Fifth Schedules); **1 November 2019** (ss 34–39, 64).
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; MOH — administered by the Director of Medical Services (s 4(1)).
**Status:** In force.

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **s 3(1)–(2)** — "human biomedical research" includes any research within scope that involves **(e) the use of individually-identifiable human biological material or (f) individually-identifiable health information** — identifiability, not sensitivity, is the trigger.
- **s 2** — "individually-identifiable" uses a linkage/mosaic test ("other information to which the person … has or is likely to have access"); "health information" covers information obtained in providing healthcare or relating to diagnosis etc.; "research subject" includes a person whose material or information is used.
- **s 3(4) + Second Schedule** — carve-outs: national public health research under IDA s 59A; NRDA collection; Statistics Act collection; **clinical trials under the Health Products Act 2007 (para 6) and the Medicines Act 1975 (para 7)** — trial data is governed by the HSA regimes in item 12, not by the HBRA.
- **Part 3 (ss 6–14)** — consent: written, witnessed "appropriate consent" (s 6), preceded by the s 12 disclosures, which expressly cover **future secondary research use of individually-identifiable information (s 12(1)(i))**, identifiable-form participation (s 12(1)(k)), re-identification on incidental findings (s 12(1)(m)) and, for tissue, **export from Singapore (s 12(2)(q))**. Withdrawal does not claw back information already obtained (s 14(3)).
- **s 13(1)(b) + Fifth Schedule, Part 2** — the **IRB consent waiver for secondary use** of biological material or health information: para 3 requires that the research cannot reasonably proceed without identifiable form, obtaining consent would involve disproportionate effort, minimal risk, no adverse effect on rights/welfare, and contribution to the **"greater public good"**; paras 4–5 cover legacy information/material collected before 1 November 2017 (para 5 uniquely requires reasonable re-contact efforts for legacy material).
- **ss 27–29 — the data-protection core**: **s 27(1)** — safeguard duty over individually-identifiable information/material, expressly including "rendering information or material non-identifiable" (defined, recipient-relative, in s 27(3)) — up to $10,000/12 months; **s 28** — **re-identification prohibition** for data rendered non-identifiable, subject to listed exceptions — up to $20,000/2 years; **s 29(1)** — **prohibition on disclosing research subjects' individually-identifiable information**, with a downstream-recipient limb (s 29(2): recipients who knew or had reason to believe the information was unlawfully communicated) — up to $20,000/2 years. **s 39** replicates s 29 for **tissue donors**.
- **s 22(2)(c) and (6)** — conducting research without appropriate consent for the use of identifiable material/information: up to **$50,000/5 years**.
- **Part 4 (ss 15–23)** — statutory IRBs: appointment and composition duties (s 15; reg 11(2) of the 2017 Regulations: at least 5 members, medically qualified chair, at least one external scientific and one external lay member); exemption and expedited review (s 17(2); regs 20–21).
- **Part 6 (ss 32–39)** — tissue banking, including **s 35(2)(f)–(g)** (export and removal-from-supervision controls).
- **Third Schedule** — *prohibited* research; **Fourth Schedule** — *restricted* research (human eggs/embryos, cytoplasmic hybrids, human-animal combinations — **not genomic data**: there is no genomics-specific statutory tier; genomic data sharing runs on the general provisions).
- **ss 40–41** — codes of practice have **no legislative effect** (s 40(3)) but are admissible in evidence and must be had regard to in assessing reasonableness (s 41(2)–(3)) — the mechanism by which the BAC Guidelines (Part II items 17–18) acquire practical legal weight.

**Key subsidiary legislation (all republished in 2025 editions):**

- **Human Biomedical Research Regulations 2017 (S 621/2017)** — institutional duties; **reg 10D**: every research institution must have, and inform subjects of, a policy on re-identification and return of **incidental findings**; IRB composition and review procedures (regs 11–22).
- **HBR (Restricted Research) Regulations 2017 (S 622/2017)** — approvals and special consent for restricted research.
- **HBR (Tissue Banking) Regulations 2019 (S 702/2019)** — **reg 15(4)**: export of individually-identifiable tissue requires **donor consent to the export plus a recipient undertaking**; reg 16: confidentiality-system duty (offence); reg 19: tissue-bank incidental-findings policy. There is **no equivalent statutory export gate for health *information*** — a genuine gap.
- **HBR (Requirements for Appropriate Consent — Exemption) Regulations 2019 (S 703/2019)** — **reg 2**: the prescribed-witness requirement is disapplied for non-invasive, non-interventional, non-restricted research, with survey/information-collection research deemed such — the practical enabler of pure data research.
- **HBR (Tissue Banking and Notification — Exemption) Regulations 2019 (S 704/2019)** — exempts entities banking tissue solely for their own IRB-approved research/trials from ss 34–37.
- *(The HBR (Exemption) Regulations 2018 were transitional and expired on 31 October 2019 — they are sometimes miscited as current.)*

**Executive summary.** The HBRA is the operative statute for research use of Singapore health and genomic data, and it bites on identifiability: any research using individually-identifiable health information or biological material is "human biomedical research" requiring IRB approval and written, witnessed consent whose content must cover future secondary use, and the linkage-based definition of identifiability makes genomic datasets hard to hold outside the Act. The lawful route to sharing and secondary use without fresh consent is the Fifth Schedule Part 2 IRB waiver, whose "disproportionate effort / minimal risk / greater public good" test is the workhorse of Singapore's retrospective and databank research. Once data is held, three offences apply: a safeguard duty naming de-identification as a compliance measure (s 27), a re-identification ban (s 28), and a disclosure ban that follows the data downstream to recipients on a knew-or-ought-to-know basis (s 29, mirrored for tissue donors by s 39). Two structural points are routinely misstated and worth fixing: the Fourth Schedule's "restricted research" tier is about embryos and human-animal combinations, not genetics — Singapore has no genomics-specific statutory tier — and clinical-trial data is carved out of the HBRA entirely and governed by the thinner HSA records regimes. Cross-border movement is statutorily controlled only for tissue (reg 15(4)), not for health information as such, leaving data-export protection to consent terms, the PDPA transfer rules and contracts.

## 5. Infectious Diseases Act 1976 (IDA)

**Citation:** Infectious Diseases Act 1976, 2020 Revised Edition; version in force from 1 April 2025 (as amended by the Infectious Diseases (Amendment) Act 2024 (Act 13 of 2024) wef 9 April 2024 and the Communicable Diseases Agency Act 2025 (Act 4 of 2025) wef 1 April 2025).
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; MOH / Director-General of Health, with operational functions since 1 April 2025 exercised through the **Communicable Diseases Agency**.
**Status:** In force.

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **s 6(1)–(3)** — criminal **notification duties**: every medical practitioner who has reason to believe a patient has a prescribed infectious disease or is a carrier must notify the Director-General; so must persons in charge of diagnostic laboratories and prescribed classes of persons. **s 6(6)** — reverse presumption of knowledge. Prescribed diseases and timelines: Infectious Diseases (Notification of Prescribed Infectious Diseases) Regulations 2008 (S 612/2008, last amended S 189/2025).
- **s 7(2)** — surveillance: the Director-General may require **any person or class of persons** to provide information and samples and submit to examination.
- **s 10(1)** — power to require any healthcare professional to obtain information from a patient and transmit it; **s 10(4)** — the professional must comply **"despite any restriction on the disclosure of information imposed by any written law, rule of law, rule of professional conduct or contract"** — the strongest confidentiality override currently in force in the health sector.
- **s 25(1)** — the Act's only confidentiality prohibition, and it is **HIV/STI-specific**: persons performing functions under the Act must not disclose identifying information about a person with HIV or an STI, subject to twelve exceptions (consent; administration of the Act; police under specified CPC provisions; court order; treating clinicians; blood/organ/semen/milk banks; **anonymised statistics and epidemiology (g)**; sexual assault victims; the Controller of Immigration; next-of-kin on death; **a Director-General public-interest gateway (k)**; ministerially authorised public-health publication). Offence: up to $10,000/3 months (s 25(2)).
- **s 25A** — authorised disclosures of HIV status to exposed healthcare workers and first responders (s 25A(1)–(2)) and **partner notification** by a practitioner under strict preconditions (s 25A(4)), with an onward-disclosure bar on recipients (s 25A(7)).
- **s 57A** — the Director-General may disclose identifying information about actual or suspected cases/at-risk individuals **to anyone who needs it to prevent spread**, and **to the WHO** to meet international obligations; recipients are purpose-limited (s 57A(3)).
- **s 57B** — the Director-General may, by written notice, authorise **provider-to-provider disclosure** of identifying information to specified recipients for outbreak prevention, with conditions and purpose limitation (s 57B(3)).
- **s 59A** — **national public health research**: the Director-General may undertake or facilitate research in the public interest and compel any person to provide information and samples (including human samples) for it (s 59A(3)) — research so conducted is excluded from the HBRA (HBRA Second Schedule).
- **s 67A** — immunity: good-faith, reasonable-care disclosure under the Act or a s 57B authorisation is no offence, no breach of confidence, no civil wrong and no disciplinary matter.
- **ss 46–51 (Part 6)** — childhood vaccination duties; **s 48(2)** — every vaccinator must record all vaccinations and notify the Director-General of Health or designated CDA officers — the statutory feed of the **National Immunisation Registry** (which has no express establishing provision of its own).

**Executive summary.** The IDA is the most sharing-permissive statute in the landscape: it is built of compelled-disclosure duties and disclosure-authorising powers rather than protections. Practitioners and laboratories must notify prescribed diseases on pain of criminal penalty with a reverse burden; the Director-General can compel information and samples from anyone for surveillance and, under s 59A, for national public health research that is carved out of the HBRA altogether; and s 10(4) overrides every source of confidentiality — statutory, common-law, professional and contractual — when the Director-General requires patient information for outbreak control. Downstream, ss 57A and 57B allow identifying information to flow to whoever needs it to stop transmission (including the WHO), disciplined only by purpose-limitation duties on recipients and a good-faith immunity for disclosers. Against this, the Act's single confidentiality prohibition, s 25, protects only HIV/STI identity and is porous — twelve exceptions, including an open-ended Director-General public-interest gateway. Since 1 April 2025 the operational machinery (including the National Immunisation Registry, fed by s 48(2) vaccination notifications) sits with the new Communicable Diseases Agency (item 11).

## 6. National Registry of Diseases Act 2007 (NRDA)

**Citation:** National Registry of Diseases Act 2007 (Act 56 of 2007), 2020 Revised Edition; version in force from **5 December 2025** (amended by the Statutes (Miscellaneous Amendments) Act 2025 (Act 19 of 2025), which re-anchored the definition of "healthcare institution" on HCSA-approved premises for nine licensable services, replacing the obsolete PHMCA-based definition).
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; MOH — Registrar appointed by the Director-General (s 3); administered through the National Registry of Diseases Office.
**Status:** In force; commenced 1 August 2009.

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **s 4–s 5** — establishes the National Registry of Diseases and requires the Registrar to maintain a register of **individually-identifiable information** for each reportable disease; the registers are **not open to public inspection** (s 5(2)).
- **The Schedule** — reportable diseases: **cancer** (including CNS tumours of all behaviours), donor nephrectomy and hepatic resection, **chronic kidney failure**, **acute myocardial infarction**.
- **s 6(1)** — the **manager of a healthcare institution must notify** the Registrar of diagnoses/treatment of reportable diseases (offence: fine up to $2,000, s 6(2)).
- **s 7(2)** — the Registrar may require additional prescribed information and **production of medical records for inspection**.
- **s 8(1)–(3)** — Registry personnel are non-compellable in proceedings and prohibited from disclosing register contents or identifiable information (offence: up to $10,000/12 months).
- **s 9** — release of Registry information **in anonymised form** on request (and mandatorily to the Director-General).
- **s 10** — identifiable disclosure for **national public health programmes** requires Director-General approval, and **s 10(3)** bars approval "unless satisfied the programme cannot be carried out with anonymised information" — a statutory anonymisation-first test.
- **s 11** — identifiable disclosure to a treating practitioner requires necessity **and the requisite consent** (s 11(c)).
- **s 12** — identifiable disclosure for **public health research** requires that the research cannot be done with anonymised information (s 12(2)(a)), **the requisite consent (s 12(2)(b))**, a quality/public-health benefit finding, and compliance conditions; researchers may not re-disclose identities, nor identify institutions or notifiers without their consent (s 12(3)–(4)); the Registrar may refer applications to a national research advisory committee (s 12(5)).

**Executive summary.** The NRDA is a narrow but instructive statute: it compels identifiable disease reporting into closed national registers (cancer, kidney failure, AMI and donor events), then guards release with the strictest necessity architecture in Singapore health-data law. Anonymised release is comparatively open; identifiable release is permitted only where the purpose *cannot* be achieved with anonymised data — a statutory data-minimisation mandate (ss 10(3), 12(2)(a)) with no counterpart in the PDPA or the HIA — and, for treatment and research, only with consent. It thus sits at the opposite pole from the IDA and the HIA's consent-override architecture, and it matters practically to researchers because registry linkage is a staple of Singapore epidemiology: any project seeking identifiable registry data must budget for the s 12 gate, and after 5 December 2025 the reporting duty attaches to HCSA-approved premises across nine licensable service categories.

## 7. Public Sector (Governance) Act 2018 (PSGA)

**Citation:** Public Sector (Governance) Act 2018 (Act 5 of 2018), 2020 Revised Edition; commenced 1 April 2018; version in force from 1 August 2026. The **Public Sector (Governance) (Amendment) Act 2026 (Act 2 of 2026)** (passed 12 January 2026, assented 3 February 2026, gazetted 16 February 2026) is **not yet in force** — no commencement notification as at 24 August 2026.
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; PMO / MDDI (public-sector data governance).
**Status:** In force (unamended text operative).

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **s 2(1)** — "Singapore public sector agency" = public bodies (bodies corporate established by public Act — which **excludes** the healthcare clusters, as companies), ministries and departments (including MOH), organs of state and declared officials. HPB and HSA are Group 1A public bodies (First Schedule, Part 1, items 14–15).
- **s 4(1)–(4)** — ministerial **directions** to agencies on "pertinent subject matter", expressly including **data governance and inter-agency data sharing (s 4(3)(e))** and data analytics (s 4(2)(c)); directions may take the form of circulars or instructions (s 9(1)(a)) and have **no legislative effect** (s 9(2)) but must be complied with (s 10). This is the statutory frame for the (unpublished) Government Instruction Manual on ICT&SS Management ("IM8").
- **s 6(1)** — where a data sharing direction is given, sharing with **another Singapore public sector agency** is authorised **"despite any obligation as to confidentiality under the common law"**; legal privilege and contractual confidentiality are preserved (s 6(2)).
- **s 7** — offences: knowing/reckless **unauthorised disclosure** by a relevant public official (s 7(1)); unauthorised **use** with gain/harm/loss (s 7(3)); contractor misuse — but **only for information other than personal data** (s 7(4); contractors' personal-data misconduct is caught by PDPA Part 9B instead). Each: fine up to $5,000 and/or 2 years.
- **s 8** — knowing/reckless **unauthorised re-identification** of anonymised information under an agency's control (same penalty), with a safe harbour keyed to the PDPA's Eleventh Schedule specified purposes (s 8(2)(c), (4)).
- **Amendment Act 2026 (uncommenced)** — will convert "data sharing directions" into "data sharing **and use** directions"; authorise sharing with **non-public sector persons** subject to ministerial "further authorisation" (new s 4(5)–(8)); extend offences to employees of recipient organisations (new s 7(1A), (4A), again excluding personal data); and insert PDPA s 48C(3) so that Part 9B offences attach to personal data shared out under a direction. MDDI frames this as enabling data flows to community and social-sector partners.

**Executive summary.** The PSGA is the public sector's answer to the PDPA's absence: since public agencies are excluded from the PDPA's operative Parts, intra-government data sharing (MOH, HPB, HSA, CDA and every ministry) runs on ministerial data-sharing directions that override common-law confidentiality between agencies, backed post-SingHealth by criminal offences for unauthorised disclosure, gainful misuse and re-identification by public officials. Two boundaries define its reach into health data. First, the public healthcare clusters and restructured hospitals are companies, not public bodies, so PSGA directions cannot authorise MOH–cluster flows; those run instead on the PDPA's public-agency disclosure exceptions (Second Schedule Part 3 Division 1 paras 1 and 3) — and, prospectively, on HIA Part 3. Second, PSGA directions are administrative and unpublished, so a material layer of the rules actually governing government-held health data (IM8 and the Government Data Security Policies incorporating the 2019 PSDSRC measures) is not publicly citable. The 2026 Amendment Act will, when commenced, extend directed sharing beyond government to vetted external partners with a two-key authorisation structure — passed the same day as the HIA, it is the other half of Singapore's 2026 data-sharing settlement, and equally not yet in force.

## 8. Cybersecurity Act 2018

**Citation:** Cybersecurity Act 2018 (Act 9 of 2018), 2020 Revised Edition; principal commencement 31 August 2018; as amended by the **Cybersecurity (Amendment) Act 2024 (Act 19 of 2024)** — passed 7 May 2024, key provisions commenced **31 October 2025** (S 677/2025); version in force from 31 October 2025.
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; Commissioner of Cybersecurity / Cyber Security Agency (CSA).
**Status:** In force. New Parts 3C (entities of special cybersecurity interest) and 3D (foundational digital infrastructure — cloud and data centres) are enacted but **uncommenced**.

**Pinpoint provisions:**

- **First Schedule — essential services**: healthcare appears as two items — **item 10 "Acute hospital care services"** and **item 11 "Services relating to disease surveillance and response"** (the First Schedule's 46 services sit under 11 sector headings).
- **s 7(1)** — designation of **provider-owned critical information infrastructure (CII)** (the 2024 Act renamed the concept); **s 7(1A)** — extension to systems located wholly outside Singapore; "computer/computer system" now includes **virtual** systems (s 2, as amended).
- **ss 10, 12–16** — CII owner duties: furnish information on design/configuration/security (s 10); comply with written directions (s 12); notify ownership changes within 7 days (s 13, penalty up to $100,000/2 years); **report prescribed cybersecurity incidents** affecting the CII and interconnected systems (s 14); biennial audits and risk assessments (s 15); participate in exercises (s 16). *(Former s 11 — codes of practice — was repealed; the power now lives in s 35A.)*
- **Part 3A (ss 16A–16L)** — the 2024 Act's **third-party-owned CII regime**: a provider of essential services running its CII on someone else's system (i.e., cloud) can be designated as responsible, and must then obtain **legally binding commitments** from the owner that prescribed cybersecurity standards will be maintained (s 16F) and that incidents will be notified (s 16I) — on pain of being ordered to stop using the system (s 16F(2)). Operationalised by the Cybersecurity (Providers of Essential Service Responsible for Cybersecurity of Third-Party-Owned CII) Regulations 2026 (S 490/2026), in force 13 July 2026.
- **s 35A** — codes of practice. The operative code is the **Cybersecurity Code of Practice for Critical Information Infrastructure 2026 (CCoP 2026, Release 1)**, effective **29 July 2026**, with phased compliance to 29 July 2027 (board accountability, interconnected-systems section) and 31 December 2027 (Cyber Trust Mark certification); a separate cloud-services CCoP is planned for H2 2026.
- Interface: **HIA s 83(2)** expressly preserves the Cybersecurity Act's operation alongside HIA Part 6.

**Executive summary.** The Cybersecurity Act protects the systems on which health data lives rather than the data itself, and reaches healthcare through two designated essential services — acute hospital care and disease surveillance/response. A designated hospital EMR or national surveillance platform carries information-furnishing, incident-reporting, audit and exercise duties, and since 31 October 2025 the amended Act closes the cloud gap: a hospital whose critical system runs on a hyperscaler must now bind that provider contractually to prescribed security and incident-notification standards or be ordered off the platform, with the 2026 regulations and the CCoP 2026 supplying the operative detail. The enacted-but-uncommenced Parts 3C and 3D will eventually regulate major cloud and data-centre providers directly. For health data governance the Act matters as the third layer of a stack: a healthcare provider can simultaneously face CSA obligations here, PDPC breach-notification obligations under the PDPA, and — once the HIA commences — MOH-facing incident and breach notification under HIA Parts 4–5, which are expressly cumulative with this Act.

## 9. MediShield Life Scheme Act 2015 (MLSA)

**Citation:** MediShield Life Scheme Act 2015 (Act 4 of 2015), 2020 Revised Edition; version in force from 1 April 2025 (amended by Act 40 of 2024).
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; MOH/CPF Board.
**Status:** In force.

**Pinpoint provisions (Part 5 — Disclosure of information, ss 26–30):**

- **s 26** — ministerially certified requisitions of **confidential information** held by Government departments and public authorities for administering the Scheme or disbursing healthcare-related subsidies; the holding entity "is to comply".
- **s 27(1)** — certified requests for **health information held by medical institutions and practitioners** to assess pre-existing-condition premium loading or claims; **s 27(2)(b)(ii)** — onward disclosure to an insurer only as a report stating **whether** a pre-existing condition exists "(without identifying the pre-existing medical condition)" and the loading amount; **s 27(3)–(4)** — patient **opt-out** right (minors from age 16).
- **s 28** — certified requests for **means information** for eligibility assessment; onward provision only as an **eligibility-tier report** (s 28(3)); opt-out (s 28(4)).
- **s 29(1)** — offence of unconsented access/use/disclosure of information obtained under ss 26–28 (up to $5,000/12 months); **s 29(2)** — good-faith immunity covering statutory offences, breach of confidence, civil liability and professional discipline; **s 29(3)(a)** — ss 26–28 and the immunity apply **"despite any other written law"** — the broadest express override in Singapore health-data law.

**Executive summary.** The MLSA's Part 5 is easy to overlook and remarkable in its drafting: to run national health insurance and means-tested subsidies, it lets the Minister certify requisitions of clinical information from any doctor or institution and means data from any relevant authority, overrides every other written law and the duty of confidence in doing so, and immunises good-faith compliance against criminal, civil and disciplinary consequences. Its saving graces are equally instructive: genuine patient opt-outs (ss 27(3), 28(4)) and hard output-minimisation — an insurer may learn only *that* a pre-existing condition exists and the loading, never the diagnosis, and a scheme administrator only the eligibility tier. It is the clearest existing model of the "statutory authorisation + minimisation" design the HIA now generalises.

## 10. Human Organ Transplant Act 1987 (HOTA)

**Citation:** Human Organ Transplant Act 1987 (Act 15 of 1987), 2020 Revised Edition; version in force from 5 December 2025.
**Type / administering body:** Primary legislation; MOH.
**Status:** In force.

**Pinpoint provisions:** **s 9** — the Director-General must maintain the register of objections (the HOTA opt-out register), which is not open to public inspection. **s 28(1)** — prohibition on disclosure enabling public identification of organ donors, consent-givers or recipients; **s 28(2)** exceptions include "(d) for the purposes of hospital administration or **bona fide medical research**" and (e) consent; offence up to $10,000/12 months (s 28(3)). **HIA s 113** will insert **s 28(2)(ba)** (disclosure as required or permitted under the HIA) on commencement.

**Executive summary.** HOTA is marginal to data sharing but earns its place for two provisions: an identity-protection offence covering the transplant triangle, and — unusually — a research exception (s 28(2)(d)) far broader than anything in the NRDA or PDPA, permitting identifying disclosure for "bona fide medical research" without consent or necessity conditions. The pending HIA-related amendment shows the new Act's gateway architecture reaching into older statutes.

## 12. Clinical trials regulations (HSA regimes)

**Citations:** **Health Products (Clinical Trials) Regulations 2016 (S 331/2016)** (made under s 72 Health Products Act 2007; commenced 1 November 2016; consolidation in force from 18 December 2023) and **Medicines (Clinical Trials) Regulations 2016 (S 335/2016)** (made under s 18 Medicines Act 1975; commenced 1 November 2016).
**Type / administering body:** Subsidiary legislation; Health Sciences Authority (regulator).
**Status:** In force. Clinical trials under these regimes are **excluded from the HBRA** (HBRA Second Schedule paras 6–7).

**Pinpoint provisions:** **reg 19(1)(n)–(o)** (both sets) — trial subjects must be told **who will access their medical records** (including HSA's inspection power) and how far identifying records will be kept confidential. **reg 23** (both) — sponsor/investigator records enabling evaluation of "the quality of the data produced", available to HSA at all times, retained at least **6 years post-trial**. **regs 23A(3)–(4)** (S 331/2016 only) — for cell/tissue/gene therapy products, a mandatory system linking product to subject "and vice versa", retained **30 years** — a statutorily compelled long-term identifiability link. **reg 27** (S 331/2016 only) — constraints on **HSA's** disclosure of confidential trial information; **reg 28** — HSA's clinical trial register. The Medicines regulations contain **no confidentiality or register provision** (their reg 27 is an information-gathering power).

**Executive summary.** Clinical-trial data sits in its own lane: carved out of the HBRA, it is governed by consent-disclosure and records-and-inspection rules rather than by safeguard/disclosure offences. Subject protection is delivered through what participants must be told (who sees their records) rather than through substantive limits on sponsors, and the CTGT traceability rules cut the other way entirely, compelling a 30-year product-to-subject identifiability link. The confidentiality obligations that do exist restrain the regulator, not the sponsor.

# Part II — Codes of practice, advisory guidelines, ethics guidance and policy instruments

## 14. PDPC Advisory Guidelines for the Healthcare Sector

**Citation/status:** Issued 11 September 2014; **revised 20 September 2023** (current as at 24 August 2026 — not yet updated for the HIA). Issued by the PDPC under **PDPA s 49(1)**, developed with MOH. **Advisory only** — not legally binding; the Act and Regulations prevail on inconsistency.

**Pinpoint positions:** para 2.3 — deemed consent by conduct legitimises the clinical episode; para 2.17 — registration at a hospital is deemed consent to collection/use/disclosure for the visit and related care, including internal case conferences; **para 2.24** — consent for treatment covers maintaining records on a database and **disclosing records to other healthcare institutions through that database** for the consented purpose (the key paragraph for shared-records design); para 2.21 — a relative's family history volunteered by the patient is collected without the relative's consent under First Schedule Part 3 para 8; para 2.22 — teaching materials need notification/consent unless anonymised; internal quality assurance generally does not; para 2.23 — emergencies run on First Schedule Part 1 para 2, with recommended after-the-fact notification of family; paras 2.14–2.16 and worked example 2.27 — the research exceptions, expressly locating use in Second Schedule Part 2 Division 3 and adding the impracticability condition for disclosure, with IRB opinion as a relevant consideration; para 4.4–4.5 — retention (no fixed statutory period; MOH's 2022 Revised Guidelines for the Retention Periods of Medical Records flagged); para 5.2 — organisations must continue to comply with the HCSA, NRDA, IDA and Advance Medical Directive Act, which prevail (PDPA s 4(6)(b)). *(Negative finding: the current edition contains no general next-of-kin disclosure guidance — "next-of-kin" appears nowhere; the statutory basis for family contact remains First Schedule Part 1 para 4.)*

**Executive summary.** This is the only PDPC instrument written specifically for health data and the primary interpretive text mapping the PDPA onto clinical practice. Its central moves are to rest routine care on deemed consent by conduct — stretched, at para 2.24, to cover shared clinical databases serving the treatment purpose — while holding a restrictive line on secondary purposes: no bundling of research or teaching consents into care, anonymise where possible, and use the two-tier research exception (with documented impracticability for disclosure) where not. Chapter 5 is its most consequential doctrinal statement: the PDPA yields to the sectoral statutes, so these Guidelines are the start, never the end, of a Singapore health-data analysis. A real currency gap: revised September 2023, they say nothing about the HIA.

## 15. PDPC Advisory Guidelines — Key Concepts and Selected Topics

**Citations/status:** *Advisory Guidelines on Key Concepts in the PDPA* — issued 23 September 2013, **revised 29 April 2026** (current). *Advisory Guidelines on the PDPA for Selected Topics* — issued 24 September 2013, **revised 23 May 2024** (current). Both PDPC, s 49(1), non-binding.

**Pinpoint positions (health-data-relevant):**

- **Key Concepts paras 12.80–12.83** — the authoritative gloss on the research exceptions: use conditions restated (12.80); disclosure adds impracticability (12.81); **12.82** — "impracticable" factors: no current contact information; disproportionate resource/time demands relative to grant and timeline (no fixed subject-number threshold); consent would skew or bias the research; **12.83** — "mere inconvenience … would not amount to 'impracticability'".
- **Key Concepts para 12.69(b)** — sensitivity of the data raises the likely adverse effect in deemed-consent-by-notification and legitimate-interests assessments — the general-regime hook for treating health data as higher-risk.
- **Key Concepts ch 20** — breach notification walked through, including the deemed-harm classes (20.15: full name/alias/ID number plus, inter alia, "specified medical information" — STI/HIV, schizophrenia or delusional disorder, substance abuse; a worked example makes a ~50-patient STI-records breach notifiable to Commission and patients) and the 500-individual scale limb (20.19–20.21).
- **Key Concepts (p 73 worked example)** — a healthcare group may **not** rely on the business-improvement exception to share medical records with an affiliated insurer for claims automation: aggregated data would do, and "the sharing of medical information for this purpose is unlikely to be considered appropriate to a reasonable person".
- **Selected Topics ch 2 (Analytics and Research)** — anonymise first (2.1); business improvement vs the "upstream" research exception (2.3–2.8); **2.9** — retention for research is a valid business reason only with "an immediate and demonstrable intent" to analyse — no indefinite "future research" retention of identifiable data.
- **Selected Topics ch 3 (Anonymisation)** — the PDPC's legal test: data is not anonymised "if there is a serious possibility that an individual could be re-identified", judged on data-plus-context and safeguards (3.4); de-identification (mere removal of direct identifiers) is expressly **not** anonymisation (3.2); health information is a sensitive "target attribute" (3.3(c)); quantified thresholds — **k=5 with safeguards for external sharing, k=3 for internal use** (3.9); the "motivated intruder" test (3.36–3.38); HIV records as the paradigm case for restraint even below the threshold (3.28); release to "a closed community of researchers" as a risk control (3.29); recipients of properly anonymised data are outside the Act but should be contractually barred from re-identification, which is in any event a Part 9B offence (3.18, 3.40); check the destination jurisdiction's definition before exporting "anonymised" datasets (3.44).

**Executive summary.** These two horizontal guidelines carry the doctrinal weight for health-data research and anonymisation. Key Concepts supplies the only official elaboration of the research exceptions' impracticability condition — a demanding, documented standard under which inconvenience never suffices — and the worked examples draw a bright line against sharing medical records with insurers under the business-improvement head. Selected Topics chapter 3 is, in substance, Singapore's anonymisation law: a contextual "serious possibility of re-identification" test with regulator-quantified k-anonymity thresholds, an explicit sensitivity uplift for health information, and endorsement of controlled research enclaves — the conceptual foundation on which TRUST's "anonymised to Whole-of-Government standards" model rests. Both are advisory; both are current (April 2026 and May 2024 editions).

## 16. PDPC anonymisation and privacy-enhancing-technology guides

**Citations/status:** *Guide to Basic Anonymisation* (first published 31 March 2022; file updated 24 July 2024) — a practical guide, expressly **not** implying PDPA compliance, scoped to structured tabular textual data only (imaging, genomic sequence and free-text clinical data are out of scope); five-step workflow with technique annexes and k-anonymity computation. *Guide on Synthetic Data Generation* — released as a "proposed guide" in 2024 (PDPC announcement of 15 July 2024; the PDPC's guidance listing also carries a 24 September 2024 date) **[date basis flagged for verification]**, developed with A*STAR under the PET Sandbox — the regulator's position on when synthetic derivatives escape the PDPA, with anti-re-identification good practices. *Guide on Federated Learning* — published **20 July 2026**, directly relevant to multi-institution health-data collaboration without pooling records. There is **no** PDPC document titled "Guide to Advanced Anonymisation" — the advanced layer is Selected Topics ch 3 plus the PET guides.

**Executive summary.** These operational guides make the anonymisation-first strategy usable: the Basic Anonymisation guide gives health-data holders the workflow and techniques (while warning that complex, longitudinal or non-tabular data — most interesting health data — needs expert treatment beyond its scope), and the newer PET guides chart the sanctioned escape routes from consent friction: synthetic cohorts and federated analysis. None is binding, and none substitutes for the "serious possibility" legal test in the Selected Topics guidelines.

## 17. BAC — Ethics Guidelines for Human Biomedical Research (2021)

**Citation/status:** *Ethics Guidelines for Human Biomedical Research*, Bioethics Advisory Committee, **2021 revised edition** (published 27 October 2021; first edition 2015). Advisory: the BAC "is a policy advisory body, not an executive body; hence it has no supervisory or regulatory power" (para 1.2). Practical force via HBRA s 41(3) (regard must be had to applicable ethics codes in assessing reasonableness).

**Pinpoint positions:** para 4.2 — three-tier taxonomy (identified / reversibly de-identified / irreversibly de-identified; the first two are "identifiable"); para 4.17 — IRB review for all personal-information research, with IRB discretion between specific and general consent; para 4.18 — de-identify as early as possible, proportionality; para 4.21 — five-limb waiver test for medical-records research; **para 4.22** — research-derived health information should not be disclosed to third parties "including employers or insurance companies" (the ethical antecedent of the insurance Moratorium, item 21); paras 3.38–3.42 — **duty to return clinically significant incidental findings**, actionable or not, to participants who asked to know; **para 3.48 and 5.14** — endorsement of **general (broad) consent** for storage and future use without re-consent, subject to donor-imposed limits and IRB oversight; para 5.15 — specific consent for "sensitive" research; para 5.6 — the "gift" model extended to research data, enabling sharing and re-analysis subject to safeguards; para 5.5 — international collaborations require agreed common ethical standards; **para 6.15** — whole-genome research participants must be told of "the difficulty in guaranteeing their anonymity with complete certainty" and of practical limits on withdrawal; para 6.17 — no clinical germline modification.

**Executive summary.** The 2021 Guidelines are the national ethics baseline that the HBRA's open-textured provisions presuppose, and their data positions are more developed than the statute's: a de-identification taxonomy finer than the HBRA's binary, an explicit endorsement of broad consent under IRB supervision, a hardened duty to return significant incidental findings, a purpose-limitation norm keeping research data away from employers and insurers, and — for genomics — a candid concession that whole-genome anonymity cannot be guaranteed, converting privacy protection into a matter of governance and security rather than data-state. Not law, but through HBRA s 41(3) the closest thing to it that Singapore research ethics has.

## 18. BAC — Ethical Use of Big Data and Artificial Intelligence in Biomedical Research (2025)

**Citation/status:** *Ethical Use of Big Data and Artificial Intelligence in Biomedical Research*, BAC, published **21 February 2025** (after a 2023 public consultation). Advisory. **Citation caution:** recommendations are cited by paragraph (12.1–12.44), not as numbered "Recommendations".

**Pinpoint positions:** 12.2 — solidarity: data as a collective resource; 12.12 — high-quality, reproducible data "should also be shared for subsequent scientifically valid proposals"; 12.16 — federated and swarm learning recommended; 12.18 — consent communications must include re-identification risks; **12.21** — international collaborations should "only share data with countries that have similar ethical guidelines"; 12.22 — institutional data-governance frameworks for sharing, especially public-private, with audit mechanisms; 12.23 — institutions as **custodians, not owners**; 12.24 — custodians must comply with (then-Bill) health-information legislation; 12.28 — early anonymisation, data minimisation, lineage tracking, periodic anonymity reassessment; 12.33 — public incidental-findings policies; robust IRB review before returning late findings not foreseen at consent; **12.37** — IRBs to evaluate the suitability of the consent model ("specific, broad, dynamic"); 12.38 — codes of practice for research data sharing with sanctions (including funding withdrawal) for deliberate re-identification, building on the **Five Safes**.

**Executive summary.** The 2025 report is the BAC's data-sharing manifesto: it reframes biomedical data as a solidarity resource whose *sharing* is an ethical good, then disciplines that stance with governance — custodianship not ownership, documented frameworks and audits for public-private flows, privacy-preserving computation, lineage tracking, and a striking ethical data-localisation norm limiting international sharing to jurisdictions with similar ethical standards. It is also the first Singapore ethics instrument to endorse dynamic consent by name and to anticipate the HIA. Its Five-Safes-based recommendation for research data-sharing codes of practice reads as an ethical blueprint for what TRUST already operationalises.

## 19. SMC Ethical Code and Ethical Guidelines (2016)

**Citation/status:** *SMC Ethical Code and Ethical Guidelines*, 2016 edition — published 13 September 2016, in force 1 January 2017; **still the current edition as at 2026** (a revision announced in December 2020 has not issued). Professional code of the Singapore Medical Council; not legislation — "if there is a conflict between the ECEG and the law, the law takes precedence" (Preamble 1(7)) — but serious disregard grounds disciplinary proceedings under the Medical Registration Act (Preamble 1(6)).

**Pinpoint positions — Guideline C7 (Medical confidentiality)** *(note: C7, not C6 — C6 is consent)*: maintain confidentiality unless the patient consents or an exception applies; take reasonable care over record-system security and comply with security protocols; **do not access patient information if not involved in the patient's care** (the anti-snooping rule); no careless or social-media dissemination; unconsented disclosure defensible when mandated by law, necessary to protect patients or others from harm, beneficial to minors via guardians, or in the patient's best interests; **in research, abide by IRB-imposed confidentiality requirements** (making IRB data conditions professionally enforceable); care-team sharing permitted to facilitate care; confidentiality persists after death, except for next-of-kin/executor requests, "legitimate clinical audit, education or research", or official inquiries.

**Executive summary.** The ECEG supplies the professional-conduct layer beneath the statutes: nearly all Singapore health data originates in a clinical encounter governed by C7, whose disclosure gate is narrower than HBRA s 29's exception list or the IDA's gateways — meaning a doctor can face disciplinary exposure for a disclosure the statutes permit, unless a statutory override (IDA s 10(4), MLSA s 29(2), PDPA s 26D(8)) expressly covers it. For data governance its two working parts are the anti-snooping rule, which polices clinician-researchers' access to records they do not clinically hold, and the research bullet, which turns IRB confidentiality conditions into professional obligations.

## 20. MOH Code of Practice — clinical genetic and genomic testing

**Citation/status:** *Standards for the Provision of Clinical Genetic/Genomic Testing Services (CGTS) and Clinical Laboratory Genetic/Genomic Testing Services (LGTS)* — Code of Practice issued **1 July 2018** under the PHMCA; updated by **MOH Circular No. 234/2020 (16 December 2020)** in consultation with the Genetic Testing Advisory Committee. **Transitional status:** its enabling statute was repealed on 18 December 2023; **MOH Circular No. 11/2025 (14 March 2025)** announced its translation into HCSA licence conditions and guidelines (Level 3 CGTS to become an MOH-approved "Specified Service" under the Outpatient Medical Service), with detailed requirements promised for 2H 2025 and a sunrise period — issuance as at August 2026 **[UNVERIFIED]**.

**Pinpoint positions:** Part A principle (d) — protection of privacy and confidentiality of personal data; para 21.1 — documented consent for Level 3 tests must cover confidentiality extent (i), further use of genetic information **including after death** (o), sample use and disposal (p), retention period and storage location of unused samples (q), and **(r) a separate HBRA-compliant consent form** where samples are stored for current or future research — the express clinical-to-research hand-off; para 22 — disclosure of Level 3 results to the patient or authorised substitute decision-makers; **para 37.3** — genetic test results and genetic information must not be disclosed "to any third parties **(including family members)**" without prior consent of the patient or the appropriate substitute decision-maker — a stricter default than the ECEG's care-team allowance, blocking unconsented cascade disclosure to relatives; para 37.1 — administrative, technical and physical safeguards; para 36 — outsourcing to foreign laboratories is gated on **accreditation** (Annex F) only — no data-protection condition on genetic data leaving Singapore; para 41.1 — prohibition on direct-to-consumer provision of clinical genetic tests by manufacturers/suppliers. The COP nowhere references the PDPA.

**Executive summary.** This Code is the only instrument regulating clinical genomic data at the point of generation, and its heart is para 37.3's family-inclusive consent gate: in Singapore clinical practice, a relative has no access to a patient's genetic result without the patient's (or substitute's) consent — the cascade-testing question is resolved for confidentiality, and any duty-to-warn argument must be built from the NMEC's older guidance (item 26) and general law. Its research hand-off is equally clean: clinical consent never carries a sample into a biobank — a separate HBRA consent is required. Its weaknesses are its transitional legal footing since the PHMCA repeal and its accreditation-only approach to cross-border outsourcing, which leaves genomic data exports without a data-protection condition.

## 21. Moratorium on Genetic Testing and Insurance (MOH–LIA)

**Citation/status:** *Amended and Restated Moratorium on Genetic Testing and Insurance between MOH and the Life Insurance Association* (the "2025 Moratorium"), effective **30 June 2025**; original entered into **27 January 2021** (which continues to govern policies written before 30 June 2025); amended after a 2023–2024 mid-term review, principally to protect the national Familial Hypercholesterolaemia programme launched 30 June 2025 (MOH Circulars 33/2025 and 36/2025). **A bilateral policy agreement, not legislation** — enforced as a condition of LIA membership with annual compliance reporting to LIA and MOH (para 10); open-ended, lapsing only if replaced by legislation (para 11); reviewed every three years (para 12).

**Pinpoint positions:** para 2 — insurers must not require or pressure anyone to take a genetic test, nor request residents to take predictive tests; **para 3** — results of genetic tests conducted "in the context of Biomedical Research" may **never** be used for underwriting, "regardless of where or when the research was conducted or the nature of the research", even where the para 4(b) exception would otherwise apply — the research shield underpinning Precision Medicine recruitment; para 4(a)–(b) + Table 1 — predictive results usable only above high sum-assured thresholds and only for approved tests (e.g. Huntington's for life cover above S$2m; HTT/BRCA1/BRCA2 for critical illness above S$500,000); para 5 — never for health insurance or Integrated Shield Plans, and never a **relative's** test result; para 6 — favourable results only, if volunteered; para 7 — absolute ban on using direct-to-consumer test results; para 8 — full protection for FH-programme tests (LDLR, APOB, PCSK9).

**Executive summary.** The Moratorium is Singapore's answer to genetic discrimination in insurance, done by agreement rather than statute: a research-result shield with global and perpetual reach (para 3), a near-total bar on predictive-test underwriting softened only by a narrow high-value "double key" exception, and an absolute DTC ban. Its enforcement is private (LIA membership condition, FIDReC/mediation for consumers), its versions bifurcate by policy date, and its own text contemplates eventual statutory replacement — the HIA's insurance "excluded purpose" and NEHR access bans are the first legislative steps in that direction. For genomic data sharing, para 3 is the operative promise made to every research participant: what the cohort learns, the underwriter never uses.

## 22. MOH's HIA implementation suite (2025–2026)

**Citation/status:** Administrative instruments issued ahead of HIA commencement (none yet has statutory force; the codes will presumably be re-issued or adopted under HIA s 90):

- **MOH Circular MOH-MHC-0018-2026, "Overview and Implementation of the Health Information Act" (6 March 2026)** — the batched timeline (see item 1), HIMS certification pathway (Synapxe-maintained list; NEHR connectivity certification plus CSA Cyber Essentials mark), and funding schemes (NEHR Connect Grant; predecessor Early Contribution Incentive and GP IT Enablement Grant). There is **no HIA regulatory sandbox** — only funding.
- **Cybersecurity and Data Security Essentials (CS/DS Essentials)**, MOH, March 2026 (first edition) — developed with CSA, IMDA and PDPC; applies to all HCSA licensees, all NEHR contributors/users and prescribed sharing entities; three sections (cybersecurity measures; data security measures; common practices — training, vendor management, protocols). The operational expression of HIA ss 66–70.
- **Guidelines on Appropriate Contribution, Use and Access to NEHR (for healthcare professionals)**, 13 March 2026 — role-based access, when NEHR should and should not be accessed, record quality.
- **Code of Practice for Data Portability**, version 2.0, effective February 2026 — data-migration roles, plan, items and formats (the HIMS anti-lock-in companion to HIA Part 7).
- **HIA Implementation Guide for Healthcare Providers**, version 2.0, August 2026 — 74 pages, including sample data-disclosure and confidentiality clauses and incident-response considerations; **NEHR Contribution Requirements** (July 2026); **FAQs v1.1** (13 August 2026 — contribution is prospective only).
- Lineage: **PSDSRC report (November 2019)** para 26 (see item 25) → **Healthcare Cybersecurity Essentials** (MOH Circular 105/2021, 6 August 2021 [circular number **UNVERIFIED** against a surviving MOH source]) → **Cyber & Data Security Guidelines for Healthcare Providers** (MOH Circular 85/2023, 4 December 2023 [PDFs no longer online]) → HIA Parts 4–5 + CS/DS Essentials (2026).

**Executive summary.** MOH is implementing the HIA administratively before commencing it legally: a full suite of guidance — security essentials, NEHR access guidelines, a portability code, contribution specifications and an implementation guide — was published between February and August 2026, together with a batched compliance timeline running to March 2030 and a funding-plus-certification pathway for practice software. Legally, all of it is soft until commencement; practically, it is the operative rulebook licensees are already building against, and it completes a five-year arc from the post-SingHealth PSDSRC recommendations to enforceable statutory duties.

## 23. AI in healthcare — AIHGle 2.0 and the model frameworks

**Citation/status:** *Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare Guidelines (AIHGle), Version 2.0*, **MOH and HSA, March 2026** (superseding the October 2021 first edition issued with IHiS; co-development acknowledged with Synapxe, IMDA, PDPC and professional bodies). Advisory: it "complements prevailing legislation" (cl 2.1.2). Horizontal layer: PDPC/IMDA **Model AI Governance Framework** (2nd ed, 21 January 2020); **Model AI Governance Framework for Generative AI** (AI Verify Foundation/IMDA, 30 May 2024 — nine dimensions including data); **Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI** (IMDA, 22 January 2026, updated 20 May 2026); PDPC **Advisory Guidelines on Use of Personal Data in AI Recommendation and Decision Systems** (1 March 2024 — maps AI training to the deemed-consent-by-notification, business-improvement and research bases).

**Pinpoint positions (AIHGle 2.0):** cl 2.2.2(a) — continuous-learning models risk "personal data disclosure after input data is ingested"; developers directed to personal-data-law compliance and **privacy-enhancing technologies** (ch 5.3.3); ch 6.7 + fnn 24–26 — incident routing: data breaches to PDPC, device adverse events to HSA, CII incidents to CSA under the Cybersecurity Act and CII Regulations (others to SingCERT); Table 6 — the compliance map, listing the PDPA, HCSA, HBRA, device regulations, the CS/DS Essentials (2026), both BAC instruments, and the PDPC AI guidelines — but **not** the HIA (uncommenced at publication).

**Executive summary.** Singapore governs healthcare AI by layered voluntary guidance over hard sectoral law, with no AI statute. AIHGle 2.0 is the sector instrument: non-binding but backed by HSA device registration and HCSA licensing, it treats data protection as a design constraint (PETs, anonymisation, ingestion risk) and routes incidents to three regulators. For data-sharing purposes its significance is mostly indexical — Table 6 is the closest thing to an official consolidated map of the instruments in this roundup — while the PDPC's 2024 AI guidelines answer the practical question of which PDPA bases permit training clinical models on patient data.

## 24. IMDA/PDPC Trusted Data Sharing Framework

**Citation/status:** Launched **28 June 2019**; the current document is the 2019 framework (91 pages; file build October 2020). Voluntary and expressly "not … an authoritative statement of the law". Claims of a "2024 edition" are unsupported — only the IMDA webpage carries a 2024 last-updated stamp. Accompanied by template **bilateral, multilateral and multilateral-with-lead data sharing agreements**, confidentiality-agreement and consent templates, and a data-valuation guide.

**Pinpoint structure:** Part 2 — legal/regulatory considerations (can the data be shared; constructing a data sharing agreement, with Appendix V's DSA checklist); Appendix VI — dynamic and iterative consent; Appendix VII — PDPA exemptions (pre-2021 law — now dated); Part 4.3 — secondary use; Part 4.4 — retention and disposal.

**Executive summary.** The TDSF is a playbook, not law: its enduring value for health data lies in its template data sharing agreements — the closest Singapore has to model clauses, directly reusable for hospital-industry or consortium arrangements — and its dynamic-consent appendix, which anticipates longitudinal research needs. Its PDPA analysis predates the 2021 amendments (deemed consent by notification, legitimate interests, breach notification, the restructured research exceptions), so its legal content must be checked against the current Key Concepts guidelines before reliance.

## 25. Government-internal instruments: IM8, Government Data Security Policies, HealthTech Instruction Manual, PSDSRC

**Citation/status:** The **Instruction Manual on ICT&SS Management ("IM8")** (GovTech, since 2001) is the Government's internal data/ICT rulebook — legally a PSGA s 4 direction (administrative, unpublished, binding on agencies via PSGA s 10). Its public window is the **Government Data Security Policies** extract (expressly non-definitive; confirms that the **PSDSRC's 2019 measures have been incorporated into IM8**; requires dataset-level data-security risk assessments; illustrates salted hashing for analytics-versus-operational systems). The **HealthTech Instruction Manual (HIM)**, MOH, is the public-healthcare analogue binding MOH Holdings group entities — covering security baselines, data classification, **inter-entity data sharing protocols** and PDPA obligations — likewise unpublished. The **Public Sector Data Security Review Committee Report (27 November 2019**; convened after the SingHealth breach; 336 systems across 94 agencies inspected) made five recommendations (24 initiatives, all implemented by 31 March 2024 per MDDI's July 2024 update); its healthcare paragraphs (24–26) prescribed full adoption of the measures for health policy/research/analytics data, contextualised adoption for patient-care systems, announced the HealthTech Instruction Manual, and committed MOH to sector guidelines and "legislation and licensing requirements" — the documented origin of the HIA.

**Executive summary.** A material layer of the rules governing government-held health data is simply not public: IM8 for agencies and the HealthTech Instruction Manual for the public healthcare clusters are binding but unpublished, visible only through summary extracts. What is public is the lineage: the PSDSRC report is the constitutional document of Singapore's post-SingHealth data-security settlement, and its paragraph 26 is the traceable origin of everything in item 22 — including the Health Information Act itself.

## 26. Historical and adjacent ethics/practice guidance

- **National Medical Ethics Committee (NMEC)** — non-statutory MOH advisory committee (est. 1994; "no statutory powers"). Its *Ethical Guidelines on Research Involving Human Subjects* are largely superseded by the HBRA and BAC instruments but remain notable for para 2.6: confidentiality cannot be breached without consent (2.6.1); code identifiable data at the earliest possible time on a need-to-know basis (2.6.2); **access to relatives must in principle be controlled by the subjects themselves** (2.6.3); and — uniquely in Singapore guidance — the contemplation of "a legal duty for the investigator to warn family members of genetically-based risks of severe harm" (2.6.4), a proposition no later instrument endorses or forecloses.
- **National Telemedicine Guidelines (2015)**, MOH — the operative telemedicine guidance (often misattributed to the NMEC), now supplemented by the **Joint MOH-HSA-SMC Circular No. 87/2024 (22 November 2024)** on telemedicine standards (clinical governance and advertising — notably *not* a records instrument; record-keeping stays with the General and OMS Regulations) and reg 39A on medical certificates (item 3).
- **PDPC enforcement practice** — the leading health-data decisions: *Re Singapore Health Services Pte Ltd* [2019] SGPDPC 3 (SingHealth breach; largest penalties to date) and ***Institute of Mental Health* [2025] SGPDPC 1 (21 May 2025)** — no breach of the consent obligation where a research officer used clinical information to screen and approach a patient for study recruitment (implied consent), but deemed consent by notification was unavailable because the institution's notification did not state the opt-out manner and period; best practice is express consent before using medical information for research recruitment. This is the operative authority on research-recruitment screening — exactly the ground the HIA's derived-information regime will later occupy.

# Part III — Bodies, platforms and access governance (the "Entrust" question answered)

## 27. TRUST — Trusted Research and Real world-data Utilisation and Sharing Tech

**What it is:** Singapore's national trusted research environment ("data platform + programme office + governance framework") for health and health-related data; operationalised **November 2022**; co-developed by MOH, MDDI (formerly SNDGO), GovTech and Synapxe (formerly IHiS). **Since 1 April 2026** the office has moved from MOH's Office for Healthcare Transformation to the **National Research Foundation** (hosted by A*STAR) as the **"RIE TRUST Office"**, with a mandate extended beyond health to urban solutions and sustainability — existing data-request and DAC processes expressly preserved. Site: trustplatform.sg.

**Legal basis:** **None specific — and TRUST claims none.** Users are bound by general law — the platform's FAQ names the **HBRA, Computer Misuse Act, PDPA and Official Secrets Act** — and by contract: institution-level **Data Request Agreements**, contributor-side **Data Contributor Pre-agreements**, and per-analyst **Personal Undertakings**. There is no published, versioned "TRUST Governance Framework" document; the governance is stated on the platform's web pages.

**Governance bodies:**
- **RIE Data System Steering Committee (RIE DSSC)** — strategic oversight; chaired by the Permanent Secretary (National Research & Development), Prof Tan Chorh Chuan.
- **TRUST Data Access Committee (DAC)** — reviews every data request for ethics and public benefit and validates conformity with contributor agreements; chaired by MOH Deputy Secretary Mr Philip Ong; members span healthcare, science, technology, law and ethics, with senior representatives of NUHS, NHG, NUS, Duke-NUS, A*STAR and NTU.
- **Research and Industry Data Exchange Subcommittee (RIDES), under MOH** — industry access: an industry partner may access TRUST data only in collaboration with an academic/research/public institution **and** with separate RIDES approval.

**Safeguards and process (as stated by TRUST):** the **Five Safes** framework (safe purpose/people/settings/data/output); anonymisation "to Whole-of-Government anonymisation standards", with NRIC numbers and names excluded and identifiers pseudonymised *(TRUST's own pages use "anonymised" and "pseudonymised" in adjacent sentences — do not over-claim full anonymisation, and PRECISE describes SG100K data on TRUST as pseudonymised)*; sensitive datasets accessible only in **MOH-certified Micro-Access Laboratories**; **IRB approval (or an institution-endorsed "Review Not Required" determination, accepted since 26 January 2024) is a precondition** to any data request; no download of data — analysis in-platform, with only aggregate analytical output extractable after vetting by the **TRUST Data Concierge**, and pre-publication review (21 working days); a **Charging Framework** since 1 January 2025; data is not stored perpetually — it remains with contributors and is deleted after approved analyses. Requests are consolidated monthly; DAC review takes ~4–6 weeks, provisioning 6–10 weeks post-approval. Transparency: a published register of approved projects (~92 at review). Public involvement: the OPEN Voices patient-partner collective (from August 2025) and a long-running public-attitudes study with PRECISE and NUS CBmE.

**Executive summary.** TRUST is the answer to "who governs access to Singapore's national health and research data": a controlled-access research environment whose authority is contractual and institutional rather than statutory. Its DAC (MOH-chaired) is the operative gatekeeper for research access to national health data — including, since 2024–2026, the PRECISE-SG100K genomic resource — with RIDES as a second key for industry. Its design tracks the international five-safes orthodoxy and the PDPC's closed-research-enclave guidance, and its 2026 migration to the National Research Foundation as the RIE TRUST Office signals its generalisation beyond health. The important legal observation is negative: nothing in statute compels contributions to TRUST, governs its DAC, or shields its data specifically — the HIA regulates the NEHR, not TRUST — so its accountability rests on the general statutes (HBRA, PDPA, Computer Misuse Act, Official Secrets Act) plus contract.

## 28. enTRUST — GovTech's cross-sector platform

**What it is:** **enTRUST** — "enabling Trusted Research and real-world data Utilisation and Sharing Tech" (entrust.gov.sg) — GovTech's central infrastructure and governance solution for **Public Private Data Sharing (PPDS)** projects, built by GovTech's Data Science and AI Division as the cross-sector generalisation of MOH's TRUST build ("Before there was enTRUST, there was the Ministry of Health's TRUST" — GovTech). Two components: a metadata catalogue and a secure data-sharing environment (IM-compliant data lab with exfiltration controls). It adopts the Five Safes, holds no directly identifying records, and reports 27+ partner agencies (including SingHealth, NUHS, NHG) and >135TB processed since 2024. **It is now TRUST's members' portal** (TRUST onboarding links to entrust.gov.sg/signIn).

**Executive summary.** This is the entity behind the user-remembered name "Entrust" — real, but infrastructure rather than authority: enTRUST is a platform and service, with no constituting instrument, no published governance charter, and no data-access committee of its own; access decisions remain with data contributors and, for health data, with TRUST's DAC and RIDES. Describe it as the pipes; TRUST is the gate.

## 29. PRECISE and the National Precision Medicine programme (genomic data)

**What it is:** **PRECISE** (Precision Health Research, Singapore), a programme of the Consortium for Clinical Research and Innovation, Singapore (CRIS), is the central coordinator of the **National Precision Medicine (NPM)** programme: Phase I (2017–2021) produced **SG10K_Health** (~10,000 whole genomes); Phase II (2022–2025, NRF-funded) produced **PRECISE-SG100K** (~100,000 participants, 30X whole-genome sequencing at A*STAR's Genome Institute, drawn from the HELIOS, SPHS, SERI-SEED and SingHeart cohorts); **Phase III "SG-INSIGHT" launched 14 November 2025** (MOH/NMRC-funded, MOUs with all three healthcare clusters, target ~450,000 people by 2031, Genomic Innovation Hubs, pharmacogenomics-first clinical rollout).

**Genomic data access governance:**
- **SG10K_Health** — governed by the **NPM Data Access Committee (NPM DAC)** at A*STAR's Genome Institute: qualified investigators at research-intensive organisations; a non-negotiable **Data Access Agreement**; analysis on the **RAPTOR** secure cloud platform without download; mandatory onboarding *(access mechanics partly sourced from search extracts of A*STAR pages — flagged for verification)*.
- **PRECISE-SG100K** — a four-stage stack: (1) PRECISE Management Team screening (Business Development for industry); (2) the **PRECISE-SG100K Scientific Committee** (strategic/scientific merit); (3) the **PRECISE-SG100K Data Access Committee**; and (4) the **TRUST DAC**, whose additional approval is required for linked electronic health records and individual-level data. The resource is resident on TRUST in pseudonymised form. Access is phased: a closed 2024 call selected 36 projects; the **2026 Call for Proposals** (2 January–14 February 2026) was limited to lead PIs at local publicly funded institutions with **no industry partnerships permitted**, alignment with participant consent required, and TRUST DAC applications from May 2026; **broad access via TRUST is anticipated from 2027**. No titled, published data-access policy document exists — governance is described on npm.sg, not published as a citable instrument.
- Programme oversight: **PRECISE Board Oversight Committee** (chair Mr Peter Ho) — not a data-access body; a lay **Patient and Public Involvement Advisory Panel**; a published register of approved SG100K projects.
- Legal frame: participants' genomic data sits under the HBRA (consent, ss 27–29 offences), the BAC guidelines (broad consent, whole-genome candour), the genetic-testing COP for any clinical hand-back, and the Moratorium's para 3 shield against insurance use.

**Executive summary.** Singapore's population genomics is governed by committee and contract, not by genomics legislation: SG10K access runs through an A*STAR data access committee into a no-download secure platform, and SG100K access runs through a four-stage PRECISE stack that terminates, for anything individual-level or EHR-linked, in the same TRUST DAC that governs national health data — making TRUST the effective convergence point of Singapore's health and genomic data governance from 2027. The regime is conservative (public-sector researchers only for now; industry case-by-case and excluded from the 2026 call) and transparent about projects but not about instruments: there is no published genomic data-access policy to cite, which is itself a governance finding.

## 30. NEHR, Synapxe and Healthier SG

**NEHR** — the National Electronic Health Record: owned by MOH, operated by **Synapxe** (the national HealthTech agency, renamed from IHiS on 27 July 2023), which the HIA designates as System Operator machinery (s 8) with investigation capability for unauthorised access. Statutory basis: the HIA (item 1) once commenced; until then contribution remains contractual/voluntary (existing arrangements will be deemed approved under HIA s 44). Access is care-only, role-based, rate-limited and audited; administrative staff have no access; insurers and employers "do not and will not" have access (MOH, Parliamentary answer, 7 January 2025). **There is no published NEHR research-access framework** — research access to national data routes through TRUST, and the HIA's derived-information regime (ss 24–27) will supply the statutory secondary-use channel.
**Healthier SG data sharing** — enrolment data flows among clusters, enrolled GPs, MOH agencies (HPB, AIC) and community partners; presently administrative, with HIA Part 3 and the Fourth Schedule use cases built to give it statutory footing (currently drawn to public healthcare institutions, cluster HQs, AIC and public agencies).
**Cluster IRBs/DRBs** (NHG DSRB, SingHealth CIRB, NUS/NTU IRBs) — the HBRA-mandated ethics gate that every TRUST/PRECISE request must clear first.
**Singapore–UK Health Data Bridge** — HDR UK–NRF MOU (October 2024): federated analysis across borders (data stays local), implemented by TRUST, GovTech and A*STAR, piloting Five Safes trusted research environments on cancer and diabetes.

# Part IV — Cross-cutting observations

1. **Two regimes, one flow.** A single national health-data pathway (restructured hospital → vendor → MOH → researcher) can cross the PDPA/PSGA boundary repeatedly: clusters and hospitals are PDPA "organisations"; MOH, HPB, HSA and CDA are PDPA-exempt public agencies governed by the PSGA and unpublished instruction manuals; the narrow bridges are PDPA s 26E (vendor breach notification to agencies) and the Second Schedule public-agency exceptions.
2. **Consent is being displaced by authorisation-plus-minimisation.** The HIA (ss 11, 50), IDA (s 10(4)) and MLSA (s 29(3)) override consent and confidentiality within purpose-limited channels; the NRDA is the counter-model (consent plus statutory necessity tests). The PDPC's guidance meanwhile narrows consent-free routes in the private sector (impracticability strictly construed; no medical-records-to-insurer sharing under business improvement).
3. **Anonymisation is the load-bearing wall** — and it is guidance, not statute: the "serious possibility" test, k-anonymity thresholds and motivated-intruder analysis live in PDPC advisory guidelines, while statutes increasingly criminalise the breach of the wall (PDPA s 48F; PSGA s 8; HIA ss 41–42) rather than define it. The BAC and TRUST work on the candid premise that genomic anonymity cannot be guaranteed, substituting governance (Five Safes, enclaves, output vetting) for data-state.
4. **Research access is gate-dependent, not uniform.** Private-sector records: PDPA research exceptions. Identifiable research on people or their data/tissue: HBRA + IRB (waiver via the Fifth Schedule). Registry data: NRDA s 12 (consent + necessity). National platform data: TRUST DAC (+ RIDES for industry; + PRECISE stack for genomics). Clinical trials: HSA regimes. NEHR data: no research route today; HIA derived-information regime tomorrow.

**Corrections applied during research (notable traps for the reader):**
- The statute is the **Health Information Act 2026**, not 2025 — and it is wholly uncommenced with no subsidiary legislation.
- The HBRA's **Fourth Schedule** (restricted research) concerns embryos and human-animal combinations, **not** genetic material; the consent-waiver schedule is the **Fifth**; the disclosure offences are **ss 28–29** (not s 27, which is the safeguard duty).
- The SMC ECEG confidentiality guideline is **C7** (C6 is consent).
- IDA **s 25** is HIV/STI-specific, not a general confidentiality clause; the IDA has no general secrecy provision.
- Cybersecurity Act **s 11 is repealed**; codes of practice now issue under s 35A; "CII" is now "provider-owned CII".
- The "HBR (Exemption) Regulations 2018" are spent (expired 31 October 2019).
- The PDPC "research chapter" is **Selected Topics ch 2 "Analytics and Research"**; its research-exception text covers *use* only — the disclosure limb is in the Healthcare Guidelines and Key Concepts 12.81–12.83.
- The claimed "2024 edition" of the Trusted Data Sharing Framework does not exist as a document; and the widely reported claim that 2026 PDPA regulations tightened breach-notification thresholds is false (S 86/2026 touches only transfer certifications).
