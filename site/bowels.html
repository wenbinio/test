Bowel habits

How often should you poo?

Anywhere from three times a day to three times a week counts as normal, and about 95% of people sit inside that band. What matters far more than the count is whether your stool is soft and easy to pass, and whether your own pattern has changed. This page covers the numbers, the Bristol scale, what stool colour does and does not mean, and the changes worth taking to a doctor.

The numbers, plainly

3×/day–3×/weekthe accepted normal range
~100–150 gtypical daily stool weight on a Western diet
~10–73 hwhole-gut transit time, median around 30–40 h

Those three figures do most of the work. The normal range is wide because bowel frequency is set by diet, fibre, fluid, activity, hormones and your own gut, not by a rule. Roughly 95% of healthy adults fall between three stools a day and three a week, and there is no health benefit to forcing yourself towards a daily average you do not naturally have.

Stool weight tracks fibre intake closely. Around 100–150 g a day is typical in the UK and similar diets; populations eating 50 g or more of fibre a day routinely produce 300–500 g a day, with shorter transit and softer stool. This is the clearest single explanation for why constipation is common here and rare there.

Testing your own transit time

You can measure whole-gut transit at home. Eat a marker that survives digestion — a decent portion of sweetcorn, or a food-dye method such as a couple of blue-iced items or blue food colouring in a meal — note the time, and note when it first appears and when it has fully cleared. First appearance under about 10 hours suggests fast transit; nothing showing by 72 hours suggests slow. It is crude, but it is a real measurement, and it beats guessing. If you want to see your own pattern properly, log a week in the tracker.

Consistency beats count

Going three times a week with soft, easily passed stool and no straining is normal. Going daily with hard pellets and ten minutes of effort is constipation. If you only remember one thing from this page, remember that the shape and ease matter more than the tally.

Time on the toilet

A normal bowel movement takes a couple of minutes. If you are routinely sitting for ten or fifteen minutes, that is usually the phone, not the bowel. Prolonged sitting and straining on an open toilet seat increases pressure on the anal cushions and is associated with haemorrhoids. Leave when you are done, and come back later if nothing happens.

The Bristol Stool Scale, all seven types

The Bristol scale is the standard clinical shorthand for stool form, and it is a useful proxy for transit time: types 1–2 mean stool has sat in the colon long enough for water to be reabsorbed, types 6–7 mean it moved through too fast.

Bristol Stool Scale: form, meaning and what to do
Type Description What it means Status
1 Separate hard lumps, like nuts, difficult to pass Severe constipation. Very slow transit; the colon has pulled out most of the water. Usually needs more fibre and fluid, and review if persistent. Get seen
2 Sausage-shaped but lumpy Mild constipation. Common with low fibre or dehydration; often the type people report while insisting they are "regular". Watch
3 Sausage-shaped with cracks on the surface Normal. Slightly firm but passes easily and without straining. Normal
4 Smooth and soft, like a sausage or snake Ideal. Passes with minimal effort and leaves little residue. This is the target. Normal
5 Soft blobs with clear-cut edges, passed easily Tending loose, and often a sign of not enough fibre — particularly soluble fibre, which adds form. Not diarrhoea. Watch
6 Fluffy pieces with ragged edges, mushy Mild diarrhoea. Fast transit. Occasional is unremarkable; several weeks of it is worth investigating. Watch
7 Entirely liquid, no solid pieces Diarrhoea. Watch fluid and salt replacement. Persistent, bloody or night-waking liquid stool needs assessment. Get seen

Most people vary by a type or two across a week. That is fine. What matters is where your centre of gravity sits, and whether it has moved.

The words doctors use, with thresholds

These terms have specific definitions. Using them accurately makes an appointment far more productive.

Clinical definitions of bowel symptoms and their thresholds
Term Definition and threshold
Diarrhoea Three or more loose or liquid stools in 24 hours, or more frequently than is normal for you. Timeframes: acute under 14 days, persistent 14–29 days, chronic four weeks or more. Loose form matters more than count — frequent formed stool is frequency, not diarrhoea.
Constipation (Rome IV) At least two of: straining; lumpy or hard stools (Bristol 1–2); sensation of incomplete evacuation; sensation of anorectal obstruction or blockage; manual manoeuvres to help evacuation; fewer than three spontaneous bowel movements per week. Each present in more than a quarter of movements, for at least three months, with symptoms starting at least six months before.
Tenesmus A persistent, painful feeling that you need to pass stool even when the rectum is empty, often with straining and little result. Associated with proctitis, inflammatory bowel disease and rectal masses. Persistent tenesmus should be assessed.
Faecal incontinence Involuntary loss of solid or liquid stool. Common, very under-reported, and often treatable — pelvic floor causes, sphincter injury after childbirth, and overflow from impacted constipation are all addressable. Worth raising even though it is uncomfortable to raise.
Steatorrhoea Fat in the stool: pale, bulky, greasy, foul-smelling stool that floats and is hard to flush, sometimes with an oily film. Suggests fat malabsorption — coeliac disease, pancreatic exocrine insufficiency, bile acid problems, giardiasis. Needs investigation, not a diet tweak.
Melaena Black, tarry, sticky, strongly foul-smelling stool from digested blood, indicating bleeding in the upper gastrointestinal tract. This is a medical emergency — seek urgent care the same day, particularly with dizziness, breathlessness or vomiting blood.
Haematochezia Fresh red blood passed with or on the stool, or in the toilet water. Usually lower gastrointestinal in origin. Common benign causes exist, but visible rectal bleeding is never something to simply assume away.

Colour and appearance: what it actually tells you

Normal stool is brown because bile pigments are broken down as they travel. Colour changes are usually food or transit speed. Two exceptions matter enormously: pale clay-coloured stool, and black tarry stool.

Stool colour, likely causes and what to do
Colour Likely cause Status
Mid to dark brown Normal. Bile pigment fully processed on a typical transit time. Normal
Light brown or yellow-brown Normal variant. Often faster transit, high dairy or high-fat meals. Not a concern on its own. Normal
Bright or dark green Fast transit — bile has not been fully broken down. Also leafy greens, iron supplements, and food colouring. Harmless unless it comes with persistent diarrhoea. Normal
Yellow, greasy, floating, foul Fat malabsorption (steatorrhoea). Consider coeliac disease, pancreatic exocrine insufficiency and giardia. Needs testing if it lasts more than a week or two. Watch
Pale, clay, putty or chalk-white Bile is not reaching the bowel — possible bile duct obstruction or liver disease, especially with dark urine, yellowing eyes or itching. See a doctor promptly. Get seen
Black, tarry, sticky Digested blood from the upper gut (melaena) — seek urgent care. Benign look-alikes: iron tablets, bismuth medicines, black liquorice, blueberries. Those give black stool that is not tarry, sticky or foul-smelling. If in doubt, treat it as bleeding. Get seen
Bright red Fresh blood. Often haemorrhoids or an anal fissure, but also polyps, colitis and bowel cancer. Beetroot and red food colouring can mimic it. Always get new rectal bleeding checked, particularly if you are over 40 or it persists beyond a few days. Get seen
Silver or aluminium Rare. The classic combination of pale fat-laden stool plus upper gut blood, suggesting biliary obstruction with bleeding. Seek medical assessment promptly. Get seen

Mucus

A small amount of clear or white mucus is normal — the bowel lining produces it constantly. Large amounts, mucus with blood, or a sudden new pattern of mucus with urgency can point to inflammation or infection and is worth reporting.

Floating vs sinking

Floating usually means trapped gas, which is common and harmless, especially with a high-fibre diet. Floating plus greasy, pale, foul-smelling and hard to flush points to fat malabsorption. Buoyancy alone is not a diagnosis.

Pencil-thin stool

A one-off narrow stool means nothing. Persistently pencil-thin or ribbon-like stool over several weeks, especially with a change in habit or bleeding, can indicate narrowing of the bowel and should be reported to a doctor.

Going too often

First, separate three different things. Frequency is passing formed stool more often than you used to. Diarrhoea is loose or liquid stool, three or more times in 24 hours. Urgency is the alarming sense that you have very little warning. They have different causes and different fixes, and describing which one you have is genuinely useful information for a clinician.

IBS with diarrhoea (IBS-D)

Common. Loose stool with cramping that eases after opening the bowels, bloating, and symptoms that flare with stress or particular foods. A diagnosis made positively on symptom criteria, but only after red flags and coeliac disease have been excluded.

Coeliac disease

Affects roughly 1 in 100 people and most remain undiagnosed. Loose stool, bloating, fatigue, iron deficiency, sometimes weight loss. Get tested before cutting out gluten — testing on a gluten-free diet gives false negatives.

Lactose and fructose malabsorption

Undigested sugar draws water into the bowel and gets fermented: bloating, wind, loose stool one to several hours after the trigger. Lactose intolerance is very common in adults worldwide. Fructose is the one people miss — think fruit juice, apples, pears, honey.

Bile acid malabsorption

Very common and badly underdiagnosed — a substantial share of people labelled IBS-D actually have this. The signature is urgent, watery, sometimes yellow diarrhoea shortly after eating, often worse after fatty meals, and it classically appears after gallbladder removal or ileal Crohn's. It is specifically testable and specifically treatable, so it is worth naming to your doctor.

Inflammatory bowel disease

Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. Suspect it with blood or mucus, night-time symptoms that wake you, weight loss, fever, mouth ulcers, joint pain, or raised inflammatory markers. This is not the same as IBS and should not be managed as though it were.

Microscopic colitis

Chronic watery diarrhoea with a normal-looking colonoscopy — the diagnosis is only made on biopsy. More common in older adults and in women, and associated with several common medicines. Easily missed if biopsies are not taken.

Hyperthyroidism

An overactive thyroid speeds the whole system: frequent loose stool, weight loss despite good appetite, palpitations, heat intolerance, tremor, anxiety. A simple blood test settles it.

Medicines and sweeteners

Frequent culprits: metformin, magnesium-containing supplements and antacids, antibiotics, SSRIs, and chronic laxative use. Sugar alcohols — sorbitol, xylitol, mannitol, maltitol — in sugar-free gum, sweets and drinks are a classic hidden cause. Check labels before assuming your gut has turned on you.

Infection and post-infectious IBS

Acute gastroenteritis usually settles in days. But a meaningful minority develop persistent IBS-type symptoms for months afterwards, with the gut behaving differently even though the infection has cleared. Diarrhoea lasting more than 14 days after an infection deserves stool testing rather than waiting it out.

Going too little

Before anything else: not going daily is not a problem if it is comfortable, easy and unchanged for you. Constipation is defined by difficulty and hard stool, not by missing an arbitrary daily target. That said, when constipation is real, the causes are usually mundane and fixable.

  • Not enough fibre. The recommended intake is around 30 g a day. Typical UK and US intake is roughly 15–20 g. That gap explains a very large share of constipation on its own.
  • Dehydration. Fibre without fluid can make things worse, not better — bulk with no water is harder to move.
  • Low physical activity. Movement stimulates colonic motility. Prolonged bed rest or a fully sedentary week shows up in the bowel quickly.
  • Ignoring the urge. Repeatedly overriding the call — no time, no private toilet, a job that does not allow it — lets the rectum reabsorb water and blunts the reflex over time.
  • Medicines. Opioid painkillers are the strongest offender and cause constipation reliably rather than occasionally. Also iron supplements, anticholinergics, some antidepressants, calcium channel blockers, and aluminium- or calcium-containing antacids.
  • Hypothyroidism. An underactive thyroid slows transit, alongside fatigue, cold intolerance, dry skin and weight gain. Another simple blood test.
  • Pelvic floor dyssynergia. The pelvic floor and anal sphincter contract when they should relax, so stool cannot be evacuated even when it is soft. Suspect it when there is prolonged straining, a need for manual manoeuvres, or a sense of blockage despite soft stool. Fibre does not fix this — biofeedback physiotherapy is the effective treatment, and it works well.

New constipation in an adult is different

Lifelong mild constipation is one thing. Constipation that is genuinely new, especially over the age of 50, or that comes with bleeding, weight loss, vomiting or abdominal swelling, needs a medical assessment rather than a fibre supplement.

What actually helps

Fibre, the right kind

Soluble fibre (oats, psyllium, beans, apples, linseed) forms a gel, holds water and softens hard stool — it also firms up loose stool, which is why it helps both directions. Insoluble fibre (wheat bran, skins, nuts) adds bulk and speeds transit but can aggravate bloating in IBS. Aim for 30 g a day total.

Psyllium first

Of the fibre supplements, psyllium (ispaghula) has the best evidence for both constipation and IBS. Start with a small dose, increase over two to three weeks, and take it with a full glass of water. Ramping too fast is the single most common reason people abandon fibre — the wind and bloating are a dosing problem, not a verdict.

Fluid

Fibre needs water to work. There is no magic number, but if your urine is consistently dark and your stool is consistently hard, drinking more is the cheapest intervention available.

Movement

Regular walking and general activity measurably improve constipation and transit time. It does not need to be exercise in any formal sense; consistent daily movement is what shows up in the studies.

Use the gastrocolic reflex

Eating triggers colonic contractions, strongest in the morning and after the largest meal. Give yourself an unhurried ten to twenty minutes after breakfast, at the same time each day, and sit whether or not you feel much urge. Training a consistent window works better than most supplements.

Posture: use a footstool

Raising your knees above your hips, leaning forward with elbows on knees, relaxes the puborectalis muscle and straightens the anorectal angle. Small studies show less straining and faster evacuation. A cheap footstool is one of the few genuinely effective bathroom purchases.

Do not strain, do not delay

Straining raises pressure on the anal cushions and pelvic floor, contributing to haemorrhoids and prolapse. If nothing comes in a few minutes, get up and try later. Equally, go when you feel the urge rather than postponing it — the reflex weakens when it is repeatedly overridden.

Leave the phone outside

Phone use extends toilet sitting well past the couple of minutes needed, and longer sitting with straining is associated with haemorrhoids. This is the least medical and most effective advice on the page.

Probiotics: be honest about the evidence

The evidence is mixed and highly strain-specific. Some strains show modest benefit in some conditions, particularly antibiotic-associated diarrhoea; most general supermarket products have little or no evidence for general bowel health. If you try one, give a single product four weeks and stop if nothing changes. Do not spend heavily on this before fixing fibre, fluid and routine.

What to look out for

Most bowel changes are benign and short-lived. These are the ones that should prompt a medical appointment rather than a wait-and-see approach.

  • Get seen Any unexplained change in bowel habit — looser, more frequent, or newly constipated — lasting more than three weeks.
  • Get seen Visible blood in or on the stool, or blood mixed through it. Not automatically haemorrhoids.
  • Get seen Unintentional weight loss alongside bowel symptoms.
  • Get seen Symptoms that wake you at night — diarrhoea or pain that pulls you out of sleep is not typical of IBS.
  • Get seen Iron-deficiency anaemia, or unexplained fatigue and breathlessness with bowel symptoms.
  • Get seen A first-degree relative with bowel cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, or a known hereditary syndrome such as Lynch.
  • Watch Persistent pencil-thin or ribbon-like stool, persistent mucus, or persistent unexplained bloating.

Seek urgent care

Go to an emergency department or call for urgent medical help now if you have: black tarry stool or you are vomiting blood; heavy or continuous rectal bleeding; severe abdominal pain with a rigid or swollen abdomen; no bowel movement or wind at all with vomiting and distension (possible obstruction); or diarrhoea with confusion, fainting, or signs of severe dehydration.

Two things worth stating plainly. First, bowel cancer incidence in adults under 50 has been rising steadily for several decades, so being young is not on its own a reason to dismiss persistent symptoms — and it is not a reason for a clinician to dismiss them either. Second, if you are unsure, being checked and reassured costs very little. The full list lives on the red flags page.

Common questions

Do I have to go every day?

No. Three times a day to three times a week is the normal range, and about 95% of people fall inside it. If your stool is soft, passes easily and your pattern has not changed, going every second or third day is entirely normal. The daily-is-essential idea has no basis in evidence.

Is it bad to hold it in?

Occasionally, no — everyone does it. Habitually, yes. When stool stays in the rectum, water continues to be reabsorbed and it becomes harder to pass, and repeatedly ignoring the urge blunts the reflex that tells you to go. Chronic postponement is a genuine and common contributor to constipation.

Why do I need to go right after eating?

The gastrocolic reflex. Food entering the stomach triggers contractions further down the colon, moving contents towards the rectum. It is a normal physiological reflex, strongest in the morning and after large or fatty meals, and it is exaggerated in some people with IBS. Rather than fighting it, you can use it — a consistent post-breakfast toilet window is one of the more effective constipation habits.

Why does coffee make me go?

Coffee stimulates colonic motor activity within minutes, and notably it is not purely the caffeine — decaffeinated coffee produces a similar though smaller effect, so other compounds and the release of gut hormones are involved. Around a third of people get a reliable urge from it. It is a normal response, not a sign of a sensitive gut.

Is blood always haemorrhoids?

No, and this is the assumption that causes the most delay. Haemorrhoids and anal fissures are the commonest causes of fresh red bleeding, but polyps, colitis and bowel cancer can look identical from the toilet bowl — and having haemorrhoids does not stop you also having something else. New rectal bleeding should be assessed, especially if you are over 40, if it persists beyond a few days, or if it comes with a change in bowel habit or weight loss.

How long should I be on the toilet?

A couple of minutes. If nothing has happened in about five, get up and come back when the urge returns rather than straining. Extended sitting, usually driven by a phone, combined with straining, is associated with haemorrhoids. Sitting longer does not help the bowel work.

Does floating poo mean something is wrong?

Usually not. Floating is most often trapped gas from fermentation of fibre, which is normal and can even reflect a healthy diet. The combination that matters is floating plus pale, greasy, foul-smelling, bulky and difficult to flush — that suggests fat malabsorption and is worth investigating, particularly if it comes with weight loss or fatigue.

Before you go

General information, not personal medical advice

This page describes what is typical across populations. It cannot account for your history, medicines or examination findings, and it is not a diagnosis. If something has changed and stayed changed, take it to a clinician — and take a written record with you. A fortnight of notes from the tracker is far more useful in a ten-minute appointment than trying to remember.

This site is general health information, not medical advice.

It cannot examine you, does not know your history, and is no substitute for a clinician who can. If a symptom is listed here as urgent, treat it as urgent. If something feels badly wrong and this site does not mention it, that is a reason to get seen, not a reason to wait.

Figures are drawn from mainstream clinical sources and are given as ranges. Where the evidence is weak, that is stated rather than smoothed over.
