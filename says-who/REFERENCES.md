# It's the Law (Says Who?): reference pack

Every line of the song carries one reference, and every reference is also a picture. The line says the idea in plain words, a name tag on screen says who it came from, and this file says where it comes from and what it means. It doubles as the video's description.

Citations are from memory and haven't been re-checked against the texts: verify them before publishing.

## Verse 1: the gate

| Line | Reference | Source | In plain English |
|---|---|---|---|
| No vehicles in the park | Hart's park rule | H.L.A. Hart, "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals", 71 Harv. L. Rev. 593 (1958) | A rule bans vehicles from a park. A car is obviously banned, but what about a bike, roller skates, a toy car? |
| Your core is clear, your edges grey | Core and penumbra; "open texture" | Hart (1958); *The Concept of Law* (1961), ch. 7 | Every rule has clear cases in the middle and unclear ones at the edges, where someone has to decide. |
| my skates are in the penumbra | Hart's own examples | Hart (1958) | The penumbra is the grey zone of doubtful cases. Hart listed bicycles, roller skates, toy cars and airplanes. |
| Lady Justice, please don't look away | Justitia's blindfold | Traditional iconography | Her blindfold stands for impartiality: she judges without looking at who you are. |

## Chorus 1: says the king

| Line | Reference | Source | In plain English |
|---|---|---|---|
| Says the king with a stick | Command theory | John Austin, *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832) | Law is an order from a sovereign, whom people habitually obey, backed by a threat of punishment. |
| Hobbes says a sword makes a promise true | "Covenants, without the sword, are but words" | Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan* (1651), ch. 17 | Promises between people only hold if a powerful state can enforce them. |
| but a mugger's got a sword there too | The gunman | Hart, *The Concept of Law*, ch. 2 | A robber also gives orders backed by threats. If that isn't law, law must be more than commands. |
| Not a threat we fear, but a rule we hold dear | Obliged vs obligation; the internal point of view | Hart, *The Concept of Law*, chs 5–6 | Being forced isn't the same as having a duty. People treat rules as reasons, not just as risks. |
| the rule of recognition | The rule of recognition | Hart, *The Concept of Law*, ch. 6 | Officials share a rule for telling which rules count as law in their system. |

## Verse 2: the staircase

| Line | Reference | Source | In plain English |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who says? The council… the statute… the constitution… | The hierarchy of norms | Hans Kelsen, *Pure Theory of Law* (1934; 2nd ed. 1960) | Each rule is valid because a higher rule authorised it: bylaw from statute, statute from constitution. |
| The Grundnorm. We assume it, that's all. | The basic norm | Kelsen, *Pure Theory of Law* | The chain has to stop somewhere. At the top is a basic norm that isn't made by anyone, just presupposed. |
| Rex's rules were secret, vague, and new each day, retroactive, clashing, and impossible to obey | King Rex's eight ways to fail to make law | Lon Fuller, *The Morality of Law* (1964), ch. 2 | Law fails if there are no general rules, or they're secret, retroactive, unclear, contradictory, impossible to obey, constantly changing, or ignored by the officials themselves. The song names six; the video shows all eight. |
| King Rex, please write it down! | Promulgation | Fuller (1964) | A rule nobody can read can't guide anybody. |

## Chorus 2: says conscience

| Line | Reference | Source | In plain English |
|---|---|---|---|
| Says right reason, Aquinas knew | Natural law | Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I–II, q. 90 a. 4; "right reason" is Cicero's phrase, *De Re Publica* III.33 | Law is "an ordinance of reason for the common good": it has to make moral sense to be law. |
| Antigone scattered dust by hand, the gods' unwritten law over Creon's command | Antigone | Sophocles, *Antigone* (c. 441 BCE) | Antigone buries her brother against King Creon's decree, appealing to the gods' unwritten laws. |
| From a Birmingham cell, Dr. King made the call: "An unjust law is no law at all" | King quoting Augustine | Martin Luther King Jr., *Letter from Birmingham Jail* (1963), quoting Augustine, *On Free Choice of the Will* I.5 | A law that is unjust loses its claim to be obeyed. |
| But Austin says bad law's still law, you see: what it is isn't what it ought to be | The separation of law and morals | Austin, *Province*, Lecture V: "The existence of law is one thing; its merit or demerit is another" | Legal positivists keep two questions apart: is this the law, and is it any good? |

## Verse 3: the courtroom

| Line | Reference | Source | In plain English |
|---|---|---|---|
| Holmes says think like a bad man would: predict the court, forget the good | The bad man; law as prediction | Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., "The Path of the Law", 10 Harv. L. Rev. 457 (1897) | To see law clearly, look at it like someone who only cares what the courts will actually do to them. |
| Not logic but experience | "The life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience" | Holmes, *The Common Law* (1881) | Law grows out of practice and history, not pure deduction. |
| what'd the judge eat today? | The "breakfast" jibe | A caricature of American legal realism, not a realist's own claim | Realists said judges' decisions depend on more than rules. Critics mocked that as "it depends what the judge had for breakfast". |
| Every canon's got a canon to parry | Thrust and parry | Karl Llewellyn, "Remarks on the Theory of Appellate Decision and the Rules or Canons About How Statutes Are to Be Construed", 3 Vand. L. Rev. 395 (1950) | For almost every rule of statutory interpretation, there's an opposite rule a lawyer can cite back. |
| hard cases make bad law | Legal proverb | Holmes, dissenting in *Northern Securities Co. v. United States*, 193 U.S. 197 (1904): "Great cases like hard cases make bad law" | Deciding by sympathy in one painful case can bend the rule for everyone after. |
| Hercules, please take my case! | Dworkin's ideal judge | Ronald Dworkin, "Hard Cases", 88 Harv. L. Rev. 1057 (1975) | Hercules is an imaginary judge with unlimited time and skill, who finds the best answer the whole law supports. |

## Chorus 3: says Hercules

| Line | Reference | Source | In plain English |
|---|---|---|---|
| Says Hercules, who reads it all through | Hercules | Dworkin (1975); *Taking Rights Seriously* (1977) | He reads every statute and precedent before deciding. |
| Elmer poisoned Grandpa for the gold: "You don't profit from your wrong," he was told | The murdering heir | *Riggs v. Palmer*, 115 N.Y. 506 (1889); Dworkin, "The Model of Rules" (1967) | The will said Elmer inherited, but the court refused, on the principle that no one may profit from their own wrong. Dworkin's point: law includes principles, not just rules. |
| Rights are trumps in the card game of state | Rights as trumps | Dworkin, "Rights as Trumps" (1984) | An individual right beats the government's appeal to the general good, the way a trump card beats the other suits. |
| each judge writes a chapter to keep the story straight | The chain novel; law as integrity | Dworkin, *Law's Empire* (1986) | Judges are like authors each writing the next chapter of one novel: they must continue the story so it hangs together. |

## Bridge: the philosophy funhouse
