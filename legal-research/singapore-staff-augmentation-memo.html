1. Executive summary & short answers ▲ top

Singapore has no dedicated personnel-leasing, labour-hire or temporary-agency-work statute — nothing equivalent to Germany's Arbeitnehmerüberlassungsgesetz (AÜG) or the EU Temporary Agency Work Directive (2008/104/EC). The only licensing regime that touches the supply of workers is the Employment Agencies Act 1958 (2020 Rev Ed) ("EAA"), administered by the Ministry of Manpower ("MOM"). The whole regulatory question therefore reduces to one issue — does the activity require an employment-agency ("EA") licence? — plus ordinary employer-law compliance (Employment Act, foreign-manpower rules, CPF, tax).

On the facts given, Model 2 (staff augmentation with the firm remaining the legal employer) is very likely not a licensable EA activity. The licensable act is brokering an employer–employee relationship between a jobseeker and a third-party (client) employer. In Model 2 the worker never becomes the client's employee — the firm keeps payroll, discipline and performance management — so there is no placement to license. This maps directly onto MOM's published carve-out that "Outsourcing your own staff to your customers' premises or offices" needs no licence, and onto the High Court's holding in Republic Airconditioning that a labour supplier which is itself the employer is not an "employment agency". The principal caveats are (i) substance over form — recruiting people req-by-req to supply a named client can flip the analysis into licensable placement; and (ii) a separate foreign-manpower overlay that applies only where the embedded staff are foreign work-pass holders.

2. The regulatory framework: there is no "personnel-leasing" statute ▲ top

The starting point — and it is a favourable one for the client — is that Singapore does not regulate "personnel leasing" as a distinct activity. There is no counterpart to the German AÜG, the EU Temporary Agency Work Directive, or the Australian-style labour-hire licensing schemes. Verified The instruments that regulate the workers — the Employment Act 1968, the Employment of Foreign Manpower Act 1990 ("EFMA"), and the CPF and tax legislation — do not create any "leasing" licence.

The one licensing statute that touches the supply or placement of workers is the Employment Agencies Act 1958 (2020 Rev Ed), whose long title is simply "An Act to provide for the regulation of employment agencies". Verified By way of structural contrast, when Parliament wants to regulate labour supply as such in a particular sector it legislates a bespoke statute — e.g. the Private Security Industry Act 2007 for the supply of security officers. No analogous statute exists for IT or professional staffing. Unverified (structural inference; no PSIA text pulled)

2.1 The two statutory triggers under the EAA

The EAA does not use a single phrase such as "the work of an employment agency". Two operative concepts do the work:

  1. The status/business definition (trigger for the entity-level prohibition in s 6(1)). Section 2 defines an "employment agency" as —
    "any agency or registry carried on or represented as being or intended to be carried on (whether for the purpose of gain or reward or not) for or in connection with the employment of persons in any capacity, but does not include any registry set up by an employer for the sole purpose of recruiting persons for employment on the employer's own behalf". Employment Agencies Act 1958 (2020 Rev Ed) s 2 — verbatim Verified. (The current 2020 Rev Ed reads "the employer's own behalf"; the older 2012 Rev Ed read "his own behalf".)
  2. The task list — "specified employment agency work" (trigger for individual-level registration and the s 6(2) individual prohibition). Section 2 lists four concrete tasks —

    (a) communication with any applicant for employment for the purpose of processing any application by the applicant for employment;

    (b) collation of the biodata or resume of any applicant for employment for the purpose of helping the applicant establish an employer-employee relationship;

    (c) submission of any application on behalf of any employer or applicant for employment to the Controller of Work Passes … under the Employment of Foreign Manpower Act 1990;

    (d) facilitation of the placement of any applicant for employment with an employer.

    EAA s 2 — verbatim Verified

Every one of the four limbs is keyed to an "applicant for employment" — a jobseeker — being placed into an employer–employee relationship with an employer. That is the mischief the Act targets. It is also how MOM operationalises the Act (see §3) and how the High Court read it in International Placements (see §4).

2.2 The prohibition and the penalty

s 6(1) A person must not carry on an employment agency unless the person is the holder of a licence …

s 6(2) [subject to s 6(3)] a person must not perform any work or activity in Singapore — (a) for or in connection with the employment of one or more persons in any capacity, whether or not those persons are to be employed within or outside Singapore; and (b) on the firstmentioned person's own behalf or on behalf of an employment agency which is carried on outside Singapore — unless … licensed.

s 6(3) Subsection (2) does not apply to … (c) any person who performs such work or activity … for the sole purpose of recruiting persons for employment on that person's own behalf.

s 6(4) [penalty] a fine not exceeding $80,000 or imprisonment not exceeding 2 years or both; on a second or subsequent conviction, up to $160,000 and/or 4 years.

EAA s 6 — verbatim Verified

Two "own-behalf" hooks thus point the same way: the s 2 definitional exclusion of a registry set up to recruit on one's own behalf, and the s 6(3)(c) disapplication of the individual prohibition for own-behalf recruitment. Note, however, that both are phrased around recruiting for one's own business; MOM's guidance separately and more broadly excludes outsourcing existing own staff. They point in the same direction but are not verbatim the same test. MOM guidance

2.3 The exemptions do not include a "secondment" exemption

The statutory exemptions (Employment Agencies (Exemption) Order 2011 (S 175/2011); Order 2014 (S 433/2014)) cover internet-only operation, job bulletin boards, one-off work-pass filers, short-stay (≤30 days/6 months) foreign visitors, recognised universities, Community Development Councils, e2i and the workforce-development agency — not secondment or personnel supply. Verified The favourable position for the client therefore does not rest on an exemption order; it rests on the point that outsourcing your own staff is not EA activity at all, supported by the s 2 / s 6(3)(c) own-behalf language and MOM's published guidance.

3. Question A — Scope of "personnel-leasing" law; is the firm an "employment agency"? ▲ top

Answer

(i) There is no "personnel-leasing law" in Singapore whose scope Model 2 could fall within — the concept does not exist as a regulated category. (ii) On the facts, the firm is not an "employment agency": it is not carrying on an agency/registry to place jobseekers into employment with third parties; it employs its own people and deploys them to deliver its own services. (iii) The provision of staff as such is not a regulated activity in Singapore. What is regulated is employment-agency activity — the brokering of jobseekers into employment with an employer. A labour supplier that is itself the employer is not an employment agency: Republic Airconditioning (S) Pte Ltd v Shinsung Eng Co Ltd (Singapore Branch) [2012] SGHC 46 at [32]–[33].

3.1 MOM's operational position — the decisive administrative statement

MOM's public page "Who needs to get an employment agency licence" (last updated 2 July 2026) states that a licence is required if a person: communicates with a jobseeker to process an employment-related application; collates a jobseeker's biodata/resume to help establish an employer-employee relationship; submits a work-pass application on behalf of an employer or jobseeker; or "helps place any jobseeker with an employer". Verified Critically, it then states that a licence is not required when:

"Recruiting persons for the sole purpose of employing them for your own company or business."

"Outsourcing your own staff to your customers' premises or offices."

MOM, "Who needs to get an employment agency licence" — verbatim Verified (confirmed by curl, HTTP 200, in the verification pass)

Model 2 maps squarely onto the second carve-out. The firm recruits engineers as its own employees, and deploys one or two of them into a client's team while retaining employer status (payroll, discipline, performance management). It is not "placing a jobseeker with an employer" so as to create an employer-employee relationship between the worker and the client — the worker's employer stays the firm. MOM guidance

3.2 The one judicial construction of the EAA confirms the placement focus

The only reported judicial interpretation of the EAA is International Placements (S) Pte Ltd v Public Prosecutor [2020] SGHC 46 (Hoo Sheau Peng J). Verified There, an unlicensed operator sourced foreign students, forwarded their biodata, arranged interviews and work-pass applications, and invoiced the host retailer monthly. The Court held:

  • at [51], "employment" bears a broad meaning — "an engagement or use to do something whether or not there is a contract of service" — rejecting a narrow contract-of-service reading;
  • at [52], the biodata collation fell within limb (b) and the placement facilitation within limb (d);
  • at [53], the operator "was an unlicensed employment agency in breach of s 6(1) of the EAA".

Every limb the Court applied is anchored to an "applicant for employment" — a jobseeker being placed into an employer-employee relationship with a third party. There is no judicial discussion anywhere of a firm supplying its own already-employed staff to a client while remaining employer. That fact pattern is judicially untested. Untested Verified (pinpoints confirmed; note the SLR report [2020] 4 SLR 1195 is unverified — cite by neutral citation).

3.3 Republic Airconditioning — a labour supplier that is itself the employer is not an EA

The closest authority in the client's favour is Republic Airconditioning (S) Pte Ltd v Shinsung Eng Co Ltd (Singapore Branch) [2012] SGHC 46 (Lai Siu Chiu J). Verified The plaintiff contracted to supply labour to the defendant; when sued for its charges, the defendant argued the contract was illegal because the plaintiff was carrying on an unlicensed employment agency. The Court rejected that defence:

"It had always been the plaintiff's case that it was not an employment agency as it was itself the employer of the workers. The contract itself … revealed that it was the plaintiff itself who was responsible for the welfare of the workers, including the payment of wages, individual taxation and the payment of levies; the defendant was merely charged a daily rate for the workers provided by the plaintiff. At all times, the plaintiff was and remained the employer of the workers, and thus cannot be said to be an 'employment agency'. There was accordingly, no basis on which s 6 of the Employment Agencies Act was applicable to the present case." Republic Airconditioning [2012] SGHC 46 at [33] — verbatim Verified

This is directly on point for Model 2: the badge of "employment agency" turns on whether the supplier is itself the employer bearing wages, tax and statutory contributions. Two honesty flags: the decision is a first-instance High Court judgment; the appeal (CA 9 of 2012) produced no decided judgment (likely settled or withdrawn), and no later case has revisited or cited it on this point — so the proposition is untested on appeal and unrevisited. Untested Verified (verified negative in the recency sweep)

3.4 The purposive reading is supported by the 2011 Hansard

Under the Interpretation Act 1965 s 9A, an interpretation promoting the Act's purpose is to be preferred, and extrinsic materials — including a Bill's Second Reading speech — may be considered. Verified The 2011 Second Reading speech frames EAs throughout as "intermediaries that link job seekers and employers" and describes the reform's object as "an EA industry that is better qualified, more professional and effective in matching employers and workers":

"Clause 5 clarifies that we wish to regulate all EA-related activities conducted in Singapore, regardless of whether they are for job placements in Singapore or outside Singapore … The new provisions are deliberately kept broad to ensure all types of EA-related activities that may give rise to malpractices are covered."

"I would like to assure the House that it is not the intention of my Ministry to penalise persons who make casual recommendations to link job seekers to employers."

Singapore Parliamentary Debates, Official Report (11 January 2011) vol 87 (Lee Yi Shyan, Minister of State for Trade and Industry and Manpower). Caveat: the volume (87) is only moderately corroborated and column numbers could not be located; recovered from MOM's (now-deleted) press version of the speech, not a Hansard transcript. Unverified (vol/cols)

The speech nowhere uses "temp agency", "contract staffing", "labour supply" as a legal category, or "secondment". Its focus is errant and unlicensed intermediaries who exploit jobseekers. Nothing in it extends the regime to a firm deploying its own employees under a service contract — consistent with the reading that own-staff outsourcing is outside the mischief. Market practice is consistent, though only weakly probative. 4. Q1 — What is "personnel leasing" in IT services; is Model 2 caught? ▲ top

Answer

Singapore law has no statutory concept called "personnel leasing", so the analysis proceeds by asking whether the arrangement is licensable employment-agency activity. The dividing line is not the label ("secondment" vs "contract staffing") but whether the firm performs "specified employment agency work" — sourcing/matching external jobseekers, processing their applications, filing work passes, or facilitating their placement into employment with the client. On the stated facts (own permanent employees, embedded 1–2 at a time, firm retains employer status), Model 2 is outsourcing of the firm's own staff — not licensable placement. The result would flip only if, in substance, the firm recruits people req-by-req in order to supply them to a named client.

4.1 The factors that decide the characterisation

Drawing the statutory limbs, MOM's guidance, International Placements and Republic Airconditioning together, the following factors determine whether an IT-services arrangement is (unlicensed) own-staff outsourcing or (licensable) placement:

Placement (licensable) vs own-staff outsourcing (not licensable) — the operative factors
FactorPoints to licensable placementPoints to own-staff outsourcing (Model 2)
Who ends up the employer?Worker becomes (or is intended to become) the client's employeeWorker remains the firm's employee — payroll, CPF, discipline, performance mgmt
What is being sold?A matching/placement service — finding and placing candidatesThe firm's own services/deliverables, delivered through its people
Sourcing stepFirm sources/matches external candidates against client demand (often req-by-req)Firm deploys people it already employs for its own service delivery
"Specified EA work" performed?Yes — biodata collation (limb b), work-pass filing (limb c), placement facilitation (limb d)No — none of the four limbs performed as a service to the client
Financial incidents of employmentClient bears wage/tax/levy risk after placementFirm bears wages, individual taxation, levies (cf. Republic Aircon [33])
Commercial framingFee per placement; candidate CVs sent for selectionContract for services / statement of work; firm accountable for output

4.2 Both sides of the textual argument (kept honest)

Narrow reading (no licence — the better view): every limb of "specified employment agency work" targets an "applicant for employment" being placed into an employer-employee relationship with an employer. A deployed permanent employee is not an applicant for employment; the client never becomes the employer. The own-behalf registry exclusion signals that acting as principal/employer is outside the mischief. This is reinforced by Republic Airconditioning and MOM's express carve-out.

Broad reading (licence — the counter-argument): the head definition is deliberately wide — "for or in connection with the employment of persons in any capacity", gain or not — and s 6(1) keys off that head definition, not solely the four limbs. A business whose real model is supplying manpower is arguably "in connection with the employment of persons" and could be caught notwithstanding payroll-employer status. On this view, payroll status does not by itself defeat licensing where the substance is a sourcing-and-matching business. The tension is genuine on the text alone; it is resolved in practice by MOM's operational gloss and by which side of the "own staff vs broker" line the facts fall.

4.3 Applying it to Model 2

On the facts given, Model 2 sits firmly on the "own staff / not licensable" side: the embedded people are the firm's permanent employees; the firm sells its software/product-development services (managed delivery and augmentation), not a placement service; and the firm retains every incident of employment. The correct conclusion is that Model 2 is very likely not licensable EA activity. The residual risk is entirely one of substance: if in practice the firm begins recruiting engineers specifically against a named client's requisitions, screening CVs for the client's selection, or filing work passes on the client's behalf, the arrangement will look like contract staffing/placement and a Comprehensive EA licence would be required — the label "secondment" will not save it. Untested

5. Q2 — Minimising the risks ▲ top

Answer

The risks fall into four buckets: (i) being re-characterised as an unlicensed EA; (ii) for foreign staff, breaching the EFMA prohibition on labour supply; (iii) the client (and the firm) attracting dual vicarious liability for the embedded worker's torts; and (iv) the worker being treated as the client's employee. Each can be materially mitigated by structuring and documentation. The measures below are protective, not exhaustive, and several rest on MOM administrative guidance rather than statute.

5.1 Stay clearly outside EA activity (Model 2)

  • Contract for services, not headcount. Structure engagements around deliverables / statements of work for which the firm is accountable, rather than "supplying N bodies to sit in the client's team". This tracks the Republic Airconditioning reasoning and the MOM outsourcing carve-out.
  • Recruit for the firm, not the requisition. Avoid recruiting candidates specifically to be supplied to a named client, forwarding CVs for the client's selection, or filing work passes on the client's behalf — these are the acts that constitute "specified employment agency work" and would flip the analysis.
  • Keep and document genuine employer status — the firm pays wages, CPF (for citizens/PRs), and taxes; owns discipline, performance management, promotion and termination; provides tools/benefits; and bears the financial risk of the engagement. These are the very factors the multi-factorial employment test weighs (see §5.3).
  • Do EA-directory due diligence before engaging any third-party supplier of people (relevant to Q5): s 30 EAA makes it an offence to engage an unlicensed EA, with a due-diligence defence available only by verifying the licence via MOM's EA directory (r 17). Verified

5.2 For foreign work-pass holders — satisfy MOM's three-condition deployment test

Where the embedded staff are foreign work-pass holders, a separate EFMA overlay applies. MOM's FAQ "Am I allowed to send my work pass holders to work at my client's place?" (last updated 8 April 2026) permits deployment under a genuine service contract only if all three conditions are met:

"— The work carried out corresponds to the occupation and sector stated in their work passes.

— The activity to be performed at your client's premises is the same as your company's declared business activity.

— Your company remains as the employer, and directly supervises the work of the work pass holders and pays their salaries."

"You are not allowed to deploy your work pass holders to a client's company for the sole purpose of supplementing its workforce. The supply of labour services is only allowed under certain conditions for the construction and process sectors."

MOM FAQ (8 Apr 2026) — verbatim MOM guidance. Operates under the EFMA / work-pass conditions, not the EAA; applies only to foreign work-pass holders (not to citizens/PRs).
Genuine unresolved tension — flag The third condition requires that "your company … directly supervises the work" — which is in real tension with the client's directing day-to-day work in a staff-augmentation model. For foreign staff this is a genuine interpretive risk. MOM's "not allowed" examples are all low-skill / unrelated-occupation body-shopping (cooks, cleaners, factory workers), and no MOM statement says "skilled IT augmentation = prohibited labour supply" — so the risk is an inference from the FAQ's logic, not a direct MOM position. MOM guidance Mitigations: retain a firm-side technical/reporting line (a firm lead or manager who supervises delivery quality and conduct), keep the work within the declared occupation/sector and the firm's declared business activity, and prefer the managed-services model (Model 1) or citizens/PRs for the most client-directed roles. For citizens/PRs, EFMA does not apply at all.

5.3 Vicarious liability and "who is the employer" — the litigation risk

Even with clean EA structuring, embedding a worker in a client's team creates two related common-law exposures.

(a) Employer identity — the multi-factorial test

Whether a worker is an employee (and of whom) is decided by the multi-factorial test authoritatively restated in Public Prosecutor v Jurong Country Club and another appeal [2019] SGHC 150 (See Kee Oon J):

"The multi-factorial approach under which the court looks at the totality of the parties' working relationship with reference to a non-exhaustive list of factors should continue to apply. This assessment should be done holistically …" PP v Jurong Country Club [2019] SGHC 150 at [49] — verbatim Verified (neutral citation only; the SLR report "[2019] 2 SLR 316" is unverified and likely wrong). The factor list is enumerated at National University Hospital (Singapore) Pte Ltd v Cicada Cube Pte Ltd [2017] SGHC 53 at [84], referenced at [54]; the headed factor-by-factor analysis is at [58]–[60]; the "qualitative balancing exercise" language is at [53].

Crucially, no single factor — including control — is decisive, and control is of "less significance" for skilled workers: an element of control is "generally not … inconsistent with an independent contractor relationship" (BNM v National University of Singapore [2014] SGCA 49; [2014] 4 SLR 931 at [25], applied in Jurong Country Club at [51]). Verified The court will not hesitate to depart from a contractual label that does not match reality: Jurong Country Club at [47]. Verified The factors include the parties' intentions, control, personal service, mutuality of obligations, financial risk/investment, provision of tools, remuneration structure, comparative treatment against acknowledged employees, benefits, and duration. This is protective of the firm: because the client's day-to-day direction is only one factor, the firm can hold employer status by retaining payroll, CPF/benefits, discipline, hiring/firing and financial risk. The multi-factorial test has not been re-applied to an agency-supplied / embedded worker by any reported 2020–2026 decision — a genuine silence. Untested

Older authority confirms that a seconded worker can become the host's employee "if the relevant control test is met", but not automatically: Asplenium Land Pte Ltd v Lam Chye Shing and others [2019] SGHC 41 at [62]. Verified (name corrected in verification: earlier drafts mis-styled the case) A caution on citation: Awang bin Dollah v Shun Shing Construction & Engineering Co Ltd and other appeals [1997] 2 SLR(R) 746 is often cited in this area, but its dispositive ratio is occupier's liability, not employer identity — the Court of Appeal held the plaintiff was not the defendant's employee (per Mohammed Shahid v Lim Keenly Builders Pte Ltd [2010] SGHC 142 at [21]). It may be cited for the right-of-control analysis but not as authority that a lent worker becomes the host's employee. Verified

(b) Dual vicarious liability — the client (and the firm) can both be liable

Singapore now recognises dual vicarious liability: both the general (permanent) employer and the temporary "borrower" can be vicariously liable for a single worker's tort. In Munshi Mohammad Faiz v Interpro Construction Pte Ltd and others and another appeal [2021] SGHC 26; [2021] 4 SLR 1371, Dedar Singh Gill J held it "permissible to hold multiple defendants vicariously liable for the negligence of a single tortfeasor" (at [53]) and "accept[ed] that the imposition of dual VL is permissible under Singapore law" (at [67]). Verified The general employer (who supplied an excavator operator) remained liable despite lending the worker out, because it trained and selected him and profited from hiring him out; the borrower was also liable through its site control. Result: both liable for 100% of the loss (at [86(e)]). The received Mersey Docks "pro hac vice" presumption — that the general employer is prima facie vicariously liable unless it proves a temporary transfer — was applied at [42]–[43], adopting Rix LJ's control-sharing approach in Viasystems (Tyneside) Ltd v Thermal Transfer (Northern) Ltd [2005] EWCA Civ 1151; [2006] QB 510 at [77] (as preferred in Various Claimants v Catholic Child Welfare Society [2012] UKSC 56, "Christian Brothers").

The Appellate Division refused leave to appeal, endorsing the reasoning: "the absence of direct control by the permanent employer does not mean that the permanent employer is exempt from vicarious liability" (Hwa Aik Engineering Pte Ltd v Munshi Mohammad Faiz [2021] SGHC(A) 1; [2021] 1 SLR 1288 at [22]). Verified The governing framework is the two-stage test in Ng Huat Seng v Munib Mohammad Madni [2017] SGCA 58; [2017] 2 SLR 1074 at [42] and [44]: (1) a relationship of sufficient closeness; (2) sufficient connection — the relationship created or significantly increased the risk of the tort. Verified

Two honesty flags on the reach of dual VL. First, Chen Qiangshi v Hong Fei CDY Construction Pte Ltd [2014] SGHC 177 accepted dual VL in principle (obiter, at [200]) and separately found the sub-contractor was the tortfeasor's employer (at [162]). Verified Second — and importantly — the most proximate later case, decided by the same judge, rejected hirer vicarious liability on its facts: in Nagarajan Murugesan v Grand Rich Electrical & Engineering Pte Ltd [2024] SGHC 36 at [54], Gill J applied Munshi and Ng Huat Seng but held the main contractor not liable because the operator was "merely the employee of an independent subcontractor" and the degree of control was "extremely limited". Verified (this corrects an earlier dossier claim that Nagarajan ignored Munshi — it applies and distinguishes it.) The upshot: dual VL is real but fact-sensitive; a genuinely arm's-length service arrangement with limited client control over how the work is done reduces exposure.

(c) Practical mitigations for (a) and (b)

  • Contractual allocation: indemnities and cross-indemnities between firm and client for third-party torts; require the client to maintain public-liability insurance covering embedded personnel; the firm to maintain professional-indemnity and employer's-liability/WICA cover.
  • Preserve firm-side control indicia: a firm lead who owns conduct, method standards, training and discipline; periodic firm reporting lines; firm-issued equipment where practical.
  • Workplace-safety duties can't be fully contracted away: a client/occupier may owe non-delegable safety duties for hazardous operations regardless of who formally employs the worker (cf. See Toh Siew Kee v Ho Ah Lam Ferrocement (Pte) Ltd [2013] SGCA 29 at [43]) — low salience for desk-based software work, but relevant if staff are placed in physical/industrial environments. Verified
  • Data protection: where embedded staff handle the client's personal data, put a data-intermediary/processing agreement in place; and see §9.4 on cross-border transfers (PDPA s 26).

6. Q3 — Do we need a licence? Requirements, timeline, cost ▲ top

Answer

For Model 2 as described, most likely no licence is needed (see §3–§4). The figures below are presented as a contingency — the position if the firm decides to run a placement/contract-staffing line, or takes a conservative view and licenses. The required licence would be a Comprehensive Licence (the "Select Licence" only covers workers earning > S$4,500/month, and is more suited to executive search). Realistic end-to-end timeline ~4–8 weeks. Government fees are modest (~S$500 plus per-person training/exam/registration); the security bond (S$20k–S$60k) is the main capital item; professional/legal fees are not publicly quoted.

6.1 Which licence, and who is eligible

Licence types: Comprehensive Licence (All / Local / non-FDW variants) covers any placement work; Select Licence covers only workers earning > S$4,500/month and does not cover placing foreigners in training programmes. Verified Eligibility for the licence (MOM, last updated 12 Feb 2026): the applicant must be a Singapore citizen or PR holding an ACRA-registered position (owner / MD & director / partner); the entity's ACRA principal activity must be SSIC 78103 or 78104; no undischarged bankruptcy, prior debarment, or relevant convictions. Verified MOM does not state a minimum paid-up capital (the circulating "S$50,000" figure is unverified and traced to SEO content). Unverified

6.2 Certificate of Employment Intermediaries (CEI)

For a Comprehensive Licence, key appointment holders ("KAHs") and EA personnel doing EA work must obtain a CEI (KAH tier or Basic tier) — CEI is not required for a Select Licence. KAHs must be certified before registration; other personnel before registration or within one month. Verified Course fees vary by provider and module (GST-incl): CEI (KAH) roughly S$534–S$654; CEI (Basic) roughly S$441–S$589; reduced non-FDW / no-EFMA variants are cheaper (down to ~S$250). The exam fee is S$174.40 (incl GST) per attempt (NTUC LearningHub, sole test provider). Unverified (course fees are provider-published aggregations; confirm current rates)

7. Q4 — Associated administrative tasks ▲ top

Answer

If the firm operates Model 2 unlicensed (the expected position), there are no EAA-specific administrative obligations — only ordinary employer administration (CPF for citizens/PRs, work passes and their conditions for foreign staff, IRAS filings, PDPA compliance) plus EA-directory due-diligence checks on any people-suppliers it engages. The obligations below arise only if the firm holds an EA licence.

7.1 If unlicensed (Model 2)

  • Employer administration: CPF contributions for citizen/PR employees (obligation stays with the contract-of-service employer); work passes and adherence to their conditions for foreign staff; payroll and IRAS obligations; Employment Act compliance (contracts of service, itemised payslips, key employment terms).
  • Supplier due diligence: before engaging any third party who supplies people, verify their EA licence via MOM's EA directory (the only route to the s 30 due-diligence defence). Verified
  • Foreign-staff deployment file: keep evidence that the three MOM deployment conditions are met (occupation/sector match; activity = declared business; firm remains employer/supervises/pays) — see §5.2.
  • Data protection: data-processing/intermediary agreements where staff handle client personal data; transfer-limitation compliance for any offshore element (PDPA s 26).

8. Q5 — Is "sub-subleasing" (temp-agency in, onward to clients) allowed? ▲ top

Answer

This is materially higher-risk than Model 2 and is very likely licensable. Engaging a worker via a temping agency and supplying that worker onward to the firm's own client is employment-agency activity by the middle firm: the own-behalf carve-out is lost because the worker is being placed for a third party's benefit. Two distinct exposures arise: (i) the firm itself needs a licence (s 6); and (ii) engaging an unlicensed sub-agent is a separate per-worker offence (s 30). For foreign work-pass holders, the onward-supply chain is prohibited altogether outside the construction and process sectors.

8.1 The EAA analysis — the middle firm does EA work

A firm that sources a worker from another agency and then facilitates that worker's placement with its own client is performing "specified employment agency work" — limb (d), "facilitation of the placement of any applicant for employment with an employer". The own-behalf exclusion in s 2 / s 6(3)(c) does not apply, because the worker is being on-supplied to a third-party client rather than recruited for the firm's own use. Verified (statutory analysis) Separately, s 30(1) makes it an offence to "directly or indirectly" engage or use the services of an unlicensed person "in connection with the employment of any person", with a fine of up to S$5,000 for each employee so engaged; the only defence is proof of due diligence — verifying the licence "in the prescribed manner" (i.e. via MOM's EA directory, r 17). Verified Section 31 additionally targets a licensed EA that files work-pass applications on behalf of an unlicensed sub-agent (up to the S$80,000 / 2-year tier). Verified The 2011 Second Reading speech confirms both were deliberate: the (then) new offences targeted engaging unlicensed EAs and licensed EAs "fronting" for unlicensed ones. Verified

8.2 The EFMA overlay — foreign work-pass holders cannot be on-supplied (outside construction/process)

For foreign work-pass holders, the work-pass conditions themselves prohibit the chain. Under the Employment of Foreign Manpower (Work Passes) Regulations 2012 (S 569/2012), Fourth Schedule Part IV:

"1. The employer shall control and supervise the foreign employee … the foreign employee shall be under the employer's direct employment.

2. The employer shall not permit the foreign employee to be employed by or contracted to any other person or business to do work for that person or business.

3. The employer shall not employ the foreign employee in either an occupation or a sector … different from that specified in the work permit."

EFM (Work Passes) Regs 2012 Fourth Schedule Part IV — verbatim Verified

The only sanctioned foreign-labour-supply chain is in the construction sector (and, per MOM guidance, the process sector): Part V para 6 permits an employer, with the worker's consent, to enter a supply-of-labour contract with a Controller-verified "eligible third party engaged in the construction industry". Verified This statutory recognition was noted by the Court in Republic Airconditioning at [38]–[39]. Outside that exception, on-supplying a foreign work-pass holder is illegal deployment: the client that "uses the service" is "employing" the worker within the extended EFMA s 2 definition and commits an offence under s 5(1) (fine S$5,000–S$30,000 and/or up to 12 months), and the sponsor breaches its work-pass conditions. Verified Enforcement is active — e.g. the Leong Kwai Tong matter (MOM, 15 Oct 2025: 40 months' imprisonment; shell companies and phantom workers) and the Ang Wee Ling charges (MOM, 4 Jul 2024: illegally deploying 33 foreigners). Verified (There is, however, no reported eLitigation judgment with paragraph pinpoints on illegal deployment — the record is MOM press releases and largely-unreported State Court sentencing. Unverified)

8.3 Local (citizen/PR) workers in a chain

EFMA does not apply to citizens/PRs, so there is no work-pass constraint. But the EAA is nationality-neutral: on-supplying local workers is still licensable EA activity (a "Comprehensive Licence (Local)" exists for exactly this), and s 30 applies regardless of nationality. Verified

Bottom line on Q5 Do not run a sub-supply/on-supply line without an EA licence and without EA-directory verification of every sub-supplier; and do not on-supply foreign work-pass holders at all outside construction/process. This is the one part of the firm's contemplated activity that most clearly triggers the licensing regime.

9. Q6 — Inbound cross-border leasing (offshore affiliate staff) ▲ top

Answer

Staff of the firm's EU/EEA or non-EU affiliate who work from their normal overseas locations for a Singapore client need no Singapore work pass — the work-pass regime is territorial, triggered by physical work in Singapore. Such wholly-offshore delivery also generally sits outside the EAA (no EA act is performed "in Singapore"), though the Singapore firm should note the EAA's reach where it arranges placements here. The main live issues are: business-visitor limits when the staff fly in; and tax/data flags (withholding tax, GST reverse charge, permanent-establishment risk, PDPA transfer limitation).

9.1 Work-pass regime is territorial

A work pass "is issued for the foreigner to perform work in Singapore" (MOM). A person who never physically works in Singapore needs no Singapore work pass for purely offshore remote work serving a Singapore client. MOM's remote-work FAQ (last updated 14 March 2024) is explicit: "if you are in Singapore and are working for or providing services to an overseas-based organisation or client, you do not need a work pass" — and conversely a pass is needed when the foreigner is in Singapore working for a Singapore-based entity. Verified MOM's Employer-of-Record FAQ (clarified 9 July 2024) reinforces the point from the other direction: an EOR that applies for work passes so foreigners can be based in Singapore while working for overseas companies "would be committing an offence". Verified Directionally, both confirm the affiliate-abroad model needs no Singapore pass while the worker stays offshore.

9.2 When the offshore staff fly in to Singapore

Short visits for genuine business-visitor activities (company meetings, corporate retreats, meetings with business partners; attending workshops/seminars/conferences as a participant) need no pass and no notification — but these activities "should not involve a contract of service or a contract for service with an employer in Singapore", and hands-on project work / on-the-job delivery does not qualify. Verified The Work-Pass-Exempt "specified activities" list is narrow (performers, journalists, speakers at qualifying events, etc.) and generic IT/consulting fly-in project work does not fit — it needs a proper work pass or a Miscellaneous Work Pass. Verified There is a short-stay exemption (≤30 days within 6 months, with registration) for a foreign visitor performing EA-type work, but that is EA-specific and not a general project-work permission. Verified

9.3 EAA territorial scope

The EAA prohibition bites on activity performed "in Singapore" (s 6(2)). A foreign affiliate arranging matters wholly outside Singapore performs no act in Singapore and is not caught; and job location is irrelevant to the licence question ("whether or not those persons are to be employed within or outside Singapore", s 6(2)(a)). Verified But note s 6(2)(b): a person who performs EA work in Singapore "on behalf of an employment agency which is carried on outside Singapore" needs a licence. So if the Singapore firm facilitates placement of the offshore affiliate's staff into a Singapore client (limb (d)) — especially acting for the offshore affiliate — that could be EA activity in Singapore, with potential s 30 exposure. Verified A genuine services contract with an offshore delivery team (staff remaining employed and directed by the affiliate) is generally outside EA licensing; the placement of an individual into the Singapore client's organisation looks more like EA activity. This distinction is fact-sensitive and, again, untested. Untested Unverified (analytical; no case on point)

10. Recent-law wave (2022–2026) — nothing changes the answer ▲ top

We checked the recent legislative and case-law developments. None disturbs the analysis above; several actively support it.

  • No EAA amendment 2022–2026 that affects professional/IT staff supply. The 2020 Revised Edition remains current; the only subsidiary change of note (S 442/2022) inserted FDW-specific fee-refund rules (rr 13A–13B), irrelevant here. There is no caps on fees charged to the client/employer (r 12(3)); only worker-side fees are capped. Verified
  • Platform Workers Act 2024 (Act 30 of 2024, in force 1 Jan 2025) is inapplicable: its First Schedule is a closed list (delivery and ride-hail only) and it expressly excludes contracts of service (s 5(2)). Seconded/embedded employees are under contracts of service. Verified
  • Workplace Fairness Act 2025 (Act 8 of 2025; expected in force ~2027) places anti-discrimination duties on the employer (the party under a contract of service); a contract for services and platform work are excluded (s 4(3)), and the ≥25-employee threshold applies. In a triangular arrangement, WFA duties therefore sit with the seconding firm, not the host client — as analysed in Ravi Chandran & Damien Xing, "Workplace Fairness" (2025) 37 SAcLJ 342 at paras 34–37, which notes the definition of "employer" (limb (d)) "can also include discrimination against seconded employees" where the client makes the adverse decision. Verified
  • Employer-of-Record clarifications: a worker employed by an EOR under a genuine contract of service is an employee with full Employment Act entitlements (Written Answer to PQ No. 5396, 7 Nov 2023); but an EOR that applies for Singapore work passes for foreigners working for overseas principals commits an offence (MOM EOR FAQ, 9 Jul 2024). Verified
  • Vicarious-liability line unchanged: Munshi's dual-VL holding has been neither extended nor doubted; the single Court of Appeal citation (Reed v Bellingham [2022] SGCA 60 at [47]) is generic; and the closest later case (Nagarajan, 2024) applied plain control reasoning and rejected hirer VL on its facts. Verified
  • Enforcement tenor: MOM continues to prosecute unlicensed EA activity (Ng Chai Hock, 2021), to suspend licences administratively (Nov 2024), and to route foreign-worker deployment/declaration misconduct through the EFMA (Wonderful Agency, 2026). Verified

Substance-over-form is a live judicial theme Recent cases show Singapore courts looking past labels to the real relationship — e.g. Guy Carpenter & Company Pte Ltd v Choi Okmi [2025] SGHC 241 at [42] and [64] (Mohamed Faizal JC), which found an employment arrangement designed to "mask" who the worker was really working for. That is a restraint-of-trade/conspiracy case, not a staffing-licence case, and is not a multi-factorial-test authority — but it underlines that documentation must match reality. Verified
