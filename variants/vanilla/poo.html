Section two · Pooing

Three a day to three a week, and the shape matters more than the count

Anywhere from three times a day to three times a week is normal, and about 95% of people sit inside that band. Whether your stool is soft and easy to pass matters far more than how often it arrives — and whether your own pattern has changed matters more than either.

01

The numbers, plainly

  • 3×/day – 3×/weekThe accepted normal range. Roughly 95% of healthy adults fall inside it.
  • 100–150 gTypical daily stool weight on a UK or similar diet.
  • 300–500 gDaily stool weight in populations eating 50 g or more of fibre a day.
  • 10–73 hWhole-gut transit time. Median around 30–40 hours.
  • ~30 gRecommended daily fibre. Typical UK and US intake is 15–20 g.
  • 2 minHow long a normal bowel movement takes. Ten minutes is usually the phone.

The normal range is wide because bowel frequency is set by diet, fibre, fluid, activity, hormones and your own gut — not by a rule. There is no health benefit in forcing yourself towards a daily average you do not naturally have.

Stool weight tracks fibre intake closely, and that single fact is the clearest explanation of why constipation is common here and rare elsewhere. It is also why the most effective interventions on this page are dull ones.

A measurement you can actually take

Testing your own transit time

Eat a marker that survives digestion — a decent portion of sweetcorn, or blue food colouring in a meal — note the time, then note when it first appears and when it has fully cleared. First appearance under about ten hours suggests fast transit. Nothing showing by seventy-two hours suggests slow. It is crude, but it is a real measurement and it beats guessing.

If you remember one thing

Consistency beats count

Going three times a week with soft, easily passed stool and no straining is normal. Going daily with hard pellets and ten minutes of effort is constipation. Shape and ease outrank the tally, every time.

The Bristol Stool Scale, all seven types

The Bristol scale is the standard clinical shorthand for stool form, and it is a decent proxy for transit time. Types 1 and 2 mean stool has sat in the colon long enough for water to be reabsorbed. Types 6 and 7 mean it moved through too fast. Types 3 and 4 are the target.

The words doctors use, with the thresholds

These terms have specific definitions. Using them accurately is one of the cheapest ways to make an appointment work better.

Colour and appearance: what it actually tells you

Normal stool is brown because bile pigments are broken down as they travel. Colour changes are usually food or transit speed. Two exceptions matter enormously: pale clay-coloured stool, and black tarry stool.

Mucus

A small amount of clear or white mucus is normal — the bowel lining produces it constantly. Large amounts, mucus with blood, or a sudden new pattern of mucus with urgency can point to inflammation or infection, and is worth reporting.

Floating versus sinking

Floating usually means trapped gas, which is common and harmless, and often just reflects a high-fibre diet. Floating plus greasy, pale, foul-smelling and hard to flush is the combination that points at fat malabsorption. Buoyancy on its own is not a diagnosis.

Pencil-thin stool

A one-off narrow stool means nothing. Persistently pencil-thin or ribbon-like stool over several weeks, especially with a change in habit or with bleeding, can indicate narrowing of the bowel and should be reported.

05

Going too often

First, separate three different things, because they have different causes and different fixes. Frequency is passing formed stool more often than you used to. Diarrhoea is loose or liquid stool, three or more times in 24 hours. Urgency is the alarming sense that you have very little warning. Telling a clinician which one you have is genuinely useful information.

Irritable bowel syndrome with diarrhoea

Common. Loose stool with cramping that eases after opening the bowels, bloating, and symptoms that flare with stress or particular foods. It is a diagnosis made positively on symptom criteria — but only after red flags and coeliac disease have been excluded.

Coeliac disease

Affects roughly one in a hundred people, and most remain undiagnosed. Loose stool, bloating, fatigue, iron deficiency, sometimes weight loss. Get tested before cutting out gluten: testing on a gluten-free diet gives false negatives.

Lactose and fructose malabsorption

Undigested sugar draws water into the bowel and gets fermented: bloating, wind and loose stool one to several hours after the trigger. Lactose intolerance is very common in adults worldwide. Fructose is the one people miss — fruit juice, apples, pears, honey.

Bile acid malabsorption

Very common and badly underdiagnosed; a substantial share of people labelled with IBS-D actually have this. The signature is urgent, watery, sometimes yellow diarrhoea shortly after eating, often worse after fatty meals, and it classically appears after gallbladder removal or ileal Crohn's. It is specifically testable and specifically treatable, which is why it is worth naming to your doctor by name.

Inflammatory bowel disease

Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. Suspect it with blood or mucus, symptoms that wake you at night, weight loss, fever, mouth ulcers, joint pain or raised inflammatory markers. This is not the same thing as IBS and should not be managed as though it were.

Microscopic colitis

Chronic watery diarrhoea with a normal-looking colonoscopy — the diagnosis is only made on biopsy. Commoner in older adults and in women, and associated with several widely used medicines. Easily missed if biopsies are not taken.

Hyperthyroidism

An overactive thyroid speeds the whole system: frequent loose stool, weight loss despite a good appetite, palpitations, heat intolerance, tremor, anxiety. A simple blood test settles it.

Medicines and sweeteners

Frequent culprits: metformin, magnesium-containing supplements and antacids, antibiotics, SSRIs, and chronic laxative use. Sugar alcohols — sorbitol, xylitol, mannitol, maltitol — in sugar-free gum, sweets and drinks are a classic hidden cause. Check the labels before assuming your gut has turned on you.

Infection, and what comes after it

Acute gastroenteritis usually settles within days. But a meaningful minority develop persistent IBS-type symptoms for months afterwards, with the gut behaving differently even though the infection has cleared. Diarrhoea lasting more than fourteen days after an infection deserves stool testing rather than waiting it out.

06

Going too little

Before anything else: not going daily is not a problem if it is comfortable, easy and unchanged for you. Constipation is defined by difficulty and hard stool, not by missing an arbitrary daily target. When it is real, though, the causes are usually mundane and fixable.

  • Not enough fibre. The recommendation is around 30 g a day; typical UK and US intake is roughly 15–20 g. That gap explains a very large share of constipation on its own.
  • Dehydration. Fibre without fluid can make things worse rather than better — bulk with no water is harder to move.
  • Low physical activity. Movement stimulates colonic motility. Prolonged bed rest or a fully sedentary week shows up in the bowel quickly.
  • Ignoring the urge. Repeatedly overriding the call — no time, no private toilet, a job that does not allow it — lets the rectum reabsorb water and blunts the reflex over time.
  • Medicines. Opioid painkillers are the strongest offender and cause constipation reliably rather than occasionally. Also iron supplements, anticholinergics, some antidepressants, calcium channel blockers, and aluminium- or calcium-containing antacids.
  • Hypothyroidism. An underactive thyroid slows transit, alongside fatigue, cold intolerance, dry skin and weight gain. Another simple blood test.
  • Pelvic floor dyssynergia. The pelvic floor and anal sphincter contract when they should relax, so stool cannot be evacuated even when it is soft. Suspect it with prolonged straining, a need for manual manoeuvres, or a sense of blockage despite soft stool. Fibre does not fix this — biofeedback physiotherapy is the effective treatment, and it works well.
The distinction that matters

New constipation in an adult is a different thing

Lifelong mild constipation is one thing. Constipation that is genuinely new — especially over the age of 50, or accompanied by bleeding, weight loss, vomiting or abdominal swelling — needs a medical assessment rather than a fibre supplement.

07

What actually helps

Fibre, and the right kind of it

Soluble fibre — oats, psyllium, beans, apples, linseed — forms a gel, holds water and softens hard stool. It also firms up loose stool, which is why it helps in both directions. Insoluble fibre — wheat bran, skins, nuts — adds bulk and speeds transit, but can aggravate bloating in IBS. Aim for 30 g a day in total.

Psyllium first

Of the fibre supplements, psyllium (ispaghula) has the best evidence for both constipation and IBS. Start small, increase over two to three weeks, and take it with a full glass of water. Ramping too fast is the single commonest reason people abandon fibre — the wind and bloating are a dosing problem, not a verdict.

Use the gastrocolic reflex

Eating triggers colonic contractions, strongest in the morning and after the largest meal. Give yourself an unhurried ten to twenty minutes after breakfast, at the same time each day, and sit whether or not you feel much urge. Training a consistent window works better than most supplements.

Use a footstool

Raising your knees above your hips and leaning forward with your elbows on your knees relaxes the puborectalis muscle and straightens the anorectal angle. Small studies show less straining and faster evacuation. A cheap footstool is one of the very few genuinely effective bathroom purchases.

Move

Regular walking and general activity measurably improve constipation and transit time. It does not need to be exercise in any formal sense — consistent daily movement is what shows up in the studies.

Do not strain, do not delay, and leave the phone outside

Straining raises pressure on the anal cushions and pelvic floor and contributes to haemorrhoids and prolapse. If nothing comes in a few minutes, get up and come back later. Equally, go when you feel the urge rather than postponing it — the reflex weakens when it is repeatedly overridden. Phone use extends toilet sitting well past the couple of minutes needed, and longer sitting combined with straining is associated with haemorrhoids. This is the least medical and most effective advice on the page.

Being honest about the evidence

Probiotics

Mixed, and highly strain-specific. Some strains show modest benefit in some conditions, particularly antibiotic-associated diarrhoea. Most general supermarket products have little or no evidence for general bowel health. If you want to try one, give a single product four weeks and stop if nothing changes — and do not spend heavily here before fixing fibre, fluid and routine.

08

What to look out for

Most bowel changes are benign and short-lived. These are the ones that should prompt an appointment rather than a wait-and-see.

  • Get seen Any unexplained change in bowel habit — looser, more frequent, or newly constipated — lasting more than three weeks.
  • Get seen Visible blood in or on the stool, or blood mixed through it. Not automatically haemorrhoids.
  • Get seen Unintentional weight loss alongside bowel symptoms.
  • Get seen Symptoms that wake you at night. Diarrhoea or pain that pulls you out of sleep is not typical of IBS.
  • Get seen Iron-deficiency anaemia, or unexplained fatigue and breathlessness with bowel symptoms.
  • Get seen A first-degree relative with bowel cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, or a known hereditary syndrome such as Lynch.
  • Watch Persistent pencil-thin or ribbon-like stool, persistent mucus, or persistent unexplained bloating.
Go now

Seek urgent care today

Go to an emergency department, or call for urgent medical help, if you have:

  • Black, tarry stool, or you are vomiting blood or something that looks like coffee grounds.
  • Heavy or continuous rectal bleeding, or any rectal bleeding with dizziness, fainting, breathlessness or a racing heart.
  • Severe abdominal pain with a rigid, board-like or exquisitely tender abdomen.
  • No bowel movement and no wind at all, with vomiting and a swollen abdomen — this pattern suggests obstruction.
  • Diarrhoea with confusion, fainting, or signs of severe dehydration.
  • New loss of bladder or bowel control with numbness around the genitals, buttocks or inner thighs — see the cauda equina section.

Two things worth stating plainly. Bowel cancer incidence in adults under 50 has been rising steadily for decades, so being young is not on its own a reason to dismiss persistent symptoms — and it is not a reason for a clinician to dismiss them either. And if you are unsure, being checked and reassured costs very little. The full list lives on the warning signs page.

09

Questions people actually ask

Do I have to go every day?

No. Three times a day to three times a week is the normal range, and about 95% of people fall inside it. If your stool is soft, passes easily and your pattern has not changed, going every second or third day is entirely normal. The daily-is-essential idea has no basis in evidence.

Is it bad to hold it in?

Occasionally, no — everyone does it. Habitually, yes. When stool stays in the rectum, water continues to be reabsorbed and it becomes harder to pass; and repeatedly ignoring the urge blunts the reflex that tells you to go. Chronic postponement is a genuine and common contributor to constipation.

Why do I need to go right after eating?

The gastrocolic reflex. Food entering the stomach triggers contractions further down the colon, moving contents towards the rectum. It is normal physiology, strongest in the morning and after large or fatty meals, and it is exaggerated in some people with IBS. Rather than fighting it, use it: a consistent post-breakfast toilet window is one of the more effective constipation habits.

Why does coffee make me go?

Coffee stimulates colonic motor activity within minutes — and notably it is not purely the caffeine, since decaffeinated coffee produces a similar though smaller effect. Other compounds and the release of gut hormones are involved. Around a third of people get a reliable urge from it. It is a normal response, not a sign of a sensitive gut.

Is blood always haemorrhoids?

No, and this is the assumption that causes the most delay. Haemorrhoids and anal fissures are the commonest causes of fresh red bleeding, but polyps, colitis and bowel cancer can look identical from the toilet bowl — and having haemorrhoids does not stop you also having something else. New rectal bleeding should be assessed, especially if you are over 40, if it persists beyond a few days, or if it comes with a change in bowel habit or weight loss.

How long should I be on the toilet?

A couple of minutes. If nothing has happened in about five, get up and come back when the urge returns rather than straining. Extended sitting — usually driven by a phone — combined with straining is associated with haemorrhoids. Sitting longer does not help the bowel work.

Does floating poo mean something is wrong?

Usually not. Floating is most often trapped gas from the fermentation of fibre, which is normal and can even reflect a good diet. The combination that matters is floating plus pale, greasy, foul-smelling, bulky and difficult to flush — that suggests fat malabsorption and is worth investigating, particularly alongside weight loss or fatigue.

This is information, not medical advice

If something feels badly wrong to you and this site does not mention it, that is a reason to be seen, not a reason to wait. Nothing here is treatment advice and no page gives drug doses.
