Section three · Warning signs

Go now, book within days, or almost certainly nothing

Most changes in how you pee and poo are benign, common and short-lived. This page is about the minority that are not. It is organised into three lists in descending order of urgency, and the honest summary is this: visible blood, and symptoms that persist for weeks rather than days, are the two patterns worth getting seen for.

01

Go now

Call 999, or your local emergency number, or go straight to A&E

Do not wait to see whether these settle

Do not wait for a GP appointment. Do not drive yourself if you feel faint or are bleeding heavily — call for an ambulance or have someone take you.

Urinary emergencies

  • You cannot pass urine at all and your bladder feels painfully full. This is acute urinary retention. The bladder needs draining urgently.
  • No urine at all for 12 hours or more, whether or not it is painful.
  • Fever with shaking chills or rigors, plus pain in the flank or back — the side or lower back, under the ribs. This can be a kidney infection tipping into urosepsis.
  • Confusion, new drowsiness or a sudden change in behaviour in an older adult who also has urinary symptoms. In older people, infection often shows up as confusion before anything else.
  • Visible blood in the urine with clots, and difficulty or inability to pass urine.
  • Sudden testicular pain, with or without swelling, nausea or abdominal pain. Testicular torsion is time-critical — the window to save the testicle is measured in hours.
  • Severe flank pain radiating to the groin, with vomiting, especially if it comes in waves. This can be an obstructing kidney stone; with fever it becomes an emergency of a different order.
  • Any urinary symptoms with signs of sepsis: very high or very low temperature, shivering, fast breathing, fast heart rate, mottled, pale or blueish skin, not passing urine, or a rash that does not fade when pressed under a glass.

Bowel and gut emergencies

  • Black, tarry, sticky stool (melaena), or vomiting blood or something that looks like coffee grounds. This suggests bleeding higher up the gut.
  • Heavy fresh rectal bleeding — filling the toilet bowl, soaking through, or simply continuing — or any rectal bleeding with dizziness, light-headedness, fainting, breathlessness or a racing heart.
  • Severe abdominal pain with a rigid, board-like or exquisitely tender abdomen, especially if you cannot bear to be moved or touched.
  • Complete inability to pass stool or wind, with vomiting and a swollen, distended abdomen. This pattern suggests bowel obstruction.
  • Sudden severe abdominal pain followed by rectal bleeding in an older adult. This can be ischaemic colitis, where a segment of bowel loses its blood supply.

Two more that need the same day

  • Dehydration with persistent vomiting and diarrhoea in a baby, a young child, a frail older person, or anyone immunosuppressed. Warning signs include very few wet nappies or no urine for many hours, a dry mouth, no tears when crying, sunken eyes, floppiness, drowsiness or difficulty rousing.
  • Diarrhoea after recent antibiotics, with severe abdominal pain and fever. This can be Clostridioides difficile infection and needs assessment and testing, not waiting it out.

If you are not sure whether it is an emergency

Being unsure is itself a reason to make contact rather than to wait. In the UK, NHS 111 will triage by phone or online at any hour and can direct you to the right place. Elsewhere, use your local urgent-care line. Nobody is annoyed with you for coming in and being fine.

02

Cauda equina syndrome

A surgical emergency — the same hour, not the same week

Say the words “I think this might be cauda equina” when you arrive

Compression of the nerves at the base of the spine can cause permanent loss of bladder, bowel and sexual function if it is not decompressed quickly. The window is measured in hours.

Go to A&E if you have a new inability to pass urine, or new loss of bladder or bowel control, together with any of the following:

  • Numbness or altered sensation around the saddle area — the genitals, the inner thighs, the buttocks or the anus. This includes not feeling the toilet paper, and not feeling urine or stool as it passes.
  • New weakness, heaviness or numbness in one or both legs.
  • New severe back pain, or back pain with any of the above.

Do not sleep on it. Do not wait for a physiotherapy appointment. Do not accept being told to come back in a week.

03

Book an appointment — days, not months

These do not need an ambulance. They do need a clinician, soon, because the conditions they can point to are far more treatable when found early.

Booking is the whole job. You do not have to work out what it is, and you do not have to be certain it is serious. Ask for the earliest appointment available and say plainly which symptom is worrying you — receptionists in UK practices triage by symptom, and phrases like “blood in my urine” or “a change in bowel habit for over three weeks” are recognised immediately.

“I'm too young for this” is not reassurance

Rates of early-onset colorectal cancer — in people under 50 — have been rising for decades across many countries. Being in your twenties or thirties does not take bowel symptoms off the table, and younger patients are diagnosed later on average, partly because both they and their clinicians attribute symptoms to something else for months.

And haemorrhoids are extremely common, which means plenty of people with something else also have haemorrhoids. Finding piles does not exclude anything, and “it's probably just piles” without an examination is not a diagnosis. If bleeding continues, go back.

04

What it is far more likely to be

Red-flag lists distort your sense of the odds, so here is the other side, honestly. For almost everyone reading a page like this, the explanation is on this list rather than the ones above. None of this cancels those sections — it sits alongside them. Use it to stop scanning symptom lists at two in the morning, not to talk yourself out of an appointment you already know you need.

Gastroenteritis

Sudden diarrhoea, with or without vomiting, peaking within a day or two and settling within about a week. Extremely common — most adults get one or more episodes a year, and viral causes dominate. It needs fluids and time, not investigation, unless there is blood, high fever, severe pain, or it drags past a week.

Irritable bowel syndrome

Affects somewhere between 4% and 10% of people, depending on which diagnostic criteria are used. Pain linked to opening the bowels, alternating loose and hard stools, bloating that varies through the day, symptoms worse under stress and better on holiday. Real, physiological and manageable — but it is a diagnosis made after red flags are considered, not before.

Haemorrhoids and anal fissures

The commonest cause of fresh red blood on the paper or on the surface of the stool; roughly half of adults will have symptomatic haemorrhoids at some point. A fissure typically brings sharp, tearing pain during and after passing stool. Both are treatable, and both still deserve a look rather than an assumption.

Simple urinary tract infection

Burning, frequency, urgency and cloudy urine. Very common in women, around half of whom have at least one in their lifetime. Straightforward cases are diagnosed and treated in primary care. It becomes a different conversation with fever and flank pain, if you are male or pregnant, or if it keeps coming back.

Benign prostatic hyperplasia

The prostate enlarges with age in most men; by the seventies, the majority have some degree of it. Weaker stream, hesitancy, dribbling and night-time trips are the usual pattern. Common does not mean ignorable — the symptoms overlap with things that are not benign.

Diet, medicines and supplements

Fibre changes, a sudden swing in coffee or alcohol, sweeteners such as sorbitol and xylitol, lactose, a large dose of fructose, spicy food, or beetroot turning urine and stool alarmingly red. Iron tablets darken stool; bismuth darkens it too. Opioids, some antidepressants and some blood-pressure drugs constipate. Metformin, magnesium and antibiotics loosen. Diuretics increase urine volume. Check the timing against any new prescription before assuming the worst.

Anxiety and the gut–brain axis

The gut is densely innervated and genuinely responsive to stress. Urgency before an exam, an interview or a flight is normal physiology, not weakness. Anxiety also amplifies attention to sensations you would otherwise not notice — which is why symptoms often seem to worsen the more closely you monitor them.

Life, generally

Travel, shift work, a new job, pregnancy, the menopause, a change in exercise, a period of poor sleep, a course of antibiotics, or simply drinking less water in winter. Bowel and bladder habits track your life closely. A change that arrives with an obvious life change and resolves with it is usually exactly what it looks like.

05

A rough rule for sorting yourself

Days of symptoms, with an obvious trigger and a clear trajectory towards better: watch, hydrate, and read peeing or pooing.

Weeks of symptoms with no explanation, or any visible blood, or weight coming off without trying: book.

Anything in the “go now” list: go now.

That is the whole triage. If you want it applied to your particular situation, with the answers written down for the appointment, use the note builder.

06

How to describe it well

Clinicians work from the story you tell them. Ten minutes of preparation genuinely changes what happens in a ten-minute appointment: it converts “things have been a bit off, I think” into a timeline that can be acted on. You do not need to be precise or scientific. You need to be specific.

What to record before you go

  • Duration. The date, or at least the week, it started. “Since the start of June” beats “a while”.
  • Frequency. How many times a day or night, now versus your normal. Your normal is the comparison that matters.
  • Stool form. Use Bristol types 1–7. It is the shared vocabulary, and “mostly type 6 for a month” is far more useful than “loose”.
  • Blood. Colour (bright red, dark red, black and tarry), amount (streaks, a teaspoon, filling the bowl), where it appeared (on the paper only, coating the stool, mixed through it, in the water, in urine), and — for urine — whether it came at the start or the end of the stream.
  • Pain. Where, what kind, whether it relates to eating, to opening your bowels or to passing urine, and whether it wakes you.
  • Fevers, sweats and chills — and any temperatures you actually measured.
  • Weight. Any change, over what period, intentional or not. A number from the scales is worth a lot.
  • Everything you take. Prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, supplements, iron, laxatives, herbal remedies, protein powders. Photograph the boxes or bring them.
  • Family history. Bowel, ovarian, bladder, kidney or prostate cancer; inflammatory bowel disease; coeliac disease — and at what age relatives were affected.
  • Travel and food. Where you have been in the past few months, and anything unusual you ate or drank.
  • Recent antibiotics or a recent hospital stay.

The note builder collects exactly these fields and prints a one-page summary you can hand over. That is easier than reconstructing six weeks from memory in a waiting room — and easier than saying some of it out loud.

Questions worth asking the clinician

  • What do you think is most likely, and what are you ruling out?
  • What would change your mind — what should bring me back?
  • Which tests are you doing, and when and how will I get the results?
  • If this test is normal, does that settle it, or is there a next step?
  • Is this urgent enough for a fast-track referral, and if not, what would make it so?
  • Is there anything I should stop, start or change while we wait?
  • Can you write down the name of what you think this is, so I can read about it properly?
07

The usual tests, and why none of them are frightening

Most of what follows is a sample in a pot, or a needle in the arm.

  • Urine dipstick and culture. You pee in a container. The dipstick reads in a minute; the culture identifies an organism and which treatments it responds to.
  • FIT (faecal immunochemical test). A tiny stool sample you collect at home and post or hand in. It detects blood invisible to the eye, and it is the main first-line test for bowel symptoms in UK primary care.
  • Faecal calprotectin. Another stool sample, measuring inflammation. It helps separate inflammatory bowel disease from IBS.
  • Blood tests. Full blood count (anaemia), ferritin (iron stores), CRP (inflammation), coeliac serology (do not cut out gluten before this or it can come back falsely normal), HbA1c (diabetes), and kidney and liver function.
  • A PSA discussion. For men with urinary symptoms or a family history, a blood test that is genuinely a discussion first — it has real benefits and real false positives, and you are entitled to hear about both.
  • Ultrasound. Painless, no radiation, gel and a probe. Used for kidneys, bladder emptying and abdominal or pelvic organs.
  • Colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy. Offered when a red flag or an abnormal FIT makes it worth looking directly at the bowel lining. Sedation is standard, the preparation is the annoying part, and polyps can be removed in the same sitting.
  • Cystoscopy. The equivalent for the bladder, and the standard next step after visible blood in the urine. Usually done awake with local anaesthetic gel, and it takes a few minutes.
08

When the thresholds shift

The advice above assumes a general adult. If you are in one of these groups, err further towards being seen, and sooner.

Pregnancy

Urinary infections in pregnancy are treated even when they cause no symptoms, because untreated they carry risk to both mother and baby. Any burning, frequency beyond the usual pregnancy increase, or an abnormal urine test needs same-day contact with a midwife or GP. Constipation and haemorrhoids are very common and worth raising rather than enduring. Any rectal or urinary bleeding, fever with flank pain, or reduced urine output should be assessed promptly.

Babies and children

Track nappies: fewer wet nappies than usual, or none for several hours, is the main dehydration signal, along with a dry mouth, no tears, sunken eyes, unusual floppiness or drowsiness. Any visible blood in a baby's stool needs medical assessment — do not wait it out. Stool that looks like redcurrant jelly, especially with episodes of drawing the legs up, inconsolable screaming and pallor, suggests intussusception and is an emergency. Fever in a baby under three months is always urgent, whatever else is going on.

Adults over 65

Presentation is often atypical: infection may show as confusion, falls, drowsiness or simply “not themselves”, with no fever and no burning. New constipation, new incontinence or a change in bowel habit carries more weight at this age, not less. Ischaemic colitis and bowel obstruction are both more likely. And polypharmacy matters — a new bladder or bowel symptom is very often a new tablet.

Spinal cord injury and neurological conditions

With a spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, spina bifida or something similar, you may not feel a full bladder, a loaded bowel or the pain that normally signals trouble — so the absence of pain is not reassurance. Watch instead for changes in your usual routine, cloudy or strongly smelling urine, or increased spasticity. Above a T6 injury, watch for autonomic dysreflexia: a pounding headache, sweating and flushing above the level of injury, with a sudden rise in blood pressure. That is an emergency, and a blocked catheter or a loaded bowel is a common trigger.

Immunosuppression and chemotherapy

On chemotherapy, high-dose steroids, biologics or immunosuppressants after a transplant, the ordinary rules do not apply. A fever may be a medical emergency in its own right — follow the neutropenic sepsis instructions and the card your team gave you, and call the number on it rather than waiting for a GP appointment. Report diarrhoea, blood or urinary symptoms to your team early.

Recent foreign travel

Mention travel unprompted, including countries and dates, and mention it again if the clinician does not ask. Diarrhoea beginning during or shortly after travel opens up parasites and organisms that need specific stool tests. Fever after travel to a malaria area is urgent regardless of bowel symptoms. Symptoms persisting for weeks after a trip warrant testing rather than more waiting.

Men and women: different defaults

A urinary tract infection in a man is treated as complicated by default, and prompts a look at the prostate and at bladder emptying. Men are also more likely to delay presenting, and unexplained iron deficiency in a man is investigated by default. In women, urinary and bowel symptoms overlap heavily with gynaecological ones — persistent bloating, early satiety, pelvic pain and urinary urgency together are an ovarian-cancer pattern worth naming explicitly at the appointment. Post-menopausal bleeding of any kind, from anywhere, needs assessment.

Anyone with a long-term condition

Diabetes, kidney disease, inflammatory bowel disease, heart failure and liver disease all change how these symptoms behave and how quickly they should be assessed. If you have an existing condition and a specialist team, use them — they usually have a nurse line that answers faster than a general appointment, and they know your baseline.

09

Questions people actually have

I saw blood once and it stopped. Do I still need to go?

Yes. This is the commonest way serious things get missed, and it applies to blood in the urine and blood from the back passage alike. Bleeding from a significant cause is very often intermittent — it stops on its own, you feel relieved, and months pass. A single episode of visible blood in the urine is enough on its own to warrant investigation, even if it was painless and never returned. Go, describe exactly what you saw, and let the tests be normal.

Can I just do a home test instead?

Home urine dipsticks and pharmacy tests have a place — they can support a straightforward suspected urinary infection in a non-pregnant woman, and some services use them to speed things along. But they cannot rule things out. A dipstick does not exclude bladder cancer, and a negative test with continuing symptoms is a reason to be seen, not a reason to relax. The tests that matter most for bowel symptoms — FIT, calprotectin, coeliac serology, a full blood count — need to go through a clinician anyway, because the point is not the number but what is done with it.

How do I bring this up without dying of embarrassment?

Lead with the sentence, before your nerve goes: “I've had blood when I open my bowels for three weeks.” Or write it on a piece of paper and hand it over — clinicians take that entirely in their stride. It helps to know that a GP hears about bowels, blood, discharge and incontinence every single working day; you are not the interesting one, and there is no version of this they have not already seen this week. You can ask for a clinician of a particular gender, ask for a chaperone, or bring someone with you. And a printed summary lets the awkward details be read rather than spoken.

Will they definitely do a colonoscopy?

No — most people with bowel symptoms never have one. The usual path is a conversation, an examination, and stool and blood tests. A colonoscopy is offered when those results, your age or specific red flags make it worth looking directly at the bowel lining. If it is offered, it is because it is the test that answers the question, and it can treat as well as diagnose by removing polyps on the spot. The preparation is the part people dislike; the procedure itself is done with sedation and most people remember little of it.

Is it worth going if my symptoms come and go?

Yes, and intermittent symptoms are exactly where a written record earns its keep. Bring the pattern rather than the current moment: how often, how long each episode lasts, what precedes it, whether it is becoming more frequent. “It's fine today” is not evidence that it is fine. Plenty of significant conditions relapse and remit, and a clinician can work perfectly well from a good history of something that is not happening in the room.

I've been told it's IBS, but something has changed.

Go back. An IBS diagnosis is not a lifetime shield, and it does not protect you from developing something else — people with IBS get inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease and cancer at the ordinary rates. What matters is change: new blood, weight loss without trying, symptoms that now wake you at night, a shift in your pattern lasting weeks, or a new anaemia. Any of those means the diagnosis needs revisiting rather than the symptoms being filed under a label you already have. Say plainly: “I have an IBS diagnosis, but this is different from my usual pattern, and here's how.”

I went, they said it was nothing, and it hasn't gone away.

Go back, and say that it has not resolved. Reassessment after a failed course of treatment or an unresolved symptom is normal medicine, not a complaint about the last clinician. Bring the timeline of what has happened since, ask what else is on the differential, and ask directly what would need to be true for further investigation to be appropriate. You are also entitled to ask for a second opinion.

This is information, not medical advice

OUTPUT cannot examine you, does not know your history and is no substitute for a clinician who can. Anything described here as urgent should be treated as urgent. If something feels badly wrong to you and this site does not mention it, that is a reason to be seen, not a reason to wait. Nothing here is treatment advice and no page gives drug doses.
