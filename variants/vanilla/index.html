How much should you pee and poo?

Six to eight pees a day. Three poos a day to three a week. Those two ranges cover most people, most of the time — but the range is not the point. What matters is whether your own pattern has changed, and whether you can see blood. This guide gives you the actual numbers, the words your doctor uses, and a short list of things that mean stop reading and get help.

Before anything else

If any of this is happening, stop reading and get help now

  • Blood you can see in your pee. Even once. Even if it was painless. Even if it has already stopped. It is the commonest first sign of bladder and kidney cancer, and it very often comes and goes. Get it looked at — do not wait for a second episode.
  • Black, tarry, sticky poo that smells strongly foul. That is digested blood from higher up the gut, and it is an emergency the same day. Iron tablets, bismuth medicines and liquorice make stool black too, but not tarry and not sticky. If you are not certain which you are looking at, treat it as bleeding.
  • You cannot pass urine at all and your bladder feels full and painful. This does not resolve on its own and it damages kidneys. Emergency department, now.
  • Numbness around the saddle area — genitals, inner thighs, buttocks, anus — together with new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels, or new leg weakness or severe back pain. This can be cauda equina syndrome, where nerves at the base of the spine are being crushed. It is a surgical emergency measured in hours, not days. Go to A&E and say out loud: “I think this might be cauda equina.”
  • Vomiting blood, or heavy rectal bleeding that keeps coming. Call 999 or your local emergency number, especially with dizziness, breathlessness or a racing heart.
  • Sudden testicular pain. Torsion is time-critical; the window to save the testicle is measured in hours.

The full emergency list, including sepsis and bowel obstruction, is on the warning signs page.

The numbers, before the opinions

These are population ranges from mainstream clinical sources, not targets you have to hit. Being outside one of them is a prompt to look, not a diagnosis.

  • 6–8×Typical pees per day. Four to ten is still ordinary.
  • 0.8–2 LUrine passed in 24 hours on roughly two litres of fluid.
  • 300–500 mLComfortable bladder capacity in a single void.
  • 3×/day – 3×/weekThe accepted normal range for pooing. About 95% of people sit inside it.
  • 100–150 gTypical daily stool weight on a Western diet. High-fibre populations pass 300–500 g.
  • 10–73 hWhole-gut transit time, median around 30–40 hours.
  • Type 3–4The Bristol stool types to aim for: soft, formed, passes without effort.
  • Pale strawThe urine colour to aim for most of the day. Not colourless.
  • 3 weeksAn unexplained change in bowel habit lasting this long is the standard threshold for investigation.
02

The one-minute version

Frequency is the least useful number you have. Ten small pees on three litres of tea is an ordinary day. Ten small pees on one litre of water is not. Volume, colour and change tell you far more than the tally.

Consistency beats count for poo. Going three times a week, soft and easy, with no straining, is normal. Going every day with hard pellets and ten minutes of effort is constipation. Shape and ease outrank the calendar.

Drink to thirst and aim for pale yellow. The “eight glasses a day” rule has no evidence base — it appears to be a misreading of a 1945 American recommendation that went on to say most of that water already comes from food. Urine colour adjusts itself for weather, exercise and body size. A glass count does not.

Two colours override everything else. Anything red or pink in the toilet, and anything black and tarry. Both get checked, both times, regardless of how well you feel.

Weeks matter more than days. A bug, a new tablet, a stressful fortnight or a fibre change explains most short-lived upsets. Something that has changed and stayed changed for three weeks or more is the pattern worth booking about.

Build a note to take to the appointment

Clinicians work from the story you tell them, and ten minutes is not long to reconstruct six weeks from memory. The note builder asks you the questions a GP would ask, in the order they would ask them.

It screens for emergencies first and stops you if it finds one. Otherwise it collects the details that actually change what happens next — duration, your normal versus now, Bristol type, urine colour, exactly what the blood looked like and where it was, weight change, medicines — and turns them into a plain-text summary you can print or hand over. There is a short daily log built in, so a fortnight of honest records replaces a fortnight of impressions.

It runs entirely in your browser. Nothing is sent anywhere, because there is nowhere for it to be sent — this site makes no network requests at all.

05

Where the evidence is thin, this guide says so

Health writing about bowels and bladders is unusually full of confident claims with very little behind them. Three examples you will find handled honestly rather than smoothed over on the pages that follow.

Cranberry juice and urinary infections. Cranberry compounds do reduce bacterial adhesion in a laboratory. Human trials are much less impressive: a modest reduction in recurrent infections for some groups, little or nothing for older adults, catheterised patients or the general population. It is not a treatment for an infection you already have.

Probiotics. Mixed, and highly strain-specific. Some strains help in some conditions — antibiotic-associated diarrhoea has the best case. Most supermarket products have little evidence for general bowel health. Fix fibre, fluid and routine before spending money here.

Eight glasses of water. No evidence base at all. See the hydration section for what the official adequate-intake figures actually say, and for the fact that you can also drink dangerously too much.

A note on tone

This site says pee and poo because those are the words people use, and being coy about the subject is how symptoms go unmentioned for months. It does not joke anywhere near a serious symptom.

This is information, not medical advice

OUTPUT cannot examine you, does not know your history and is no substitute for a clinician who can. Anything described here as urgent should be treated as urgent. If something feels badly wrong to you and this site does not mention it, that is a reason to be seen, not a reason to wait. Nothing here is treatment advice and no page gives drug doses.

Figures are drawn from mainstream clinical sources and given as ranges. In the UK, NHS 111 will triage by phone or online at any hour; elsewhere, use your local urgent-care line. Nobody is annoyed with you for turning up and being fine.
